# 3L Visual Style Authority

This file governs **presentation style only**. Current manuscript prose remains story authority. If art conflicts with prose, the art is wrong.

The goal is a recognizable 3L visual family across covers, character portraits, location pieces, and occasional release art without turning the project into an image-heavy pipeline.

## Core house look

- Graphic fantasy illustration with **hip-hop / graffiti / editorial-poster energy**.
- Ink-heavy, brushy, painterly, rough, and tactile rather than glossy.
- Strong silhouettes first. A figure or creature should read as a clear dark shape before small details are visible.
- Use dry brush, paint drag, splatter, scratches, grime, paper texture, rough edges, and occasional graffiti-like strokes.
- Prefer large intentional shapes and negative space over uniform micro-detail.
- Limited palette per image: charcoal/black + dirty cream/tan + one or two strong accents such as rust red, ochre/gold, teal/blue, or purple.
- High contrast is good. Constant shine is not. Light should catch selected planes, edges, faces, tools, glass, or metal instead of illuminating everything equally.
- Environments should feel worn, functional, and inhabited: dirt, grease, dust, scuffs, stained cloth, battered stone, oxidized metal, scratched tools, imperfect surfaces.
- The image should feel **authored**, not like maximalist AI fantasy wallpaper.

## Character framing

### Human figures

Humans stay human. Large animal silhouettes may be used as **symbolic graphic framing** behind a character, almost like an ink shadow, stencil, or graffiti motif. They are not literal transformations, familiars, spirit animals, or canon powers unless the manuscript says so.

### S-rank figures

S-rankers may be framed monumentally: relaxed danger, strong posture, confident negative space, heavy silhouette, and the sense that everyone else naturally gives them room. They do not all need armor or weapons pointed at the viewer.

### Greg, Second Life

Greg should usually be **less visually badass than the people around him**.

He can be capable without looking like the poster hero. Favor worker posture, grime, ordinary practical clothing, tired or awkward humanity, tools, sitting positions, imperfect stance, and moments where other characters visually dominate the frame. His competence should often emerge from context rather than costume.

### Nessa Vale

Nessa is **not S-rank**.

Her visual identity is Guild voice, pressure desk, information flow, operational competence, paperwork, station familiarity, and the ordinary intimacy of Greg's Second Life. She can look sharp, attractive, self-possessed, and memorable without reading as a battle queen or elite combatant.

### East Four

East Four is a working Line station. Its people are **Line hands / station workers**, not generically labeled "mechanics."

Visual language: grease, soot, dust, work clothes, gauges, manifolds, valves, inspection slates, pressure desks, tools, pipes, worn gloves, stained walls, patched equipment, cramped working spaces. They should look like people who keep civilization functioning while the fantasy world happens around them.

### Ithar

Ithar should read as **near-black translucent glass**, not metal armor or a gemstone dragon.

Most of his body may disappear into darkness. Light should reveal the glass selectively along edges, thinner structures, horns, face planes, neck, claws, or internal reflections.

His hoard is not a curated royal treasury. Mix coins and jewels with **broken furniture, bent pots, rusted tools, bottles, armor scraps, wagon parts, ceramics, chains, crates, bones, fabric, useless objects, and accumulated trash**. Valuable things can be half-buried in junk.

### Nhal

Nhal remains the ancient bound manticore-like figure from the manuscript. Keep the same rough visual language: ominous, old, physical, graphic, and strange rather than polished fantasy-monster rendering.

## Composition rules

- Default to **one subject per image**. Standalone portraits are preferred over split panels or lineups unless the request explicitly calls for a group.
- Use strong diagonals, off-center framing, low or slightly unusual camera angles, and cropped forms when useful.
- Crowds can collapse into silhouette blocks and texture instead of becoming hundreds of individually rendered faces.
- Tournament or arena architecture may function as graphic framing: banners, walls, shadows, poles, dust, paint, crowd mass.
- Animal silhouette motifs should usually be large, flat, abstracted, and secondary to the human subject.
- Tools, rods, weapons, gauges, junk, or furniture can become compositional anchors without turning into ornamental clutter.

## Text rules

Prefer **no text** unless the image needs identification.

If text is used:

- use only canon names, locations, ranks, or terms that are true for the depicted moment;
- keep it minimal, usually one or two labels;
- do not invent epithets, fake quotes, slogans, lore captions, ranks, titles, factions, or Japanese-style decorative writing;
- do not label East Four as "the mechanics" unless future manuscript prose explicitly adopts that term.

## Avoid

- glossy 3D-fantasy rendering;
- hyper-detailed AI wallpaper where every surface competes for attention;
- jewel-saturated lighting everywhere;
- pristine costumes and perfectly clean heroes;
- generic RPG armor as the default visual language;
- random decorative text or invented lore;
- literal animal anatomy on human characters merely because an animal silhouette is used;
- every character looking equally heroic, dangerous, beautiful, or expensive;
- making Greg more visually dominant simply because he is the protagonist.

## Consistency ratio

Aim for roughly **70% shared house language / 30% character-specific variation**.

The shared 70% is silhouette, rough ink, grime, limited palette, strong graphic composition, selective detail, and hip-hop/graffiti energy.

The flexible 30% is palette accent, environment, posture, symbolic animal silhouette, costume specifics, props, and how much negative space a character gets.

This should allow a large body of art to feel related without forcing every image into the exact same template.
