## RECORD 024

## THE WRONG MAN

I ruined a man's job interview because he had not done something yet.

That is difficult to defend.

I tried anyway.

His name was Verren Holt.

I knew the name before I knew the face.

That should have stopped me.

It did not.

District survey needed another field inspector because there was simply too much work.

Old passages. Foundation discrepancies. Station books arguing with walls. Operators who had written the same complaint for thirty years and finally had enough attention to become inconvenient.

Six people applied.

Verren was fourth.

I was not supposed to be in the interview.

That matters.

Halden asked me to sit in because I would work with whoever got hired.

Tova said this was a bad reason.

“Why?”

“Because you have opinions.”

“So do you.”

“Yes. Mine are certified.”

“Fuck you.”

Halden looked between us.

“Can we hire someone before both of you retire?”

Tova was fifty-four.

I was thirty-two.

I looked at her.

She looked at me.

“Probably not,” she said.

The first three interviews were ordinary.

Then Verren walked in.

Thirty-eight. Thin. Clean coat. Brown hair going gray early at the temples.

No resemblance to the man I remembered except the way he looked at the room before sitting.

Door.

Window.

Tova's tools.

Halden's papers.

Me.

First life arrived as a report.

Verren Holt. Senior load assessor. Forty-six. Temporary supports outside Vey. Repeated test readings copied because the real test would delay opening. Seven injured. Two died later. Hearing. Falsification. License suspended.

I remembered the summary because the failed route delayed one of my contracts.

People dead.

Greg inconvenienced.

Excellent historical method.

Verren sat.

“Good morning.”

I already disliked him.

That is the ugly sentence.

He inherited guilt from a man eight years older in a history nobody else remembered.

Halden asked about experience.

Six years structural survey. Four years station reconstruction. Two years bridge-Line support inspection. Current certification. No discipline.

Tova asked what he did when field measurements contradicted approved drawings.

“Measure again.”

“Then?”

“Ask who made the drawing.”

“Why?”

“Construction drawings and record drawings lie in different ways.”

Tova's mouth moved slightly.

Approval.

I hated that.

Halden gave him an East Two problem.

Unknown bronze sleeve. Responsive to load. Movement below intervention threshold.

“Close the station?” Verren asked.

“Your call.”

“Then no.”

I leaned forward.

“Why?”

“Not enough information.”

“You have movement under load.”

“Yes.”

“Unknown legacy structure.”

“Yes.”

“And you leave it operating.”

“Depends on movement, service consequence, alternate feed, observation frequency, and whether the response repeats.”

Reasonable.

I pushed harder.

“Would you sign continued operation?”

“Maybe.”

“Convenient.”

Verren frowned.

Tova looked at me.

Halden did too.

I knew.

I kept going.

“What would make you sign?”

“Enough evidence that restricted operation is safer overall than isolation.”

“Safer overall can hide a lot.”

“It can.”

“You comfortable signing because delay is expensive?”

There.

Too close.

Verren's expression changed.

“Expensive to whom?”

“Does it matter?”

“Yes.”

“Answer.”

Halden said, “Greg.”

I ignored him.

Verren looked at me for another second.

“No. Cost alone does not make a pressure condition safe.”

“But it can make you accept risk.”

“Of course.”

I laughed once.

Wrong sound.

“So yes.”

“No. Cost changes decisions. It does not change whether the structure is safe.”

“Difference without distinction if you sign.”

Verren's eyes narrowed.

“Have we met?”

The room went quiet.

That question again.

Bren had asked it for another reason.

“No.”

“Then I don't understand what you're asking me.”

Neither did anyone else.

Halden ended the interview.

Professionally.

After the door closed, Tova said, “What the fuck was that?”

She did not swear often.

Good indication.

“Don't hire him,” I said.

“Based on what?”

“I know him.”

Halden's face changed.

“You just said you had never met.”

“I haven't.”

Tova closed her eyes.

Halden did not have enough context to enjoy the sentence.

“Then you don't know him.”

“I know the name.”

“From?”

“Old records.”

That answer had been dying for years and still crawled out when frightened.

“What records?”

I could not tell him.

So I said the worst possible thing.

“Trust me.”

Tova stood.

“No.”

I looked at her.

“No what?”

“No. That is not evidence.”

“I didn't say it was.”

“You are asking us to reject a qualified applicant because you have a feeling attached to a name.”

“Not a feeling.”

“Then give the thing.”

I could not.

Halden asked the current questions.

Disciplinary record?

No.

Complaint?

No.

Bad reference?

I did not know.

“Then your objection is noted.”

That should have been the end.

It was not.

I had influence now.

Not much.

Enough.

The position went to Mara Vel, the station hand with handwriting that appeared to be a personal attack.

She was good.

Tova liked her. Halden liked her. She deserved the job.

That made my guilt harder to measure.

Verren took a temporary reconstruction contract instead.

Three months later an old stair collapsed at Market Five.

Nobody was on it.

Almost.

Verren had closed the passage twenty-two minutes earlier.

Fresh separation in a lintel. His supervisor told him to keep the route open until the masonry team arrived because closure added two hours to material transport.

Verren closed it anyway.

Then wrote his own name beside the decision.

The lintel dropped.

No injuries.

The report crossed my desk.

I read his statement twice.

OBSERVED FRESH JOINT SEPARATION.

SUPERVISOR REQUESTED CONTINUED LIMITED ACCESS.

DECLINED.

PASSAGE CLOSED 14:10.

COLLAPSE 14:32.

SIGNED: VERREN HOLT.

I stared at the name.

First-life Verren had falsified a test to avoid delay.

Second-life Verren had created delay against instruction because the evidence looked wrong.

Same name.

Different history.

I wanted a hidden explanation that preserved my judgment.

Maybe training changed him.

Maybe Halden's reforms changed the office.

Maybe he would still become dishonest later.

That last one was the easiest because nobody could disprove it today.

You can wait forever for a future crime and call every decent act temporary.

Convenient.

Tova found me with the report.

“You saw.”

“Yes.”

“Want to tell me now?”

“No.”

She nodded.

Not approval.

Boundary accepted.

“Then tell him.”

“What?”

“You were an ass in his interview.”

“I did not hire him.”

“You were still an ass.”

“Do you schedule apologies professionally?”

“Yes.”

Of course.

I found Verren at Market Five the next day.

Tram.

Rain.

Shoulder aching because weather had opinions.

He stood beside the collapsed stair with a mason.

“Inspector,” he said when he saw me.

“Verren.”

“We met.”

“Yes.”

“Memorable.”

“I was an asshole.”

The mason looked between us and quietly left.

Coward.

Verren folded his arms.

“Yes.”

That was easier than I deserved.

“I treated you like I knew something about your judgment that I could not support with current evidence.”

Very specific apology.

Occupational hazard.

“Why?”

I could not give him the impossible answer.

“I had information I believed applied to you.”

“From where?”

“I can't explain that in a way you should accept.”

His mouth tightened.

“Then why should I accept this?”

“You shouldn't have to.”

That stopped him.

“I influenced the interview. Mara earned the position. I am not saying it was yours. I am saying my objection to you was unfair.”

Verren looked at the collapsed stair.

“Can you fix that?”

“No.”

“Then what do you want?”

“Nothing.”

He laughed once.

Not kindly.

“Everyone wants something.”

“Fine. I want to stop being the man who did it and never told you.”

“That is for you.”

“Yes.”

Good.

He did not absolve me.

I said, “Your closure was right.”

“I know.”

“Good.”

“My supervisor says luck.”

“It wasn't.”

“Fresh separation could have settled.”

“Yes.”

“I did not know it would fall.”

“No.”

“I closed because if I was wrong, we lost two hours. If I was right and left it open, somebody might be under it.”

That sentence could have come from Tova.

Not from the report I remembered.

Verren watched me.

“You really thought I was dangerous.”

“Yes.”

“Do you still?”

There was no safe answer.

Good.

“No current evidence says you are.”

He smiled slightly.

“Current.”

“I am trying.”

“That sounds exhausting.”

“It is.”

He went back to work.

We did not become friends.

Good.

That would have been too convenient.

Years later I saw his name on a reconstruction standard. A good one.

I never found the first-life falsification in second-life records.

Maybe it never happened.

Maybe that job never existed.

Maybe a different person made the choice.

Maybe Verren remained capable of becoming the man I remembered and simply did not.

That possibility took me longest.

Verren Holt had once become a man who signed false numbers.

Verren Holt had also become a man who shut a stair against instruction and put his name beside the delay.

Both were memories I possessed.

Only one belonged to the world I was standing in.

That distinction should have been obvious after Bren.

It was not.

Lessons repeat because students do.
