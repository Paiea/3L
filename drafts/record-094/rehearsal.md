## RECORD 094

## THE SECOND

The Authority eventually asked who was second.

Not because they cared about our internal titles.

Because forms prefer one name.

We had become large enough that somebody decided Greg could no longer be the answer to every box.

Primary contact.

Greg.

Operational authority.

Greg.

Emergency authorization.

Greg.

Property signatory.

Greg.

Regional response coordinator.

Greg.

Then one clerk reached the line:

SECONDARY AUTHORITY.

He looked up.

“Who?”

“Tracy.”

He looked at her.

Then at me.

Then at her field mark.

B-class by then.

Still not what people expected beside an S.

“Your second?”

“Yes.”

Arcrutus was sitting three chairs away eating cashews.

The clerk noticed him.

Then noticed Rellan across the room.

Then looked back at Tracy.

“Her?”

Tracy did not react.

I did.

Badly.

“Yes.”

He cleared his throat.

“I only mean in the event you’re unavailable.”

“Yes.”

“Wouldn’t an S-class deputy make more sense?”

Arcrutus laughed.

Helpful.

“No.”

The clerk looked at him.

Arcrutus pointed at Tracy with one cashew.

“She tells me where the wagons go.”

Tracy closed her eyes.

“Do not help.”

“I am helping.”

“No.”

The clerk looked increasingly unhappy.

Good.

I said, “Put Tracy.”

He did.

That was the formal beginning.

Not the real one.

The real beginning happened three months earlier when I disappeared for nine days.

Not literally.

Field work.

Northern reinforcement failure.

Ugly enough that I went.

Far enough that messages took a day.

Arcrutus was gone too.

Different contract.

Rellan was south.

Brann had a crew in the fen.

For once, everybody people associated with visible power was somewhere else.

Then three things broke.

Of course.

A washhouse pump failed.

One transport team came back with six workers showing the same fever.

And an Authority shipment we had promised to move arrived two days early because somebody in an office had interpreted “not before Thursday” as “Tuesday is probably fine.”

Tracy got all three before breakfast.

I learned this later.

At the time I was fifty miles away holding a reinforcement line together and complaining about rain.

Tracy was eating eggs.

One of the transport clerks came into Marrin’s.

“Problem.”

She kept eating.

“Which one?”

That answer mattered.

Not what happened.

Which problem.

He told her about the fever.

She stopped eating.

Medical first.

The pump could wait.

The shipment could wait.

Six workers with the same symptoms could become twelve while people argued about invoices.

She moved them into the washhouse isolation room.

That room existed because she had insisted an empty room near medical storage should remain empty.

I had called it wasted space.

She had called me stupid.

Historical record favored her.

The fever was not poison.

That would have been easier for Tracy emotionally.

It was a marsh-borne infection.

Not severe yet.

Contagious enough.

The workers had shared one return wagon.

She isolated the wagon.

Then the driver.

Then everyone who had unloaded it.

Stopped kitchen cross-traffic.

Changed meal delivery.

Sent for a general healer.

Then another.

Not because she could not heal.

Because infection was not her best domain.

That was also Number Two.

Knowing when not to be the expert.

The first healer wanted to move the sick workers to the Guild clinic.

Tracy said no.

He objected.

She asked how many open isolation beds the clinic had.

He knew.

Two.

We had six.

He stopped objecting.

She converted an upstairs storage room into clean overflow.

Moved equipment.

Changed routes through the building.

Assigned one door in.

One door out.

No shared utensils.

No staff rotation between sick and clean rooms.

Then she went back to the eggs.

Cold.

She ate them anyway.

Money and food.

Not dignity.

The pump failed harder before lunch.

One wash line shut down.

Processing slowed.

Someone proposed running the backup at full load.

Tracy said no.

“Why?”

“Because then we have no backup.”

“We’re losing product.”

“We can lose product.”

That sentence surprised everyone.

Especially people who thought Tracy loved money more than oxygen.

She did.

Almost.

But she knew categories.

Product could be lost.

People could be lost.

Backup capacity could be preserved.

She cut intake.

Diverted two incoming marsh loads to the old processor at terrible rates.

Then called the old processor personally.

They charged her more because they knew we were desperate.

She agreed.

Later someone asked why she did not negotiate harder.

“Because I was desperate.”

Honest.

Then she wrote the amount down.

Never forgot it.

The Authority shipment arrived before noon.

Too early.

Forty crates.

Priority seals.

A junior officer insisted they had a written commitment.

Tracy read it.

Then asked:

“What day is it?”

He told her.

“What day does that say?”

“Thursday.”

“Good.”

“We need movement now.”

“Then you need someone else.”

“We contracted you.”

“For Thursday.”

“This is Authority priority.”

“Then pay Authority priority.”

He stared.

She named a number.

He got angry.

She ate a piece of bread while he did.

Eventually he paid it.

Not because she bullied him.

Because moving forty crates on Tuesday required calling reserve drivers, breaking two scheduled commercial routes, and using the spare wagon she had once argued was not idle.

Priority cost money.

She charged the cost.

Then more.

Good.

By evening the shipment was moving.

The washhouse was limping.

The fever had stopped at eight cases.

Eight, not six.

Two unloaders developed symptoms.

Nobody else did.

The Guild infection specialist arrived after dark.

She reviewed Tracy’s isolation choices.

Changed two.

Kept the rest.

That mattered.

Not perfect.

Good.

On day two, one of the sick workers deteriorated.

Fast.

Respiratory strain.

Channel inflammation secondary to fever.

The general healer started brute stabilization.

Correct.

It was not enough.

Tracy saw the pattern first.

Not because she knew the infection better.

Because she knew cumulative toxin and inflammatory load.

Different cause.

Same body problem.

Contain.

Stabilize.

Repair.

Recover.

She stopped one restorative compound because it was increasing circulation faster than the worker could clear the inflammatory byproduct.

The general healer objected.

Tracy said, “Watch his hands.”

They watched.

Color improved after stopping it.

The healer looked at her.

Then changed the plan.

Not surrender.

Collaboration.

That was better.

The worker lived.

So did all eight.

By day four the pump was replaced.

Not repaired.

Tracy decided repair was false economy.

The Authority shipment arrived where it needed to go.

We lost product.

Made money anyway.

Not much.

Enough.

No disaster.

No song.

No article.

Exactly the kind of week that makes an organization real.

I came back on day nine.

Walked into Marrin’s.

Everything looked normal.

That bothered me.

Not because I wanted damage.

Because I had expected evidence of my absence.

Tracy was at the back table eating.

I sat.

“What happened?”

She looked at me.

“Which thing?”

There it was again.

I knew then.

Not completely.

Enough.

“Everything.”

She told me.

Not dramatically.

Pump.

Fever.

Shipment.

Eight infected.

One bad.

All alive.

Some product loss.

Priority fee.

New pump.

She slid the numbers across.

I read them.

“You charged Authority that much?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

“I know.”

“Anyone mad?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

“I know.”

I looked at the infection notes.

“You changed a B-class healer’s treatment.”

“Asked.”

“He changed it.”

“Yes.”

“Because of you.”

“Because it was making the patient worse.”

No pride.

Useful.

Then I saw another paper.

Guild evaluation request.

Her name.

“Who filed this?”

“The healer.”

“What for?”

“Poison and exposure specialty.”

“This wasn’t poison.”

“I know.”

“Then?”

“He thinks my load management crosses enough exposure cases that I should be re-evaluated.”

She sounded irritated.

“Why irritated?”

“Paperwork.”

Of course.

The evaluation took months.

Cases.

Field history.

Observed work.

Controlled scenarios.

A long argument about whether her capability belonged under poison, toxic exposure, metabolic crisis, or advanced medical support.

Institutions love categories after somebody becomes inconvenient to categorize.

The final result gave Tracy an A-class capability rating in poison and exposure medicine.

Not general healing.

That distinction mattered.

Put her beside an A-class trauma healer with a crushed chest and she still preferred the trauma healer.

Put her beside that same healer with a complex venom cascade and the room increasingly looked at Tracy.

She read the result.

Then the compensation schedule.

“Does this change my field rate?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

I looked at her.

“That is all?”

“What else?”

“A-class.”

“In one specialty.”

“Yes.”

“So?”

I laughed.

She did not.

Then she asked where we were eating.

That was the celebration.

Expensive fish.

Very expensive fish.

Arcrutus came.

Called her Clerk.

She threatened to poison him.

He looked delighted.

Probably because by then everyone knew she actually could.

The next Authority review asked again who was second.

This time the clerk did not look at Arcrutus.

Did not look at Rellan.

Did not look at anyone else.

He wrote Tracy before asking.

That was closer.

But still not the point.

Number Two was not a rank.

Not a compliment.

Not a ceremony.

It meant if I disappeared, the work did not become an emergency merely because I was gone.

Tracy had built enough systems that absence could remain absence.

Not catastrophe.

That was one of the greatest things anyone ever did for me.

I did not understand how valuable it was until my second life.

Until Nessa died.

Until the children died.

Until Hale took my leg.

Until I woke up in a body and a world where none of the people I had built with knew me.

Ithar’s head lifted.

“Tracy existed in your second life.”

“Yes.”

“Did she know you?”

“No.”

The cave changed around that answer.

Not actually.

Memory did.

Ithar watched me.

“You found her.”

“Yes.”

“After Hale?”

I looked at the peg.

“Yes.”

“How long after?”

“Not immediately.”

“Why?”

Because I had made a different mistake first.

I leaned back against the stone.

“Hale was cleared.”
