## RECORD 093

## THE RETIRED

Arcrutus retired badly.

Nobody was surprised.

He did not retire from being S-class.

That would have required becoming someone else.

He retired from obligations.

Some.

Not all.

Enough.

Aren took more of the family work.

Then more.

Contracts.

Property decisions.

People asking which Dray needed to sign what.

The kind of responsibility Arcrutus had accumulated because he had been alive, powerful, and difficult for too long.

Aren was good at it.

Better than Arcrutus admitted.

Arcrutus handed things over slowly enough to complain and quickly enough to become bored.

That was when he started appearing at Marrin’s.

Frequently.

Too frequently.

At first we thought he liked the food.

He did.

Then we thought he liked irritating Tracy.

He did.

Then we realized he had nowhere he needed to be.

Dangerous.

He arrived before lunch one Tuesday.

Sat in the back room.

Ate.

Read half a report that did not belong to him.

Criticized the chair.

Went upstairs.

Asked a transport planner why one northern route crossed the central market.

Came back down.

Ate again.

Tracy watched him from across the room.

“He needs a job.”

I looked at her.

“You want to hire Arcrutus?”

“No.”

“You said he needs a job.”

“For someone else.”

Arcrutus looked up.

“I can hear you.”

“That is part of the problem,” Tracy said.

He smiled.

“Clerk.”

By then Tracy was B-class.

Her eye twitched every time.

Arcrutus knew.

Of course.

I looked at him.

“What are you doing?”

“Eating.”

“After that.”

“Digesting.”

“Arcrutus.”

He leaned back.

“Nothing.”

There.

He did not like saying it.

I understood.

Too well.

First-Life Greg had built identity around usefulness.

Arcrutus had done something similar for much longer.

Different shape.

Same trap.

“What do you want to do?”

He looked offended.

“I am retired.”

“That was not my question.”

Tracy slowly put her papers down.

This had become interesting.

Arcrutus said, “I have spent forty years being told what needs doing.”

“Now nobody tells you.”

“Yes.”

“And you hate it.”

“No.”

He hated it.

“Work for me.”

Silence.

Tracy closed her eyes.

Arcrutus stared.

Then laughed.

Not politely.

Actually laughed.

“For you.”

“Yes.”

“I am S-class.”

“So am I.”

“Support.”

“Yes.”

“Different.”

“Yes.”

He stopped laughing.

Interesting.

“You are serious.”

“Yes.”

Tracy said, “No.”

I looked at her.

“You wanted him employed.”

“Not by us.”

Arcrutus looked delighted.

Excellent.

I ignored both of them.

“What would it cost?”

Arcrutus leaned forward.

“Greg.”

“How much?”

“You cannot afford me.”

That was probably true once.

Not anymore.

“How much?”

He gave me a number.

Absurd.

Tracy made a choking sound.

Not food.

Money.

Rare.

I thought.

Looked at the number again in my head.

Then said, “Fine.”

Arcrutus stopped smiling.

Tracy stood.

“No.”

I looked at her.

“No what?”

“No fine.”

“He gave a number.”

“He gave an insult.”

“Same structure.”

“No.”

Arcrutus was watching me differently now.

Not joking.

“You would pay that.”

“Yes.”

“For what?”

Good question.

Tracy sat back down slowly.

Also interested.

I thought about the answer.

Combat?

We could hire combat.

Training?

We could hire training.

Prestige?

Useful but not enough.

Contacts?

Already had many.

Then I understood what I wanted.

“You.”

Arcrutus frowned.

“That is not a role.”

“It can be.”

“No.”

Tracy looked offended by the lack of job description.

Good.

I continued.

“I want you near us.”

“That sounds worse.”

“Probably.”

“For what purpose?”

“When something is above us.”

“Above you?”

“Yes.”

He smiled slightly.

Good.

“When I am wrong.”

“Frequently.”

“When Tracy is wrong.”

She glared.

“Less frequently.”

“Debatable.”

“When we need someone who remembers what institutions looked like thirty years before we arrived.”

Arcrutus’s expression changed.

Small.

I continued.

“When an S-class problem walks into the room.”

“You are S-class.”

“Yes.”

“You have other S-class contacts.”

“Yes.”

“Then why me?”

“Because you are already here eating.”

Tracy laughed.

Finally.

Arcrutus did not.

Then did.

Fine.

“What are the terms?”

That was Tracy.

Of course.

Arcrutus looked at her.

“Clerk.”

“Terms.”

“I said a number.”

“That is compensation, not terms.”

He leaned back.

“I do not report to you.”

“You report to somebody.”

“No.”

“Then we do not hire you.”

Arcrutus looked at me.

I ate.

No rescue.

Progress.

He looked back at Tracy.

“I choose my field work.”

“Within conflict rules.”

“I do not attend routine meetings.”

“Good.”

That surprised him.

Tracy continued.

“You will attend meetings where your presence is the reason the meeting exists.”

“Define that.”

“I will.”

“No.”

“Yes.”

“I do not fill forms.”

“You will provide information to someone who does.”

“Fine.”

“You do not borrow transport without logging it.”

He stared.

“Absolutely not.”

“Then walk.”

I nearly choked.

Arcrutus looked at me.

“She speaks to S-class like this?”

“Mostly you.”

“Good.”

He smiled.

Bad.

They negotiated for three hours.

I left halfway through.

Not because I was bored.

Because I had learned something.

If two competent people are arguing about terms they both intend to keep, leaving can improve the outcome.

When I returned, there was a contract.

Short.

Arcrutus had won freedom.

Tracy had won clarity.

His base retainer was obscene.

Field work paid separately.

He could refuse almost anything.

We could refuse almost anything he proposed.

No exclusivity.

No ceremonial obligations.

No pretending he worked for us in ways he did not.

He had access to the yard, washhouse, Marrin’s offices, medical staff, and planning network.

He was required to notify someone before taking equipment.

That clause had apparently caused the longest fight.

He signed.

Tracy signed for the enterprise.

Then she pointed at the transport log.

“Use it.”

Arcrutus looked at me.

I looked away.

No rescue.

He called her Clerk for the next five years.

She scheduled him anyway.

The public reaction was stupid.

Not because it was wrong to care.

Because everyone cared for the wrong reason.

An old S-class Named Ranker had stepped back from his own formal responsibilities and chosen to work inside Greg’s organization.

That looked enormous from outside.

Validation.

Endorsement.

Alliance.

Maybe succession.

People invented motives.

Arcrutus had a simpler explanation.

“I was bored.”

Nobody believed him.

They should have.

Rellan came to Marrin’s two days after the announcement.

“You hired Arcrutus.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“He was available.”

“That is not how people hire S-class.”

“Apparently it is.”

“How much?”

“No.”

“Enough to be stupid?”

“Yes.”

Tracy passed behind us.

“Very.”

Arcrutus was already seated at his usual table.

Rellan looked at him.

Then at me.

“You understand what this looks like.”

“Yes.”

“Do you?”

“No.”

Better answer.

It looked like the network had crossed another line.

Before Arcrutus, people could call it a collection of practical businesses around an unusual S-class support.

After Arcrutus, they started using other words.

Faction.

House.

Bloc.

Syndicate again.

One paper used empire.

Tracy clipped that one too.

Asset estimate wrong.

Of course.

But Arcrutus changed the inside too.

He noticed things.

Not proactively like Tracy.

Differently.

He had seen institutions fail slowly.

Seen families split over property.

Seen talented people destroy themselves because everyone was afraid to tell them no.

Seen Authority policy swing one direction and back.

Seen S-class people mistake being difficult to kill for being difficult to replace.

He did not mentor me.

Not formally.

He had already refused that word years earlier.

He simply existed nearby and said things.

Often rude things.

Sometimes useful.

Tracy hated how often I listened.

I hated how often she agreed with him.

Arcrutus hated schedules.

Everyone suffered.

It worked.

One evening, months after signing, I found him eating cashews in the upstairs office.

No paperwork.

Naturally.

“You like it here?”

“No.”

“Good.”

“The food is adequate.”

“High praise.”

“The clerk is oppressive.”

“She can hear you.”

“I know.”

From the next room:

“Transport log.”

Arcrutus closed his eyes.

I smiled.

Then he looked at me.

“You have become expensive.”

“What?”

“Not you.”

He gestured.

Building.

People.

Noise below.

Everything.

“This.”

I leaned against the door.

“Bad?”

“Dangerous.”

“How?”

“People build plans around things that work.”

I waited.

“If this stops working,” he said, “more breaks than you.”

That stayed.

Tracy built redundancy because she understood poison.

Arcrutus understood another layer.

Once people trust infrastructure, failure becomes social.

Not only financial.

That was when Number Two stopped being a joke.

Not because Tracy was second strongest.

She was not.

Arcrutus could have killed almost everyone in the building before dessert.

Number Two meant something else.

If I disappeared, did this continue?

At first the answer had been no.

By then, increasingly, the answer was Tracy.
