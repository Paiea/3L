## RECORD 112

## THE CHOICE

Tracy labeled the plans A and B.

Of course.

No dramatic names.

No:

PATH OF RESTORATION.

No:

PATH OF REVENGE.

Just A.

B.

I hated that.

“Which is which?”

“You know.”

“I want you to say it.”

She looked at me.

“Fine.”

Plan A.

Reopen outward capacity.

Reduce closed-loop load.

Increase relational work.

More multi-person support.

Long-duration low-stress linking.

Less hostile stabilization.

Less solo impact routing.

More recovery.

More time.

Preserve the possibility of rebuilding toward First-Life architecture.

Not guarantee.

Possibility.

Plan B.

Continue current path.

Increase closed-loop duration.

Increase self-anchoring.

More hostile routing.

More terrain integration.

More compressed return.

Less emphasis on outward bandwidth.

Accept progressive loss of relational ceiling.

Faster solo combat growth.

Faster route toward Hale.

There.

She said it cleanly.

I sat.

Tracy did not.

She stood beside the table.

Current room.

Better window.

Still cold.

Progress.

“How certain?”

“Which part?”

“Loss.”

“Not certain.”

“Percentage.”

“No.”

“Tracy.”

“No.”

“You love numbers.”

“I love honest numbers.”

Good.

“What would make you certain?”

“Autopsy.”

I looked at her.

“Helpful.”

“You asked.”

Fair.

She sat.

“Greg, magic adapts.”

“I know.”

“Channels adapt.”

“I know.”

“Body adapts.”

“I know.”

“You have spent four years teaching the same routes to close.”

“Yes.”

“They are getting better at closing.”

“Yes.”

“They are getting worse at staying broad under stress.”

“Yes.”

“Not gone.”

“No.”

“Not broken.”

“No.”

“Changed.”

“Yes.”

There.

That word.

Not injury exactly.

Adaptation.

Bodies become what you repeatedly ask of them.

Magic too.

First-Life Greg had built outward for decades.

Second-Life Greg had spent four years telling every problem:

Come back to me.

Close here.

Route here.

Trust nothing outside.

The body listened.

Of course.

“What if I choose A for two years?”

Tracy thought.

“Better chance.”

“At?”

“Recovering breadth.”

“How much?”

“I don’t know.”

“Would I lose the loop?”

“Some efficiency.”

“Combat?”

“Yes.”

“Hale gap?”

“Yes.”

There.

Real.

“What if I choose B for two years?”

“Closed-loop performance improves.”

“How much?”

“I don’t know.”

“Combat?”

“Yes.”

“Hale gap?”

“Smaller.”

“And outward?”

“Worse.”

“How much?”

“I don’t know.”

We sat with uncertainty.

Annoying.

People like decisions because they imagine decisions create clarity.

Mostly they create responsibility.

“Can I alternate?”

Tracy almost laughed.

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because adaptation is not a calendar appointment.”

“One month A. One month B.”

“You will become mediocre at both.”

“I am already mediocre at many things.”

“Current data.”

Fair.

She pulled another page.

My field history.

Aqueduct.

North Ward.

Quarry.

Tunnel jobs.

Ordinary support.

Outward work had improved when I used it.

Closed work improved faster.

Because I trained it more.

Because I cared more.

Because Hale.

There.

The target was in the data without his name.

“How long until B becomes permanent?”

“Permanent is too clean.”

“Fine.”

“How long until changing back becomes unreasonable?”

Better question.

Tracy answered.

“Maybe a year.”

That hurt.

Not fifteen.

Not ten.

One.

“Maybe less.”

Worse.

“What does unreasonable mean?”

“You could always retrain.”

“Always?”

She stared.

“Do not be stupid.”

Fair.

“Could I?”

“Yes.”

“How long?”

“Years.”

“Back to First-Life ceiling?”

“I do not know.”

There.

Again.

No prophecy.

No magical law.

Choice under uncertainty.

Worse.

I looked at Plan A.

There was something humiliating about it.

Not because outward support was weak.

It had once made me S-class.

Because Plan A asked me to trust time.

Years.

People.

Teams.

Relationships.

A future long enough to justify broad development.

Plan B asked less.

Hale exists.

Become dangerous sooner.

Simple.

That was why it appealed.

Tracy saw me reading.

“What are you thinking?”

“That A is healthier.”

“Yes.”

“Better long-term.”

“Probably.”

“Closer to who I was.”

“Yes.”

“And B is stupid.”

“Yes.”

I looked at her.

“And you still brought it.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because it is your body.”

There.

Current Tracy.

Not Number Two.

Not owner.

Not parent.

Healer.

Consultant.

Mine only in the sense that people become part of each other after enough honest work.

“You could refuse.”

“I did.”

“Before.”

“Yes.”

“And now?”

“Now you understand the cost.”

That mattered.

Consent changed when knowledge did.

I looked at Plan B.

“What would First-Life you say?”

Tracy’s face hardened.

Good.

Wrong question.

I knew.

“Sorry.”

She waited.

Current question.

“What do you say?”

She thought.

Longer than expected.

“I think Hale is making choices in your body without touching you.”

That hurt.

I looked away.

She continued.

“I think you are letting a man you hate choose what kind of support you become.”

“Yes.”

“You agree?”

“Yes.”

“Then stop.”

Simple.

I almost laughed.

Did not.

“Would you?”

“No.”

That surprised me.

Tracy looked annoyed at herself.

“If someone killed my family?”

“Yes.”

“I do not know.”

Better.

“Then do not pretend this is easy.”

“I am not.”

She tapped Plan A.

“This is the better thing.”

“Yes.”

“And?”

I looked at Plan B.

“This is the thing I am going to do.”

There.

No thunder.

No magic change.

No irreversible sensation.

Just sentence.

Tracy closed her eyes.

Then opened them.

“Okay.”

That hurt more than argument.

“Okay?”

“I heard you.”

“You’re not going to fight me?”

“I already did.”

“Could do it again.”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because eventually disagreement becomes theater.”

Good.

She took Plan A.

Folded it.

Put it in the drawer.

Did not throw it away.

That mattered.

Plan B stayed on the table.

“Rules change,” she said.

“How?”

“I measure outward function more often.”

“Fine.”

“Any sudden drop, we stop escalation.”

“Fine.”

“Pain reporting becomes stricter.”

“Fine.”

“Field work counts as load.”

“Obviously.”

“You say obviously and then ignore it.”

Fair.

“Fine.”

“Rest after high-loop use.”

“Fine.”

“No hidden tests.”

I looked at her.

“Greg.”

“Fine.”

“No revenge target without telling me.”

I stared.

“That is not medical.”

“It is if you want me to manage the body afterward.”

“Fine.”

She pointed.

“Say it.”

“I will tell you before pursuing a revenge target.”

“Good.”

I hated that one.

Useful.

Then she wrote a date at the top of Plan B.

Not Sera.

Not Hale.

Today.

“This is the date?”

“Yes.”

“Point of no return?”

“No.”

I looked at her.

“Then what?”

“Point where you stopped pretending you were not choosing.”

There.

Better.

We trained.

Not immediately.

Two days later.

Rest first.

Of course.

Then loop.

Duration.

Load.

One variable.

Weeks passed.

The closed route improved.

Outward tests worsened slightly.

Tracy marked everything.

Months.

Work.

Named Ranker jobs.

Food.

Arguments.

Hale remained alive.

I tracked him less obsessively.

Not because I wanted him less.

Because the plan existed now.

Plan B.

The revenge had become training rather than panic.

That was healthier.

Maybe.

Dangerous word.

Nine months after the choice, Tracy stopped a test.

Not dramatically.

She looked at the numbers.

Then at me.

“Again.”

I did the outward link.

Weighted frame.

Basic.

Old.

The link formed.

Stable.

Then under stress, narrowed.

Not failed.

Narrowed.

The body preferred return.

I felt it too.

There.

Not theory.

Habit.

Architecture.

Tracy said:

“Again.”

I did.

Same.

She looked at Plan B.

Then at the drawer where Plan A still lived.

“What?”

I asked.

She did not answer immediately.

Then:

“If you switched now, I think you could recover more than half.”

I stared.

“More than half.”

“Yes.”

“First-Life ceiling?”

“No.”

There.

Clean.

Not impossible forever.

Not magical curse.

But the old path was no longer reasonably available from here.

Not in this body.

Not on this calendar.

Not without rebuilding so much that Hale would become an old man first.

Maybe.

Maybe not.

I looked at the graph.

Then at her.

“This is it?”

“I think so.”

Point of no return.

Not a gate.

A medical opinion.

More frightening.

“What do you want to do?”

Tracy asked.

Good.

Still mine.

I closed the outward link.

Then opened the loop.

Plan B.

“Continue.”

She nodded.

Wrote the date.

That was the permanent trade.

Not when I invented the loop.

Not when I killed Sera.

Not when I signed a plan.

Here.

When someone I trusted told me the old ceiling was leaving, and I chose speed anyway.

Hale was still alive.

I wanted him dead more than I wanted to become First-Life Greg again.

That is the truth.

Ithar said nothing for a long time.

Then:

“You traded S-class.”

“No.”

His eyes narrowed.

“Explain.”

“I traded the path that had made me S-class once.”

“Difference?”

“Yes.”

“Important?”

“Very.”

I looked at my hands.

“I did not own S-class.”

The cave stayed quiet.

“I owned choices.”

“And chose Hale.”

“Yes.”

There.

No excuse.

That was the point.
