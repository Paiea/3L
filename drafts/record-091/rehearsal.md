## RECORD 091

## THE TABLE

We bought Marrin’s because the owner wanted to retire.

That is the official explanation.

The real explanation was onions.

Not literally.

Mostly.

By then Marrin’s had become where things happened.

Not important things.

That would have sounded intentional.

Meals.

Debriefs.

Hiring conversations.

Contract arguments.

Post-mission silence.

Birthday dinners.

One breakup.

Three reconciliations.

A healer interview that accidentally became dinner with six people.

Arcrutus once slept in the back room for forty minutes because he had arrived early and considered punctuality a personal failure.

The staff knew us.

That mattered.

They knew Tracy wanted the good bread.

They knew Brann hated sweet sauces.

They knew I would forget to eat if a meeting ran long.

They knew which crews wanted food immediately after field work and which needed ten minutes of quiet first.

They knew when to bring another pot of tea without asking.

They knew when not to interrupt.

You cannot put that on an invoice cleanly.

Tracy tried.

Of course.

She estimated it anyway.

The owner told us on a Tuesday.

Her name was Mara Marrin.

The restaurant belonged to her family before it belonged to her.

She had run it thirty-one years.

Her knees hurt.

Her son lived three regions away.

Her daughter had no interest in feeding Rankers who argued about contract law over soup.

Reasonable.

Mara stood beside our table after dinner.

“I’m selling.”

Tracy stopped eating.

I stopped too.

That was how serious it was.

“The restaurant?” I asked.

“The business.”

“Building?”

“Separate.”

Tracy immediately said, “No.”

Mara looked at her.

“I wasn’t asking you.”

“I know.”

I looked at Tracy.

“You don’t know the price.”

“I know restaurants.”

“You love restaurants.”

“I love eating in them.”

Different.

Mara smiled.

“She’s right.”

“Thank you,” Tracy said.

“That was not support.”

“I’ll take it.”

Mara had an interested buyer.

Not terrible people.

That would have been easier.

A small regional dining group.

Good reputation.

They wanted the location.

Probably keep some staff.

Change menu.

Renovate.

No guarantee on the back room.

I hated them immediately.

Unfairly.

Greg maturity had limits.

After Mara left, I said:

“We should buy it.”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Restaurants are terrible.”

“This one makes money.”

“Small money.”

“Money.”

“Compared with what we do?”

“That isn’t the point.”

“It is if you are buying it.”

I looked around.

The room was nearly empty.

Late.

One server resetting tables.

Kitchen cleaning.

The back room door open.

Our usual table.

Not ours.

That was the problem.

“We use it.”

“We use roads. We are not buying a road.”

“Do not give me ideas.”

“Greg.”

“We spend enough here.”

“Not enough.”

“Meetings.”

“Still no.”

“Catering.”

“No.”

“Crew food.”

“No.”

“Hiring.”

“No.”

“Social events.”

“No.”

“Private room.”

“No.”

“You like it.”

“Yes.”

“So?”

“That is not accounting.”

I stared at her.

She smiled.

I had used that line on her once.

Cruel.

We left.

I assumed the conversation was over.

This was stupid.

Two days later Tracy asked Mara for the books.

Mara gave them to her.

Traitor.

Tracy spent a week complaining.

Not about the restaurant.

About restaurants generally.

Spoilage.

Staff turnover.

Margins.

Equipment.

Licensing.

Suppliers.

Rent.

Customers who believed buying one meal gave them authority over furniture placement.

Then she found the rent.

“What?”

We were in the washhouse office.

She had the books spread out.

“Building.”

“What about it?”

“Separate owner.”

“Mara said.”

“The lease resets in fourteen months.”

“So?”

“So whoever buys the restaurant is exposed.”

“Rent increase?”

“Or sale.”

I waited.

Tracy stared at the page.

Then at me.

“No.”

I smiled.

“Do not.”

“What?”

“You’re enjoying this.”

“A little.”

“We are not buying a restaurant.”

“Okay.”

“If we buy the restaurant, we buy the building.”

I stopped smiling.

“What?”

She tapped the lease.

“I am not putting capital into a food business that depends on someone else deciding whether we keep the location.”

“You just said we are not buying it.”

“I said if.”

“That sounded less hypothetical.”

“Shut up.”

Progress.

The building cost more than the restaurant.

Of course.

It contained two upper floors.

One mostly empty.

One divided into offices nobody wanted because the stairs were narrow and the plumbing temperamental.

Tracy looked at those too.

“You’re thinking.”

“No.”

“Face.”

“I hate you.”

“No you don’t.”

“No.”

We ran the numbers.

Restaurant alone: mediocre investment.

Building alone: acceptable.

Restaurant plus building plus our existing use: annoyingly sensible.

Not because dinner became magically profitable.

Because we were already paying for meeting space elsewhere.

Catering elsewhere.

Temporary offices.

Private event rentals.

Staff meals.

Guest meals.

Travel dinners.

Client dinners.

And, increasingly, the social cost of finding somewhere appropriate every time twelve people needed to talk without an Authority seal on the wall.

Marrin’s already solved those problems.

We were just paying them in pieces.

Tracy hated that.

Recurring friction.

Again.

Mara sold us the restaurant.

The building owner sold after making us wait three weeks because he realized we cared.

Tracy disliked him forever.

We did not fire the staff.

That would have defeated the point.

Mara stayed three months during transition.

The kitchen manager stayed.

Servers stayed.

Menu mostly stayed.

Tracy changed the bread supplier.

Immediately.

Mara objected.

Then tasted the new bread.

Stopped objecting.

I did not run the restaurant.

This surprised people.

“You own it,” Rellan said once.

“Yes.”

“So who runs it?”

“The people who know how.”

He looked at me.

That concept remained difficult for some high-rankers.

Tracy did not run it either.

She reviewed numbers.

Ate there.

Occasionally terrorized suppliers.

Different.

The upper floor became offices.

Not grand.

Useful.

One room for contracts.

One for medical records.

One for transport planning.

One for people who needed somewhere to sit quietly after bad work.

The back room remained a back room.

That mattered.

We did not turn it into a boardroom.

Still had food.

Still had stains.

Still had a chair nobody trusted because one leg was shorter.

People came anyway.

Then more people.

Not because we summoned them.

Because somebody knew somebody.

A healer needed transport.

A transport client needed a pressure specialist.

A warehouse owner had a property problem.

Brann knew a crew.

Tracy knew a poison case.

I knew an Authority planner.

Rellan knew somebody military.

Arcrutus knew everyone and pretended otherwise.

The table became faster than formal channels for some things.

Not illegal.

Usually.

Mostly.

That became the joke.

Someone called it Greg’s table.

Tracy objected.

“Why is everything his?”

The server said, “Because Greg tips.”

Tracy looked at me.

“Traitor.”

I tipped more.

Years later one writer called Marrin’s the informal headquarters of the Greg network.

Another called it the dining room of a private syndicate.

Mara mailed us that article from retirement.

She had underlined syndicate.

Written:

KNEW IT.

Tracy framed the page.

Not because she liked the accusation.

Because the article estimated the restaurant value correctly.

Arcrutus mocked the purchase for years.

“Terrible investment,” he said.

Then ate there four nights in one week.

“You live here now?” I asked.

“I am helping you recover the investment.”

“By making us feed you?”

“Yes.”

“That is not how revenue works.”

“You should have hired a better accountant.”

Tracy looked up from across the room.

“I heard that.”

Arcrutus took another bite.

“Clerk.”

By then Tracy was B-class.

She still hated when he called her that.

He knew.

That was why he did it.

The restaurant never became our most profitable asset.

Not even close.

It became one of the most valuable.

Different.

The marsh made money.

The yard moved things.

The washhouse processed them.

Marrin’s connected people.

That was harder to price.

Tracy tried anyway.

Of course.

Eventually she gave up.

Mostly.

Ithar lowered his head.

“So you built an empire around lunch.”

“No.”

“A syndicate around poison.”

“No.”

“A network around inconvenience.”

I considered that.

“Closer.”

He looked almost pleased.

I was not.

Because by then another problem had emerged.

The more things worked around me, the less anyone could tell where my personal capability ended and the organization began.

Some people thought that meant I was less S-class.

Others thought it made me more dangerous.

Rellan had opinions.

So did the Authority.

Eventually they made the mistake of asking me to explain the difference.
