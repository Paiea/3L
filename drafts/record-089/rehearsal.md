## RECORD 089

## THE PARTY

The first S-class party I attended was not a party.

This disappointed me.

There was food.

There was alcohol.

Music.

Expensive clothing.

People pretending not to look at each other while looking at each other constantly.

Technically party.

Spiritually meeting.

Tracy understood this before I did.

“You have to go,” she said.

“No.”

“Yes.”

“No.”

“You’re S-class.”

“I know.”

“People expect to see you.”

“They have seen me.”

“Not like this.”

“That sounds ominous.”

“It is.”

I looked at the invitation.

Heavy paper.

Authority seal.

No agenda.

Suspicious.

“What do they want?”

“Everything.”

“Helpful.”

She was eating while we argued.

Of course.

Marrin’s had started sending food to the washhouse twice a week because Tracy negotiated a standing rate.

She claimed volume discount.

I claimed addiction.

Both could be true.

I waved the invitation.

“It says reception.”

“Yes.”

“That means party.”

“No.”

“Reception means receiving.”

“Exactly.”

“Receiving what?”

“Requests.”

I looked at her.

“Then definitely no.”

She took another bite.

“You’ll go.”

“Why?”

“Because political surprises are expensive.”

There it was.

Poison again.

Everything became poison eventually.

I went.

Tracy came too.

Not because she cared about S-class culture.

Because I said there would be food.

That was all it took.

The reception occupied the upper hall of an old civic building near the Authority quarter.

I had been there once for an evaluation.

Same stone.

Different chairs.

Better lighting.

Much better food.

Tracy disappeared within six minutes.

I found her at a table examining small pastries like a healer assessing a wound.

“Good?”

She nodded.

“Excellent.”

“Useful event.”

“Potentially.”

“Professionally?”

“No.”

Fair.

I looked around.

Three of the living Seven were present.

Not all.

That was normal.

S-class people had the unique privilege of being important enough that absence became a statement even when it meant nothing.

One old clan head never attended anything.

Another attended everything and left early.

Arcrutus came when food was decent or somebody interesting might be irritated by his presence.

He was there.

Cashews in one hand.

Of course.

He saw me.

Raised the cashews.

That counted as greeting.

Mael was not there.

I respected him for that.

The room held more A-class people than I had ever seen in one place without something trying to kill us.

Authority officials.

Guild heads.

Contractors.

Military staff.

Researchers.

Property people.

Healers.

People whose jobs I did not understand but whose clothing suggested I should.

Everyone appeared relaxed.

Nobody was relaxed.

I learned this slowly.

At first I tried to talk like a normal person.

Mistake.

A regional transport director asked how the marsh operation was going.

“Fine.”

He waited.

I waited.

Apparently fine was not enough.

“Expanding?”

“A little.”

Another pause.

I looked at him.

He looked at me.

This was terrible.

Tracy appeared beside me with two pastries.

“Greg doesn’t discuss active expansion before contracts close.”

I had no such policy.

The director nodded like she had said something meaningful.

“Of course.”

He moved on.

I looked at Tracy.

“What?”

“You were dying.”

“I was having a conversation.”

“No.”

She ate one pastry.

“Who is he?”

“No idea.”

“Excellent.”

She left again.

Mentorship.

Across the room stood the man who looked more S-class than I did.

He was not S-class.

That distinction mattered to him.

Rellan Tor.

High A-class.

Combat reinforcement.

Close-range pressure.

Excellent field record.

Good family.

Good sponsors.

Good coat.

Everything about him fit the story people already knew how to tell.

He had trained under Pava for several years.

Not exclusively.

Enough.

Before Vey, people had already begun speaking about him as one of the obvious future S-class candidates.

Then Pava died.

Then I lived.

Then the Authority recognized me.

Nobody had stolen anything from Rellan.

There was no seat.

No quota.

No promise.

That did not mean nothing had changed.

He crossed the room.

People made space for him naturally.

That annoyed me.

Not because he had done anything.

Because I had to ask people to move around wagons.

“Greg.”

“Rellan.”

“Congratulations.”

“Thank you.”

He meant it.

Mostly.

That made things harder.

His eyes moved briefly to my coat.

Practical.

Good quality now.

Still practical.

Then to Tracy, who had returned because somebody nearby had fresh food.

“Your healer?”

“Partner,” Tracy said.

Rellan looked at her field mark.

D-class.

Then at me.

He did not hide the confusion fast enough.

Tracy noticed.

Did not care.

She took something from a tray.

Rellan recovered.

“Business partner?”

“Yes.”

Tracy nodded.

“Half.”

I looked at her.

“Do you have to tell everyone?”

“Yes.”

Rellan blinked.

“Half?”

“Distributable.”

“Ah.”

He had no idea what that meant.

Neither had I before Tracy.

He looked back at me.

“You’ve been busy.”

“So have you.”

He had.

Two high-risk clearances.

One military deployment.

A coastal breach.

The kind of work newspapers understood.

Individual.

Visible.

Clean.

I had marsh infrastructure.

Wagons.

A washhouse.

A D-rank healer eating pastry beside me.

If you asked the room which one of us looked like the future of S-class, I knew the answer.

That bothered me more then.

Rellan said, “Pava would have hated this.”

He meant the reception.

I laughed before I could stop myself.

“Yes.”

Something eased.

He looked toward the room.

“She said any event with this many polished shoes was a hostage situation.”

That sounded like her.

For several seconds we were just two people who missed the same person differently.

Then somebody approached him.

Military coat.

Important.

Rellan shifted smoothly into another conversation.

Name.

Context.

Shared contact.

Recent deployment.

Future possibility.

No visible effort.

He was good at this.

I hated him slightly for that too.

Tracy watched.

“You should learn that.”

“No.”

“Yes.”

“I refuse.”

“You are bad at being important.”

“Thank you.”

“That was not criticism.”

I looked at her.

She meant it.

Interesting.

“Do I need to be good at this?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because if you only talk to people when you need something, every need becomes expensive.”

There it was again.

The step before the problem.

I looked around differently.

The reception changed.

Not socially.

Structurally.

That transport director controlled permits in two regions we might eventually use.

The healer near the window ran a specialist clinic.

One Authority deputy had been involved in my recognition review.

A supplier we bought through three intermediaries was standing twelve feet away drinking wine.

A woman Tracy had spoken with for twenty minutes owned two warehouses near the canal.

“You knew who they were.”

“Some.”

“And you didn’t tell me.”

“You would have left.”

“Yes.”

“Exactly.”

Cruel.

I stayed.

That became another mistake.

Not staying.

Talking.

An Authority planner asked whether I might be available for a southern reinforcement review in winter.

I said, “Probably.”

He smiled.

“Excellent.”

Two weeks later I received scheduling documents.

I brought them to Tracy.

“What is this?”

“Your winter reinforcement review.”

“I did not agree to this.”

“You said probably.”

“That is not agreement.”

“At that party, apparently it is.”

I stared.

She smiled.

“Political surprises are expensive.”

“I hate you.”

“No you don’t.”

“No.”

We made a rule.

No casual probably.

No casual maybe.

No “we should talk” unless I actually wanted to talk.

No “sounds interesting” around Authority planners unless I wanted paperwork.

Language had consequences.

Again.

Years later I became better at those rooms.

Not charming.

Never Rellan.

I learned who mattered to work I cared about.

Who knew things.

Who could say yes.

Who could quietly make something impossible.

Who liked being asked early.

Who hated being surprised.

Who needed public credit.

Who preferred none.

I attended selectively.

Sometimes Tracy came.

Mostly when the food was good.

She became surprisingly effective because she did not care whether anyone thought she belonged.

A famous healer once spent ten minutes explaining a basic poison mechanism to her.

I was offended.

Tracy ate an olive.

When he finished, she said, “Yes.”

That was all.

Later he asked her for help on a difficult toxin case.

She charged him.

Reasonable.

Rellan remained better at the room.

For years.

That mattered.

He looked like what people thought power should look like.

I increasingly looked like a man who had brought his accountant-healer to dinner because she wanted dessert.

But something shifted.

People started coming to me differently.

Not asking what I could do personally.

Asking whether I knew someone.

Whether our yard could move something.

Whether Tracy had an opinion.

Whether the washhouse had space.

Whether I could introduce them to Brann.

Whether we had a healer available.

Whether the private room at Marrin’s was free Thursday.

That was new.

At some point, without intending to, I stopped needing the party to find the network.

The network started finding me.

Tracy noticed first.

Of course.

After one reception, she said:

“We should stop doing these here.”

“What?”

“These meetings.”

“This is not our building.”

“I know.”

“Then how?”

She looked at me.

“Marrin’s.”

I sighed.

“We are not buying the restaurant.”

“I did not say that.”

“You were thinking it.”

“No.”

“Face.”

She glared.

Progress.
