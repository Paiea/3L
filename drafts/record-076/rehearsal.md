## RECORD 076

## THE CHOICE

Her eyes opened.

Mael said, "Nobody move."

We did not.

That sounds obvious.

It was not.

Arcrutus had spent most of the descent behaving like a man being professionally prevented from touching things.

Now there was a living prisoner in front of him.

Maybe prisoner.

Definitely living.

His whole body changed when her eyes opened.

He still did not move.

That mattered.

The healer whispered, "Pupils responsive."

Pava said, "To what?"

"Light first."

The eyes moved.

Not much.

Toward the healer.

Then Mael.

Then Arcrutus.

Then me.

I had called the body human-shaped.

That was what poor light and too much blue lattice had given me.

It was wrong.

Her head lifted less than an inch.

The movement changed the silhouette.

What I had taken for hanging cloth around the shoulders separated into long dark filaments. Not hair exactly. Too thick at the base. Too responsive to the pressure around her.

They lifted in a slow wave as three blue paths changed.

Below them, the body was built on four limbs.

Large ones.

Very large ones.

The forequarters alone would have filled most of my bedroom at home.

Dark hide showed between the restraints. Not fur. Not clean skin either. It had the dull mineral texture of stone that had been polished by water for a thousand years.

The chest and shoulders had the heavy forward build of a great hunting cat, but stretched into proportions no cat owned.

Huge paws rested against the black floor beneath the restraints.

The face was broad and feline in the loosest possible sense.

Heavy jaw.

High cheek structure.

Dark mane-filaments framing it instead of ordinary fur.

Not a lion.

Not anything I had seen alive.

Old bestiaries had drawn creatures with roughly that shape.

Four great limbs. A predator's chest. Too many teeth. A long dangerous tail.

The artists always ruined the face by making it human.

This face was not human.

Thank every god for that.

It was fully nonhuman and unmistakably intelligent, which somehow made the old drawings feel ruder.

Her eyes stayed on me.

I looked away first.

Professional instinct.

Probably.

Pava was already working.

"Movement changed pillar two, four, and six."

The survey lead checked her own slate.

"Four before two."

"By how much?"

"Less than a quarter count."

"Again?"

The healer said, "Do not ask her to move again."

Pava looked up.

"I wasn't."

"Good."

Mael said, "No prompted movement. No contact. Passive observation only."

Arcrutus finally spoke.

"Can she hear us?"

The healer looked at Nhal.

"I don't know."

Good sentence.

Arcrutus took one slow breath.

Then, without moving closer, he said, "Can you understand me?"

Her eyes shifted to him.

Nobody celebrated.

That was not proof.

People look toward sound.

Animals do too.

Some doors if they are built badly enough.

Arcrutus knew that.

His jaw tightened anyway.

The survey lead crouched beside the nearest pillar without touching it.

"Six primary interfaces."

Pava said, "Still?"

"More obvious now."

She pointed without crossing Mael's line.

Four of the major paths aligned with the limbs.

Not attached at the paws.

Higher.

Forelimb, forelimb, hindlimb, hindlimb.

A fifth relationship ran through the chest and disappeared behind the spine.

The sixth went down.

I followed it with my eyes.

The tail had been hidden under two layers of blue light and one black support rib.

It was longer than I expected.

Thick at the base.

Tapering slowly rather than ending in anything like the stinger from bad bestiaries.

The last several feet were dark and hard.

Not metal.

Not exactly bone.

A natural anchoring structure, ridged and dense, driven into the black floor at an angle.

I stared at it.

The survey lead noticed.

"What?"

"That one is different."

Pava checked.

"Pressure?"

"Geometry."

The survey lead shifted position.

She was quiet for a while.

"Yes."

Mael said, "Different how?"

"The limb interfaces close around her. The chest interface crosses her."

She pointed at the tail.

"That one doesn't."

Arcrutus said, "Meaning?"

"Meaning I don't know."

He made a sound through his nose.

The survey lead looked at him.

"Would you prefer I invent something?"

"No."

"Then enjoy the answer."

I liked her.

The tail connection bothered me.

The limb restraints looked like restraints.

Even before we knew their function, their shape was clear enough.

Something outside the body had been built to hold a body in place.

The tail did not look held.

It looked seated.

That was all I was willing to say.

Even to myself.

Nhal breathed again.

The mane-like filaments lifted.

Pava's slate changed.

"There."

The healer said, "What?"

"Upper shaft compensation."

"From breathing?"

"Maybe from muscle tension around breathing."

The survey lead checked the timing.

"No. Before the chest expansion."

Pava looked at me.

I did not enjoy becoming the person people looked at when cause and effect got rude.

"Don't," I said.

"I didn't say anything."

"Your face did."

Mael said, "Greg."

I looked at the blue paths around the chest.

"Could be pre-load. She changes one condition before the body moves so another condition stays stable."

Pava said, "Voluntarily?"

"No idea."

"Reflex?"

"No idea."

"System response?"

"Still no idea."

"Useful."

"I try."

Arcrutus said, "Ask her."

Everyone looked at him.

He looked back.

"She is awake. We are standing ten feet away discussing whether she's part of a machine. Ask her."

Mael said, "We do not know whether she understands the language."

"She looked at me."

"So does a horse."

"You have met some very judgmental horses."

"Arcrutus."

He stopped.

Not because the joke was bad.

It was.

Because Mael had used his name the way a leader uses a hand on a door.

Arcrutus looked at Nhal again.

"Then prove it."

That part was harder to dismiss.

Pava glanced down at her instrument case.

I followed the glance.

Two passive pressure rods remained in the top tray.

Same make.

Same calibration family.

The kind used for comparative readings.

I said, "Can we give her a choice that doesn't matter?"

The survey lead immediately said, "Explain."

"If every movement we see has a structural reason, we can't separate her from the system."

Pava said, "So ask for a movement the system has no reason to make."

"Small one."

Mael looked at the rods.

Then the floor.

"Risk?"

The survey lead answered before I could.

"If we use surface pressure only, negligible. No direct connection to the blue paths."

Pava added, "We can keep it below a quarter of the lightest test pulse."

Mael looked at the healer.

"Medical objection?"

"To asking her to move pressure, yes."

"To asking her not to move her body, only shift local surface load?"

The healer thought.

"If she can do that without straining."

"We don't know that."

"Then I object to anything stronger than measurable minimum."

Mael nodded.

"Good."

That was approval.

Not mine.

His.

Important distinction.

Pava placed the two rods on the black floor six feet in front of Nhal.

Left and right.

Equal distance from the nearest pillar.

The survey lead checked the floor beneath them for existing differential.

Almost none.

She moved the right rod two inches.

Checked again.

Better.

Pava zeroed both.

"Baseline stable."

Mael looked at me.

"Your idea. Use the fewest words possible."

"Cruel."

"Greg."

I looked at Nhal.

Her eyes were already on the rods.

That was encouraging.

Or terrible.

"If you understand us," I said, "press the left rod. Very lightly."

Nothing happened.

Five seconds.

Ten.

Arcrutus shifted his weight.

Mael did not look at him.

Pava watched the slates.

The healer watched Nhal.

I watched the left rod because apparently I had become an optimist at forty-eight.

The right rod moved.

Barely.

One line on Pava's slate.

Then settled.

Arcrutus exhaled.

I said, "That was the right one."

Nhal's eyes narrowed.

I swear they did.

Pava covered her mouth with one hand.

The survey lead said, "Do not anthropomorphize."

"I didn't say anything."

"Your face did."

Fair.

The left rod moved.

Exactly one line.

Then stopped.

Nobody spoke.

Pava checked the right rod.

"Baseline."

The survey lead checked the floor.

"No corresponding structural shift."

Mael said, "Reset."

They swapped the rods.

Not positions.

Actual instruments.

Same locations, different rods.

Re-zeroed.

The survey lead picked the next instruction.

"Right rod. One light movement."

The right rod moved.

One line.

Stopped.

Again.

This time Pava moved both rods to new places.

Unequal distances.

Survey checked local pressure.

Still safe.

Mael pointed at the farther one.

"That rod only."

One line.

Stopped.

The chamber felt different after that.

Same load.

Same old stone.

Same six pillars.

But we were no longer testing a system that happened to contain a living body.

Something in front of us had heard arbitrary instructions and chosen to answer them.

That did not tell us where Nhal ended and the lattice began.

It told us the distinction mattered.

Arcrutus said, very quietly, "Ask her if she wants out."

I looked at him.

"We don't have yes and no."

"We have left and right."

"We have requested pressure placement. That is not language yet."

"Greg."

"And 'do you want out' is a stupid question."

His face changed.

Not anger.

Hurt, maybe.

I hated systems less than people because systems rarely required you to notice that.

I pointed at the restraints.

"If she's a prisoner, yes. If she's keeping half the north from falling into something worse, also yes. If releasing her kills everyone above us, still yes. The answer doesn't tell us what to cut."

Arcrutus looked back at Nhal.

"It tells us what she wants."

That shut me up.

Good sentence.

Mael let the silence sit.

Then said, "We establish communication before we ask questions we cannot interpret safely."

Arcrutus did not like it.

He nodded anyway.

Nhal watched him.

I wondered how much of that she understood.

Probably more than I wanted.

Pava had not stopped working.

She moved around the arc of pillars with the survey lead, calling readings softly.

"Forelimb one is carrying lateral correction."

"Mark."

"Hind two has deep compression."

"Mark."

"Chest path is not one path."

"How many?"

"At least fourteen relationships through the main interface."

The survey lead swore.

Pava smiled without humor.

"Tail is worse."

I went closer to the safe line.

Mael noticed.

Did not stop me.

The tail anchor carried less visible restraint pressure than the limbs.

It carried more total relationship.

Not force.

Connections.

Blue lines entered the floor around it and disappeared outward in every direction.

Some climbed.

Some descended.

Some vanished into the chamber wall.

Some did things my channels could feel but my vocabulary could not hold.

I said, "That one may be the oldest."

The survey lead looked at me sharply.

"Evidence?"

"None."

She waited.

"Fine. Shape. Wear. The other interfaces were built around correction. That one has growth around it. Integration."

Pava crouched on the other side.

"The black material is continuous with the floor."

"Yes."

"And with the anchor."

"Looks like it."

The survey lead said, "Could still be later encasement."

"Yes."

Good.

Possibly.

Nhal's tail moved.

Not free.

A small muscular tightening near the base.

The blue lattice changed before the visible motion.

Pava pointed at the trace.

"There. Again. Pre-compensation."

Mael said, "Can we isolate one?"

The survey lead understood immediately.

"One relationship?"

"One that appears redundant."

The healer said, "For what?"

Mael looked at Nhal.

"To learn whether she is choosing the compensation or being forced into it."

The healer's expression hardened.

"By making her stop?"

"By asking for the smallest safe reduction we can measure."

Arcrutus said, "No."

Everyone looked at him.

Interesting reversal.

He nodded toward the limb restraints.

"You don't test a prison by tightening the bars."

Mael said, "I agree."

Arcrutus looked almost disappointed.

Mael continued.

"We will not tighten anything. If we cannot identify a low-consequence relation she can relax voluntarily, we do not test."

Pava said, "Give me time."

We gave her two hours.

This is another part stories skip.

The awakened ancient being remained ten feet away while seven adults argued over instrument placement and ate cold ration paste because nobody trusted the chamber enough to light a stove.

Nhal watched us.

Sometimes.

Sometimes her eyes closed.

The healer monitored breath and pupil response.

Arcrutus sat cross-legged just outside the safe line.

He did not speak to her again.

That was probably the hardest thing he had done all week.

I ate half a grain bar.

It tasted like compressed wall.

The other half went back in my pocket because terror does strange things to appetite.

Pava and the survey lead found one candidate.

A minor thermal balancing relation running through the left forelimb interface.

Not inactive.

Nothing here was inactive.

But current temperature across the upper shaft was stable and two parallel relations were carrying the same class of correction.

Pava modeled a reduction smaller than natural drift.

The survey lead checked it independently.

Then made Pava do it again because independent means something.

Mael reviewed both.

The healer objected to the limb interface.

They found another candidate through the chest.

Worse consequences.

Back to the forelimb.

Another hour.

Finally the healer said, "If she shows distress, stop immediately."

Mael said, "Obviously."

"Say it anyway."

He looked at her.

Then at all of us.

"Any distress, any unmodeled movement, any upper-shaft change beyond predicted drift, test ends. Arcrutus braces the descent platform only if ordered. Greg does nothing unless ordered. Pava observes. Survey observes independently. Healer owns medical stop."

He looked at Nhal.

"And she owns whether she does it at all."

Arcrutus nodded once.

That mattered too.

Pava set one rod beside the forelimb interface.

Not touching Nhal.

Touching the black structure carrying the relation.

The survey lead marked the expected baseline.

Mael looked at me.

"Explain only the requested change."

I faced Nhal.

Her eyes were open.

"The path beside your left front leg," I said. "If you can control it safely, let it fall a little. One count only. Then restore it."

Nothing happened.

I waited.

We all waited.

Then the rod dropped one count.

Pava inhaled.

"Holding."

The blue line dimmed.

One second.

Maybe less.

A black band at Nhal's shoulder tightened.

Not much.

Enough that the hide beneath it compressed.

Her forepaw opened against the floor.

Claws came out.

The tail anchor took load.

Pava's slate jumped.

"Stop."

The healer said it at the same time.

Nhal restored the forelimb relation.

The shoulder band loosened.

The tail load fell.

Upper shaft stayed inside predicted drift.

Nobody moved.

The healer was already staring at Nhal's breathing.

"Pain response."

Arcrutus stood.

Mael held up a hand.

"Nobody approaches."

"You saw that."

"Yes."

"It tightened on her."

"Yes."

Pava had gone pale.

She pointed at the trace.

"She reduced the path exactly one count."

The survey lead said, "And the system replaced the lost condition through two other interfaces."

"Tail first."

"Then shoulder."

The healer said, "The shoulder compression happened after the tail load."

I looked at the six pillars.

Then Nhal.

She had done what we asked.

The system had answered too.

Not by stopping her.

By making the same stability appear somewhere else.

Through her.

Arcrutus's voice was very quiet.

"So she can let go."

Pava looked at the trace.

"A little."

The survey lead said, "And something else compensates."

I kept looking at the tail anchor.

The dark ridges had not moved.

The floor around them had.

Just enough to leave a fresh pale line in centuries of black dust.

Nhal looked at me.

One of the mane filaments lifted.

Then another.

The left rod moved one count.

No one had asked this time.

It held there for a breath.

Then returned to zero.

Pava stared at it.

I did too.

The choice had been hers.

The cost had not.
