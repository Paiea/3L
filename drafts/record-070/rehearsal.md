## RECORD 070

## THE ACCOUNTANT

The first time I put a price beside displacement, I wanted to erase it.

Eren Pol moved my pencil out of reach.

Good accountant.

Bad comfort.

We were in a district review room that had been built for eight people and was currently holding eleven, three rolled maps, one box of stale biscuits, and the consequences of River Four.

The west support there had been the problem that waited.

Market Seven, Hillcross, and North Cut had taken the available repair money first because their conditions were worse by the evidence we had at the time. River Four had stayed in service under monitoring, alternate-heat planning, and a promise that deferred would not quietly become forgotten.

It worked.

Mostly.

That was not the same as the decision being easy.

Regional office wanted a better way to compare the next set before somebody had to invent one during another meeting with an angry station forewoman watching us choose whose problem waited.

So there we were.

Eren had written twelve silver in a box.

TEMPORARY DISPLACEMENT, ONE HOUSEHOLD, ONE WEEK: 12 S.

I stared at it.

“No.”

He looked at me.

“No what?”

“You cannot write that.”

“Why?”

“Because that is a family.”

“Yes.”

“You wrote twelve silver.”

“That is the estimated temporary housing and transport cost.”

“That is not the cost.”

“No.”

He waited.

Accountants are dangerous when patient.

Nessa sat across from us with three station schedules open and a pencil tucked behind one ear. Tova had claimed the chair nearest the window because the room was too warm and she believed ventilation outranked diplomacy.

Pellin had attended the first half hour, discovered nobody needed historical translation, eaten two biscuits, and escaped.

Wise man.

Eren tapped the number.

“What do you want me to put instead?”

I did not answer.

That was the problem.

Twelve silver was not a child missing school.

Not an old woman sleeping somewhere unfamiliar.

Not medicine left in a cupboard during a fast evacuation.

Not the extra hour a night worker spent crossing town because the temporary room was farther from the mill.

But if we refused to put any cost beside displacement because the human part resisted arithmetic, the budget would price it as zero.

That was worse.

Nessa looked at the box.

“Keep twelve.”

I looked at her.

“Traitor.”

“Add what it does not include.”

Eren nodded immediately.

Of course he did.

He wrote NOTES REQUIRED beside the line.

“Such as?”

“School interruption,” Nessa said.

I added, “Care disruption.”

Tova said, “Accessibility.”

“Season,” Nessa said.

“Transport to work.”

“Alternate family housing.”

Eren held up one hand.

“If every box requires another page, this will not be used.”

He was right.

I hated him slightly.

The worksheet existed because River Four had exposed something larger than River Four.

We were already ranking unlike risks every time money ran short.

Structural urgency against service interruption.

Possible injury against certain inconvenience.

A school boiler against a market branch.

Households with no alternate heat against workshops that lost a day of production.

Emergency repair time.

Crew availability.

Asset cost.

Political pressure, which everyone pretended was not a category while behaving as though it was.

My objection was that writing the ranking down might make it look objective.

Eren's objection to my objection was simpler.

“Your instincts already rank things.”

Nessa said, “That's what I told him.”

“I remember.”

“You ignored me more elegantly.”

“I was married then.”

“You are married now.”

“Tragic.”

Tova hit the table once with the end of her pencil.

“Can we build the thing before you divorce over accounting?”

Probably.

We started with the numbers least likely to start a moral argument.

Repair cost.

Replacement cost.

Estimated downtime.

Connected buildings.

Alternate capacity.

Crew availability.

Even those misbehaved.

Connected buildings meant nothing by itself.

One workshop and one healer annex were both one building.

Downtime meant different things in July and January.

Alternate capacity existed on paper until three sites needed the same portable boiler.

Every clean number became dirty when it touched a place.

Good.

That meant we were looking at it properly.

Then Tova wrote FAILURE CONSEQUENCE across a fresh sheet.

“Probability?” I asked.

“Separate.”

“Why?”

“Because people collapse low probability and high consequence into one vague feeling.”

Eren smiled.

“Accountants call that bad expected-value work.”

Tova looked at him.

“I call it people being stupid.”

Same discipline, different vocabulary.

We built rough consequence bands.

Minor injury.

Severe injury.

Multiple casualties.

I hated the boxes.

Tova hated them too.

We kept them.

Then came displacement.

Eren wrote the twelve silver.

That was where I tried to erase it.

The first full version took three days.

Not continuously.

Nobody's life respected committee work enough for that.

Tova left twice for field calls.

Nessa missed one afternoon because South Fen changed a transfer window.

I spent a morning at the shop because Rin had found a calibration problem and apparently my name remained attached to reality outside regional policy.

Eren stayed.

I began to suspect finance employees survived by outlasting everyone else.

On the third evening we ran the worksheet against nine actual projects.

The result was nonsense.

Good.

A high-revenue market branch ranked above three residential supports because merchant losses were easy to count and household harm was not.

Eren checked the arithmetic twice.

“Math is right.”

Nessa leaned over the table.

“Then the model is wrong.”

“How?”

“Because it thinks a cold apartment and a closed shop become comparable only after both are translated into money.”

Eren did not defend the sheet.

That was why he remained useful.

He drew a line through the total column.

“No single score.”

The regional administrator at the end of the table made a noise of pain.

“We need a ranking.”

“Later,” Eren said.

I liked him more.

We separated the categories.

Safety.

Essential service.

Displacement burden.

Economic loss.

Asset cost.

Recoverability.

Each received its own rating and its own evidence note.

The paper got uglier.

Better.

A project could rank high because failure might kill people.

Another because two thousand residents had no realistic alternate heat.

Another because one small repair prevented a much larger replacement later.

Same queue position did not mean same reason.

That mattered.

Then Nessa found another hole.

“Schools.”

“What about them?” Eren asked.

“If a heating failure closes a school, what do you count?”

“Repair and displacement if residential service is affected.”

“No. Children go somewhere.”

He looked at her.

“Home.”

“With whom?”

There it was.

A school closure did not only move children.

It moved workers out of jobs to watch them.

Moved meals.

Moved transport.

Moved care.

Most of that labor never appeared in the market column because nobody invoiced the region for staying home with a seven-year-old.

“Can we price that?” Eren asked.

“Poorly,” Nessa said.

“Better than zero?”

“Yes.”

That became a phrase we used too often.

Poorly but visibly.

Better than absent.

Then came the category nobody wanted.

Visibility.

A failure at the royal market created more public pressure than the same outage on Rope Street.

Everybody knew this.

Nobody wanted to write it down because writing it down felt like approving it.

Eren said, “If we leave it out, decision-makers add it later where nobody can see.”

Dera had joined that meeting late and still wore her field coat.

She listened for two minutes.

Then said, “Don't put it in the score.”

Eren looked at her.

“Where?”

“Override.”

That was it.

We created OVERRIDE FACTORS.

Any final change to the ranked list outside the model required a written reason.

Festival.

Royal visit.

Military need.

Legal order.

Existing political commitment.

Whatever the reason was.

Not forbidden.

Named.

If somebody wanted the royal market moved ahead of a school heating branch, they could do it.

They just had to put their name beside why.

Some officials hated that.

Useful sign.

The first live ranking was nine projects.

We worked through them in the same cramped room.

By then the stale biscuits had been replaced with stale rolls.

Institutional growth.

A school heating branch ranked first.

Moderate failure probability.

Severe winter consequence.

No alternate heat.

River Four's deferred repair came second.

Market Seven archive access seventh.

Nobody objected much.

I became suspicious.

Then a trade district project landed sixth despite enormous expected commercial loss.

Good.

The merchants objected enough for everyone.

Their representative arrived with actual closure data.

Not rhetoric.

Better numbers.

Lost wages were higher than our estimate.

Recovery time longer.

We had undervalued the economic impact.

We changed it.

Project moved from sixth to fifth.

Not first.

The representative remained unhappy.

Correct response to losing an argument by one place instead of five.

The worksheet never became neutral.

That was the most important thing we eventually wrote on it.

Weighting severe injury above asset loss was a value judgment.

Weighting displacement above temporary commercial inconvenience was a value judgment.

Counting lost wages was a value judgment.

Not counting unpaid household labor would have been one too.

Numbers did not remove morality.

They stored it.

Sometimes so neatly people forgot it was there.

One afternoon Eren put two budget versions beside each other.

Same projects.

Same total money.

Version A maximized expected financial loss avoided.

Version B maximized combined safety and essential-service risk reduction.

Different winners.

“Which is correct?” he asked.

“B.”

He smiled.

I had learned to hate that smile.

“Why?”

Trap.

I looked at the sheets.

There was no equation that made B morally true.

“I value those outcomes more.”

“Good.”

Not mathematically correct.

Chosen.

Then defendable.

That was more honest.

We kept version history.

Weights.

Override reasons.

Data sources.

Confidence where it meant something.

Unknown where it did not.

Three regions adopted the worksheet within two years.

Two modified it.

One removed the displacement category because gathering the data took too much staff time.

Nessa sent them a letter sharp enough that Dera asked for a copy recreationally.

They restored a simpler version.

Not victory.

Influence.

Messy.

The tool often helped.

That matters too.

Do not make every system Greg touched secretly guilty of what happened decades later just because old Greg knows where the story goes.

Different tools.

Different people.

Different failures.

This one made ugly priorities harder to hide.

It also taught me something that frightened me more with age.

A clean table can carry an argument after everyone forgets there was an argument.

The headings look inevitable.

The weights look technical.

The missing categories become invisible.

That is why I kept the notes.

Not because the notes made us right.

Because they made it possible for someone later to see what we had chosen and disagree.

Years later people would ask when I first understood that budgets were safety systems.

There was no one moment.

Maybe River Four.

Maybe the grant.

Maybe payroll.

Maybe my mother's medicine.

Money kept appearing wherever values became recurring obligations.

Eventually even I noticed.

I never got comfortable with the twelve silver beside displacement.

Good.

Comfort would have been the dangerous part.
