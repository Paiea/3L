## RECORD 103

## THE PLAN

Tracy’s treatment plan fit on one page.

That offended me.

Not because it was short.

Because it was right.

She had divided the page into three columns.

BODY.

MAGIC.

BEHAVIOR.

I hated the third one immediately.

“What is that?”

“You.”

“That is not medical.”

“It is if you keep making medical problems.”

Fair.

Under BODY:

Socket tolerance.

Hip control.

Left shoulder compensation.

Skin integrity.

Balance.

Sleep.

Food.

I pointed at the last two.

“Those are broad.”

“You are broad.”

“That sounds insulting.”

“It is anatomical.”

Probably.

Under MAGIC:

Channel endurance.

Return lag.

Closed-loop duration.

Barrier precision under fatigue.

Recovery after Draw.

Outward-link retention.

I stopped.

“What is that?”

She looked at me.

“You know.”

I did.

“Why are we measuring it?”

“So we know what you are losing.”

There.

No moral argument.

Measurement.

Worse.

“You think I am going to lose it.”

“I think you already are.”

“How much?”

“Not enough data.”

“Guess.”

“No.”

“Tracy.”

“No.”

She pointed at BEHAVIOR.

That column had fewer items.

Training disclosure.

Load escalation.

Pain reporting.

Sleep after field work.

No unscheduled testing alone.

I laughed.

She did not.

“Absolutely not.”

“Which one?”

“Last.”

“Why?”

“Because I am not twelve.”

“Then stop behaving like a twelve-year-old with access to magic.”

I stared.

She stared back.

Current Tracy.

Not old.

Still rude.

Good.

I looked again.

“Unscheduled according to whom?”

“Me.”

“No.”

“Then whoever is supervising.”

“No.”

“Then do not test alone.”

“That is not practical.”

“Neither is finding you unconscious.”

“I have not been unconscious.”

“Yet.”

I hated yet.

It was always doing work.

We negotiated.

This took forty minutes.

Not the magic.

The rules.

I could test alone under low-load thresholds.

I had to disclose new architecture before increasing intensity.

Any numbness, flow lag, skin breakdown, chest pressure, visual changes, or shoulder drop ended the session.

No argument.

I argued.

She wrote NO ARGUMENT in larger letters.

“You enjoy this.”

“No.”

“You do.”

“I enjoy not repeating myself.”

Liar.

She absolutely enjoyed it.

We moved to schedule.

Rehabilitation three days.

Magic endurance two.

One full rest day.

One flexible day.

“Seven again,” I said.

“Yes.”

“Still excessive.”

“Time remains offensive to you.”

“Yes.”

“Unfortunate.”

She gave me a progression rule for the loop.

Not optimization.

Containment.

Increase only one variable at a time.

Duration or load.

Never both.

If return lag increased, step back.

If outward-link precision dropped, step back.

That last part was the one I disliked.

“What threshold?”

She gave one.

Too conservative.

“No.”

“Then find another healer.”

“Manipulative.”

“Accurate.”

We kept it.

She had me test an outward link to a weighted frame.

Basic support.

Old language.

Small.

The frame took load.

I moved it.

Fine.

Again.

Fine.

Then closed loop.

Rest.

Outward again.

Slight degradation.

Not much.

Enough.

Tracy marked it.

I watched.

“Do not look pleased.”

“I am not.”

“You are.”

“I found the cost.”

“That is bad.”

“It is useful.”

She looked at me.

There.

Difference.

To Tracy, cost discovered meant limit found.

To me, cost discovered meant price known.

Not the same.

She saw that too.

“Do not turn every warning into permission.”

That line stayed.

I looked at the page.

“You are good at this.”

She frowned.

“Do not.”

“What?”

“Do not make that first-life.”

“I am not.”

“You are.”

I stopped.

Fair.

“Current data.”

She waited.

“You are good at this because the plan is good.”

Better.

She nodded.

“Thank you.”

Small.

Earned.

We kept going.

She changed the socket fitter again.

Not because the second one was bad.

Because the second one was too focused on comfort and not enough on field use.

The third fitter hated both of us.

That was promising.

He reinforced the side wall.

Changed strap angle.

Added a replaceable contact plate under the peg.

Not magical.

Mechanical.

Important.

The loop had been using the peg as an anchor whether we admitted it or not.

Tracy refused to let that become an excuse to romanticize the injury.

At one fitting I said:

“It is useful.”

She answered immediately.

“It is worse than a leg.”

“I know.”

“Say it.”

“I know.”

“Say it.”

I sighed.

“The peg is worse than a leg.”

“Good.”

“Happy?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because useful adaptations become stupid stories very quickly.”

That was good.

I hated it.

We tested again.

The contact plate improved consistency on stone.

Worse on wet wood.

Terrible on gravel.

Nothing solved everything.

Good.

The plan expanded.

Still one page.

Smaller writing.

Under behavior she added:

DO NOT CHASE HALE BECAUSE YOU HAD A GOOD WEEK.

I stared.

“That is not medical.”

“It is preventative.”

Fair.

Months passed.

Not many.

Enough.

The body improved.

Not dramatically.

Real.

I could walk farther before the socket overheated.

Turn without throwing the left shoulder first.

Stand from a low chair without using both hands.

Run badly.

Then less badly.

The loop improved too.

Short.

Controlled.

Still costly.

Outward support remained.

Also improved.

Slower.

That mattered.

For a while I convinced myself I could keep both.

Maybe I could have.

Then.

Not forever.

At the time, the difference remained small enough to lie about.

I did not lie to Tracy.

Mostly.

Growth.

One afternoon, after a clean session, an Authority courier found me at the treatment room.

Not unusual.

I had started doing small contract work again.

Money mattered.

Revenge was expensive.

So was food.

The courier handed me a sealed request.

Emergency response.

Structural failure.

Eastern aqueduct transfer station.

Civilian district.

Possible secondary collapse.

Support specialist requested.

I read it once.

Then again.

Tracy saw my face.

“What?”

I handed it over.

She read.

Then stopped.

“Hale Rusk.”

His name sat under FIELD LEAD.

Of course.

Tracy looked at me.

“No.”

I laughed once.

Bad sound.

“I agree.”

“Good.”

Then she kept reading.

Her expression changed.

I knew before she spoke.

“Evacuation incomplete.”

“Yes.”

“Pressure chamber unstable.”

“Yes.”

“Secondary residential load path.”

“Yes.”

I looked at Hale’s name again.

Then the district map.

People lived underneath the problem.

Again.

Of course.

Tracy folded the request.

“You should go.”

I looked at her.

“You just said no.”

“I changed the question.”

There.

Good.

“What question?”

“Do you want to work with Hale?”

“No.”

“Do these people need you?”

I looked at the map.

“Yes.”

“Then go.”

Simple.

Horrible.

She handed the request back.

“Am I coming?”

I stopped.

Old instinct said yes.

Current rules said ask.

“Do you want to?”

She looked at the field notes.

Then at my plan.

Then at Hale’s name.

“No.”

Fair.

Pause.

“Will you?”

Longer pause.

“Yes.”

Not because she was Number Two.

Not because she owed me.

Because she decided.

That mattered.

The aqueduct station failed before sunset.
