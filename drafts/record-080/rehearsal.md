## RECORD 080

## THE JOB

“The job was ordinary,” I told Ithar.

He waited.

I waited too.

Neither of us moved.

Eventually he said, “You have said that as if ordinary is evidence.”

“It is.”

“Of what?”

“That I should have taken it.”

Stone pressed cold through my palm.

Ithar’s eyes stayed on me.

“Second life?”

“Yes.”

“Nessa alive.”

“Yes.”

“The children alive.”

I swallowed.

“Yes.”

“Before the catastrophe.”

“Yes.”

He lowered his head.

“Then begin with the ordinary job.”

Fine.

The offer came on a Thursday.

Three nights away.

Four if the inspection went badly.

Good money.

Not miraculous money.

Good enough that I read the number twice.

A district pressure cooperative had lost one of its regular inspectors to a broken ankle and another to a pregnancy that had apparently begun months earlier without consulting their maintenance calendar.

They needed somebody who understood transfer housings, old municipal pressure work, and the particular kind of repair where the person who installed the original system had been dead long enough to escape criticism.

I qualified.

Unfortunately.

I brought the offer home.

Nessa was sitting on the floor with our daughter between her knees, trying to get a knot out of her hair without causing a diplomatic incident.

Our son was building a road from wooden blocks to somewhere under the table.

I put the paper on the table.

Nessa glanced at it.

“No.”

“That was fast.”

“You put work on the table during dinner.”

“We are not eating.”

“We are about to.”

“Different crime.”

She finished the knot.

Our daughter immediately shook her head hard enough to undo part of the work.

Nessa closed her eyes.

I enjoyed this.

Then she picked up the contract.

“How long?”

“Three nights.”

“Maybe four.”

“Maybe.”

She gave me the look.

“Four.”

“Probably three.”

“Pay?”

I pointed.

She read it.

Then read it again.

“Why?”

“Because somebody broke an ankle.”

“No. Why are they paying that?”

“Short notice. Travel. They want the western intake inspected before the next load increase.”

“Dangerous?”

“No.”

“Greg.”

“Normal dangerous.”

“There are several kinds of normal in your profession.”

“Municipal pressure work. Old housings. Bad stairs. Somebody may have installed a valve backward in 118 years ago.”

“Terrifying.”

“Exactly.”

She read the paper again.

We were not poor.

That mattered.

People tell stories after loss in ways that make every earlier choice look desperate.

It was not.

We had food.

Rent was current.

The children had clothes.

Mostly.

They kept changing size without notice.

Our son needed boots again.

Our daughter had developed strong opinions about a school program that charged money for materials because apparently education had discovered commerce.

My mother’s healer visits had become more frequent.

We had replaced the washing basin after the stone incident.

Nessa’s regional travel had been heavier that season.

The emergency fund had absorbed several ordinary emergencies and now looked less impressive than the name suggested.

Also, we had started saying maybe about another child.

Maybe is free.

Everything after maybe is not.

Nessa looked at me.

“You want to take it.”

“I want the money.”

“That is not the same answer.”

“I would rather not go.”

That was true.

The twins had finally reached an age where leaving for three nights no longer meant coming home to two entirely different humans.

Mostly.

Our son had started telling stories that lasted longer than some contracts.

Our daughter had learned that questions could be repeated indefinitely if the adult answer was unsatisfactory.

I liked being there.

That remained strange enough that part of me still noticed.

Nessa tapped the number.

“This replaces what came out of savings.”

“Most of it.”

“And the boots.”

“Yes.”

“And your mother’s next two visits.”

“Probably.”

She thought.

Our daughter escaped from between her knees.

Nessa let her.

“Take it.”

I looked at her.

“You sure?”

“No.”

“Useful.”

“I am sure we could use the money.”

“I can pass.”

“I know.”

That mattered too.

She reached across the table and took my hand.

“Three nights.”

“Probably.”

“Greg.”

“Three nights.”

“If it becomes four, send word.”

“I will.”

“If it becomes five, I am selling your side of the bed.”

“Which side is mine?”

“Whichever one gets the better price.”

Fair.

Our son appeared beside me.

“Where are you going?”

“Work.”

“No.”

Strong position.

“Three sleeps.”

He considered that.

“Two.”

“Negotiation.”

Nessa said, “He gets that from you.”

I did not take the accusation seriously.

Our daughter climbed into my lap and asked whether work had dogs.

“I don’t know.”

“Then why go?”

Excellent question.

“Money.”

Nessa laughed.

I packed that night.

Not much.

Work clothes.

Tools.

Two clean shirts.

Rain layer.

Shoulder wrap.

The left shoulder had been bothering me before weather again.

Three days of ordinary medication.

Food for the road because buying meals at every stop offended me then.

Later I would become much less principled about food.

Our son put a stone in my work bag.

I found it before leaving.

I almost removed it.

Then left it.

Our daughter tried to put the blue cup in too.

That I returned.

“Need it,” she said.

“So do you.”

She considered this.

“Bring back.”

“I am not taking it.”

“Bring back.”

Parenthood.

I kissed both of them.

Then Nessa.

Then the children again because they had noticed the distribution.

Nessa followed me to the door.

“You can still say no.”

I looked at the bag.

At her.

At the contract folded inside my coat.

“I know.”

She kissed me once more.

“Then go make money.”

So I did.

That is the part I kept trying to rewrite afterward.

Not the goodbye.

The permission.

As if Nessa had forced me.

She did not.

As if I had abandoned them for greed.

I did not.

As if some good husband or good father would have seen the shape of history through the wall and stayed home.

He would not have.

I took a job.

That was all.

The western intake was exactly as boring as promised.

Beautifully boring.

One cracked mounting bracket.

Two valves installed badly enough that the current crew blamed the dead.

A pressure regulator drifting six percent under sustained load.

One access ladder that should have been condemned years earlier and had instead been painted.

Municipal tradition.

I spent the first day inside housings.

The second arguing with a supervisor who wanted to replace a whole transfer assembly because one sleeve had worn unevenly.

The assembly did not need replacing.

The sleeve did.

He called me cheap.

I called him welcome to pay for the assembly himself.

We kept the assembly.

At dinner, one of the younger workers sang.

Not well.

This was important only because the song was familiar.

Everybody knew it.

A revenge song.

Old enough to have several versions and recent enough that people still argued about which verse was original.

Three people wronged badly.

A house that refused restitution.

Years of pursuit.

A final return.

Blood answering blood.

First Life had liked that sort of song.

Not personally.

Culturally.

The rhythm was good.

People knew the chorus drunk.

I knew enough of it to join badly.

The worker pointed at me.

“You know this one?”

“Unfortunately.”

“Which version?”

“The ugly one.”

“They’re all ugly.”

“Then mine is authentic.”

He laughed.

We went back to eating.

That is how little the song meant to me then.

Even in Second Life.

I remembered the avengers.

I remembered why people thought they were justified.

I remembered the famous confrontation near the end.

I did not remember the people who lived around it.

History has foreground.

History has scenery.

In First Life, that town had been scenery.

I had never needed its name.

The third morning, we finished early.

I remember being pleased.

That detail stayed.

I packed my tools before lunch.

The supervisor signed the completion sheet without replacing the transfer assembly.

Growth.

My pay cleared through the cooperative office.

I checked the amount.

Also growth.

I bought sweet bread for the children on the way to the road station.

Two pieces.

Then four.

Nessa liked it too.

The road station was crowded.

Not unusual.

Then I noticed nobody was leaving north.

A freight driver stood beside his wagon swearing at a road clerk.

Two riders had come in hard enough that one horse was still foaming.

A woman was crying near the water trough.

That was unusual.

I slowed.

Somebody said fire.

I kept walking.

Fires happened.

Somebody else said Authority.

That narrowed nothing.

Then I heard one of the names from the song.

I stopped.

Not metaphorically.

My body stopped.

The sweet bread was warm through the paper in my hand.

A man near the station door said the name again.

One of the avengers.

Different pronunciation than the song.

Real people often disappoint music.

I crossed the room.

“What happened?”

He looked at me.

“Road’s closed.”

“What happened?”

“Fight.”

“Where?”

He named the district.

My district.

My brain did not understand.

Not immediately.

It offered geography.

Travel time.

Alternate roads.

Whether the southern bridge would still be open.

Useful things.

Cowardly things.

“Where in the district?”

He told me.

Closer.

I asked again.

He described the hill.

The old administrative house above the market road.

I knew it.

Everybody who lived there knew it.

The song had called it a hall.

Of course it had.

Songs hate municipal naming.

My hand tightened around the paper bag.

One of the loaves folded.

“Town?”

The man’s face changed.

He understood why I was asking.

“I don’t know.”

Wrong answer.

I grabbed his sleeve.

“Town?”

“Bad.”

One word.

That was enough.

I was moving before he finished the next sentence.

The north road was closed.

I took it anyway.

There are moments where later memory gives you drama you did not possess at the time.

This was not one of them.

I did not think about fate.

I did not think about the song.

I did not think about revenge.

I thought about distance.

My shoulder.

The weight of my bag.

How long I could run before the leg started cramping.

Where I could steal a horse without wasting time explaining why.

Which bridge would save twelve minutes.

Whether Nessa would take the children toward her mother’s house if something happened near the market.

Whether my father would hear the warning bells.

Whether Lena was working that day.

Whether the blue cup was on the table.

Everything became practical.

That was worse.

The first column of smoke became visible before I reached the northern bridge.

Too wide.

Too dark.

Not one building.

I stopped long enough to know that.

Then kept going.

In the cave, Ithar said nothing.

Good.

I did not need a question.

I could still feel the paper bag crushed in my hand.

Sweet bread.

Four pieces.

I had bought four because I was going home.
