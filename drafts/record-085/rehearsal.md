## RECORD 085

## THE MARSH

“It is stupid,” Tracy said.

We were standing over a map.

I had expected more ceremony.

“You haven’t heard the whole idea.”

“I heard enough.”

“You heard one sentence.”

“Yes.”

“That seems premature.”

“You said, ‘What if we stayed longer?’”

“Yes.”

“In Black Fen.”

“Yes.”

“Where people get poisoned.”

“Yes.”

“Repeatedly.”

“Yes.”

She looked at me.

“No.”

The others agreed immediately.

That was discouraging.

The expedition leader, Brann, actually laughed.

Not cruelly.

Professionally.

“No.”

“You too?”

“Especially me.”

He pointed at the map.

Black Fen was profitable in the same way a burning house could contain valuable furniture.

Technically true.

Operationally inconvenient.

Glassroot grew there.

Ugly plant.

Excellent money.

Its lower rhizome held a translucent resin used in high-grade wound sealants, pressure dressings, and several expensive stabilizing compounds.

It also grew best in the wet bands where everything else wanted to poison you.

Harvest crews did short pushes.

Get in.

Cut enough.

Get out.

If someone started showing symptoms, stop.

If someone showed serious symptoms, burn an antidote.

If the antidote failed, find a healer and begin becoming poor.

The economics were well known.

So well known nobody questioned them anymore.

A good run paid well.

A bad run erased three good ones.

A very bad run could cost a house.

Literally.

Brann had done that once.

He told the story while eating dried fruit.

“Late fenbite.”

“How late?”

“Couldn’t feel my left hand.”

Tracy looked at him.

“You waited that long?”

“I was working.”

“That was not my question.”

Brann smiled.

“Eight hours from first symptom.”

Tracy stopped smiling.

“How much?”

He named the amount.

I looked at him.

“That much?”

“Specialist vial.”

Tracy said, “Plus transport.”

Brann pointed at her.

“Plus transport.”

“Plus healer.”

“Plus healer.”

“Plus four days unable to work.”

“Six.”

Tracy looked at me.

There.

The face again.

I asked, “How often?”

Brann shrugged.

“Bad enough? Every few years.”

“Across how many crews?”

“Depends.”

“Roughly.”

He gave me the number.

I started doing math.

Tracy saw it.

“No.”

“I haven’t said anything.”

“You have your math face.”

“That is not a thing.”

“It is now.”

Brann looked between us.

“What are you trying to do?”

I pointed at the marsh.

“Farm it.”

He laughed again.

Harder.

“Absolutely not.”

“Why?”

“Because you’ll get poisoned.”

“We already get poisoned.”

“That is not an argument.”

“It is if poisoning is already priced into the work.”

Brann stopped laughing.

Not convinced.

Listening.

I continued.

“Right now the model is short entry, limited harvest, leave when exposure becomes expensive.”

“Yes.”

“What if we make exposure cheaper?”

Tracy folded her arms.

“No.”

I looked at her.

“Still?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because you skipped six steps.”

“Which?”

“Exactly.”

Annoying.

Ithar interrupted in the cave.

“She was your employee?”

“Partner.”

“You gave her half.”

“Yes.”

“And she told you no.”

“Constantly.”

“Did you resent that?”

“At first.”

He waited.

“Then?”

“I learned to ask why.”

The marsh smelled like warm rot.

That was the polite description.

The first trial used eight people.

I wanted twenty-four.

Tracy wanted six.

We compromised badly.

Eight.

Outer band only.

Three days.

No deep harvesting.

No overnight work inside the wet zone.

Everyone treated before entry.

Not immunity.

Tracy was aggressive about that language.

“Nothing makes you immune.”

Brann said, “Then what’s the point?”

“The point is making the poison work harder.”

She laid out the regimen.

Pre-exposure binder.

Skin seal at wrists, ankles, neck.

Boot treatment.

Breathing cloth for the low fog bands.

Scheduled wash.

Scheduled checks.

Mandatory rotation.

No one got to say they felt fine and skip the check.

Full antidote capacity still came with us.

Five doses.

Exactly what Brann would have carried before.

That confused him.

“I thought we were avoiding these.”

“We are.”

“Then why five?”

“Because avoiding a fire does not mean removing the water bucket.”

Brann looked at me.

“She always like this?”

“Yes.”

“I have known you eleven days,” Tracy said.

“Long eleven days.”

She ignored me.

The first morning went badly.

Not dangerously.

Badly.

The skin seal took too long.

One worker hated the breathing cloth.

Another had forgotten to eat enough and got lightheaded before exposure, which briefly made everyone think he was poisoned.

Tracy stopped the whole crew.

Made him eat.

Made us wait.

I complained about lost time.

She looked at me.

“Do you want data or theater?”

“Data.”

“Then shut up.”

Mentorship.

By noon, we were slower than a conventional crew.

Brann enjoyed this.

“Profitable.”

“Day one.”

“Mm.”

I hated him.

Then we found the first useful difference.

A harvester named Del had a minor exposure on the wrist.

Normally he would have washed it, watched symptoms, kept working unless something obvious developed.

Tracy caught him rubbing the same spot twice.

“Stop.”

“I’m fine.”

“Good. Stop while you’re cheap.”

That became one of her better sentences.

She checked the skin.

Tiny puncture.

Not dramatic.

Early enough that the local binder and wash protocol handled most of it.

Del lost forty minutes.

No full antidote.

No healer.

No half-day evacuation.

He went back in on the next rotation.

Brann watched that carefully.

So did I.

By the end of day one, we had harvested less glassroot than a conventional push.

Also used no antidotes.

Day two, the process got faster.

People knew the seal routine.

Gear was laid out in order.

Tracy moved one check station closer to the water because workers were wasting time crossing camp.

I wanted to increase the work interval.

She said no.

“Why?”

“Because today is not proof that yesterday was too cautious.”

Annoying.

Correct.

By day three we had harvested slightly more than a conventional three-day push.

Still no full antidotes.

Two early exposures.

One binder.

One topical treatment.

One worker rotated out half a day early because Tracy disliked the color under his nails.

He complained.

She ignored him.

That evening he developed mild symptoms.

They resolved overnight.

Brann stopped laughing.

We went home.

I wanted to scale immediately.

Tracy said no.

I argued.

She won.

Not because she outranked me.

Because when I finally stopped talking, she asked:

“How many runs tell you this works?”

“One.”

“No.”

“Two.”

“No.”

“Three.”

“Maybe.”

“Four?”

“Now you’re negotiating with math.”

We did four.

Not identical.

That mattered.

Different weather.

Different workers.

One deeper route.

One week with heavy insects.

One with low fog.

On the third run we used a full antidote.

One.

A worker slipped in water, tore the wrist seal, and took two exposures before anyone noticed.

Tracy treated early.

The antidote worked.

He lost a day.

Brann said, “There goes your theory.”

Tracy looked at him.

“No.”

“We used the expensive bottle.”

“Yes.”

“You said prevention.”

“I said reduction.”

Brann frowned.

Tracy pointed at the remaining four doses.

“Old pattern, that mistake becomes two or three people exposed while you’re evacuating him, then you start making expensive decisions under pressure.”

She tapped the used slot in the case.

“One bottle is not failure.”

That mattered to me.

Greg ideas liked clean success.

Tracy ideas tolerated controlled mess.

By the fourth run, the numbers stopped being subtle.

Glassroot harvested per worker-day: up.

Lost work time: down.

Full antidotes consumed: dramatically down.

Specialist healer cost: zero.

Emergency transport: zero.

Serious systemic poisoning: zero.

Not impossible.

Zero so far.

Tracy underlined that twice.

I looked at the totals.

Then at Brann.

“Profitable.”

He sighed.

“Yes.”

“More profitable.”

“Yes.”

“How much?”

He named the margin.

I smiled.

Tracy said, “Don’t.”

“What?”

“That face.”

“What face?”

“The one where you decide the first successful thing should become thirty things.”

I looked at the marsh.

She stepped between me and the map.

Physically.

Small person.

Effective.

“We can scale,” she said.

I stopped.

That was new.

Brann stopped too.

“You just spent a month saying no.”

“I spent a month saying not yet.”

She pointed at the numbers.

“Now we know where the failures are.”

I leaned in.

She moved her finger down the page.

“Seal application is still too slow.”

“Fixable.”

“Yes.”

“Check station placement.”

“Yes.”

“Training.”

“Yes.”

“Transport.”

She tapped that one harder.

“We are wasting money and time hauling people and root back every day.”

Brann said, “That’s why people don’t stay.”

Tracy ignored him.

“Storage is bad.”

“Yes.”

“Processing delay is worse.”

“Yes.”

“And if you increase harvest without increasing clean-water capacity, your prevention system collapses.”

I looked at her.

She looked back.

There.

That was the thing I had hired.

Not an answer.

Sequence.

I saw the finished operation.

Camp.

Walkways.

Processing.

Storage.

Transport.

Bigger crews.

Tracy saw the next stable state.

“Eight more workers,” she said.

“Sixteen.”

“Twelve.”

“Fourteen.”

“Twelve.”

“You are bad at compromise.”

“Twelve.”

“Fine.”

“Second wash station.”

“Fine.”

“Dedicated gear prep.”

“Fine.”

“Someone other than me trained to run exposure checks.”

“No.”

She stared.

I corrected.

“Fine.”

“Good.”

Brann looked between us.

“You two are going to ruin this marsh.”

Tracy looked at the glassroot totals.

“No.”

Then at me.

“We’re going to farm it.”

That was the first profitable thing we built together.

Not the largest.

Not even close.

But it taught both of us the wrong lesson.

Greg saw that a dangerous thing everybody had accepted could be redesigned.

Tracy saw what happened when somebody gave her enough capital to redesign the whole process instead of patching one exposure at a time.

The marsh made money.

Then more money.

Then enough money that the next question became unavoidable.

Why were we paying someone else to move all of it?
