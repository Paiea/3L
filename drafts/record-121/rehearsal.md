## RECORD 121

## THE CLEAN ROUTE

Kelan asked for the loop in year two.

Of course.

By then he had seen enough.

Not everything.

Enough.

Peg-Leg Greg stories traveled.

Quarry.

South Cut.

Sera, though people got that one wrong.

Hale.

Very wrong.

Some versions had me kill Hale by collapsing a mountain.

No.

Humanity again.

Kelan knew I could do things I had not taught him.

He waited almost a year before asking.

Growth.

We were training in a Guild yard.

Three people.

Kelan.

Me.

A rotating partner named Ena.

Good fighter.

Terrible patience.

Useful.

Kelan finished a paired reinforcement sequence cleanly.

Not strong.

Clean.

Then said:

“Show me the anchor.”

I knew immediately.

“What anchor?”

“Don’t.”

Fair.

“The one you use through the peg.”

“No.”

He stared.

“I’ve seen it.”

“Yes.”

“You said support should be practical.”

“Yes.”

“It is practical.”

“Yes.”

“Then why not?”

There.

Important.

Because it works.

Because it is one of the reasons I killed Hale.

Because it also helped close the architecture I am trying not to reproduce in you.

All true.

I chose the useful answer.

“Because you don’t need it.”

Kelan laughed.

“That is not your decision.”

Good.

He had learned.

I nodded.

“You’re right.”

That surprised him.

Then:

“Because it costs too much.”

“What?”

“Channel shape.”

He frowned.

I explained.

Not everything.

Enough.

Closed return.

Narrow routing.

Self-anchoring.

How repeated adaptation makes some paths easier and others less available.

How my solo architecture grew at the expense of relational breadth.

Kelan looked at my peg.

Then my hands.

“So it made you worse at support.”

“Yes.”

He looked offended.

“You are support.”

“Yes.”

“That sounds stupid.”

“Yes.”

“Then why did you do it?”

Hale.

Still answer.

“Revenge.”

Silence.

Ena stopped pretending not to listen.

Good.

Kelan asked:

“Did it work?”

“Yes.”

Bad answer.

True.

He looked interested.

There.

Danger.

I hated myself for seeing it.

Not because Kelan wanted revenge.

Because effective costs are tempting.

“What did you lose?”

“Possibility.”

“That is vague.”

“Yes.”

“Specifically.”

I thought about Plan A in a drawer.

Graph.

First-Life breadth.

S-class route.

“People.”

Kelan’s expression changed.

Not sentimental.

Confused.

“What?”

“I built magic that worked best when I did not need anyone else.”

“Yes.”

“That changes how you train.”

“Okay.”

“How you plan.”

“Okay.”

“How you trust.”

His face tightened.

There.

Less abstract.

“How you see help.”

Kelan looked at Ena.

Then me.

“You think the spell did that?”

“No.”

Important.

“I think I chose the spell because part of me wanted that.”

There.

Cleaner.

“The magic followed.”

Kelan thought.

Then:

“Still want it.”

Of course.

I laughed.

He did not.

“Why?”

“Because sometimes I am alone.”

Good.

Real.

Not stupidity.

I looked at him.

“You should have tools for alone.”

“Then?”

“Not this one.”

“That sounds like religion.”

Fair.

So I showed him something else.

Self-reinforcement.

Normal.

Healthy.

Barrier anchoring through terrain without closing the full return.

Object support.

Weapon stabilization.

Load routing that could vent outward if a partner appeared.

Solo-capable.

Not solo-committed.

He hated that distinction.

Then understood it months later.

Good.

Year three.

Kelan became better.

Not famous.

Important.

He reached C-class combat.

Support lagged behind.

Fine.

He remained a spear fighter who could link.

That made some evaluators uncomfortable.

Categories.

Always.

One report called him:

COMBAT REINFORCEMENT WITH SECONDARY SUPPORT CAPACITY.

Another:

HYBRID SUPPORT-COMBAT.

Kelan hated hybrid.

“What is wrong with it?”

“Sounds like livestock.”

Fair.

I liked relational support.

He hated that more.

“Sounds like therapy.”

Also fair.

We used:

Spear.

Good.

Rank language can wait.

His field value changed faster than his classification.

Familiar.

Teams started requesting him.

Not because he was the strongest fighter.

Because everyone near him got a little cleaner.

Not Greg cleaner.

Kelan cleaner.

Different.

He liked aggression more than I ever did.

His support followed.

He stabilized forward movement.

Reinforced pivots.

Shared recoil through paired strikes.

Helped another fighter commit harder without losing exit.

Support architecture built around combat rhythm.

His.

Good.

That mattered enormously.

If he had become me, the whole project would have been useless.

I did not say project.

Not out loud.

Tracy noticed.

Of course.

We watched Kelan work a four-person field drill.

He linked Ena and another fighter for six seconds.

Short.

Enough.

Then broke link before his own spear sequence.

Good choice.

Tracy said:

“He doesn’t support like you.”

“No.”

“You sound pleased.”

“I am.”

“Why?”

“Because he shouldn’t.”

Good answer.

She kept watching.

“Your notes are getting worse.”

“What?”

“More notes.”

“Better data.”

“Worse behavior.”

Fair.

I wrote less for a month.

Then more again.

Humans.

Year four.

Kelan failed badly.

Good.

Not field death.

Training.

A three-person link overloaded.

He tried to preserve everyone.

Wrong.

Support people love that mistake.

Nobody wants to choose who gets the failure.

Sometimes you must.

His whole structure collapsed.

Ena got thrown.

Kelan dislocated a shoulder.

Third partner vomited.

Excellent lesson.

After healing, Kelan was furious.

“At what?”

“Myself.”

“Why?”

“I saw it.”

“What?”

“The overload.”

“And?”

“I thought I could carry it.”

There.

Greg disease.

I knew.

“Could you?”

“No.”

“Then next time?”

He looked at me.

“Drop someone.”

“Ugly.”

“Yes.”

“Correct.”

He hated that.

Good.

We trained failure selection for months.

Not preventing collapse.

Choosing where collapse goes.

Cheap place.

Recoverable place.

Unimportant system.

Support is triage before blood.

Kelan learned.

Slow.

Good.

Year five.

He asked again for the loop.

Not because it was cool now.

Because he understood the cost.

That made the question harder.

“I still think I could use a small version.”

“Yes.”

He stopped.

“What?”

“Yes.”

“You said no.”

“I changed the answer.”

His eyes narrowed.

“Why?”

“Because now you understand what not to build.”

There.

We designed a limited self-anchor.

Not closed return.

No recursive route.

No training toward isolation.

A temporary stabilizer.

Tool.

Not architecture.

Kelan tested.

Useful.

Then stopped when I said.

Interesting.

He trusted the boundary because he had seen the alternative.

That was better than ignorance.

Tracy reviewed it.

Approved.

Not happily.

Enough.

“Do you wish you had done this instead?” Kelan asked.

Good question.

“No.”

He looked surprised.

“Why?”

Because then Hale might still be alive.

Honest.

“I needed what I built.”

“Even now?”

“No.”

There.

Different.

Kelan looked at the peg.

Then at me.

“Would you do it again?”

Hale’s question.

Again.

Everything circles.

I thought.

“If I had the same family.”

Same town.

Same Hale.

Same years.

“Yes.”

Kelan nodded.

Not approval.

Learning me.

Good.

Then:

“But you won’t teach me.”

“No.”

“Because?”

“You are not me.”

There.

Simple.

True.

That became the rule.

Not:

I know better.

Not:

My path was wrong.

Not:

Revenge corrupts.

Just:

You are not me.

So your architecture does not owe my history anything.

Ithar had become very still.

I noticed late.

“What?”

He looked at me.

“You spent five years preventing him from making your choices.”

“No.”

His eyes narrowed.

“I spent five years preventing him from making choices for reasons he did not have.”

“Difference?”

“Yes.”

“Important?”

“Very.”

He waited.

I hated when he waited.

“You let him keep solo tools.”

“Yes.”

“But not the architecture that closed you.”

“Yes.”

“You preserved his outward routes.”

“Yes.”

“Earlier than yours were preserved.”

“Yes.”

“You tracked his development against a baseline.”

I stopped.

There.

Not accusation yet.

Close.

I looked away.

“Kelan needed good training.”

“Of course.”

Dragon.

Annoying.

He let it pass.

For then.

Outside the memory, the western Line failed its first public test that winter.
