## RECORD 114

## THE DECISION

I spent six months proving Hale guilty.

Again.

That sounds stupid.

It was necessary.

Grief had already convicted him.

The town had already convicted him in my head.

Authority had already found misconduct.

Hale had admitted enough.

Still.

Before I killed him, I needed one thing clean.

Not:

Was Hale there?

Yes.

Not:

Did his revenge destroy the town?

Yes.

Not:

Did civilians die?

Yes.

Not:

Did he regret it?

Yes.

Not:

Would he do it again?

Yes.

I needed the decision.

The point where Hale Rusk knew what the next action would cost and chose it anyway.

That was harder.

Years blur responsibility.

Songs simplify.

Reports separate events into sections.

Witnesses remember impact better than sequence.

Everyone agrees afterward that something was inevitable.

Usually it was not.

I started with maps.

Old town.

Hill compound.

Market.

School.

Our street.

Drainage.

Pressure mains.

Subgrade support channels.

The house on the hill had been built into older municipal reinforcement.

Rich people enjoy attaching private structures to public systems.

Efficient.

Dangerous.

The hill compound had defensive anchors tied into the slope.

Not illegal when built.

Later modified.

Very illegal.

The man Hale came for had turned municipal load paths into private fortification.

That mattered.

When Hale’s group attacked, the hill did not fail alone.

Force moved into the town.

Not immediately.

First breach.

Localized.

Second.

Still mostly hill.

Then defenders sealed the upper route.

Hale had two options.

That was the part people argued about.

Option one:

Withdraw.

Regroup.

Bring Authority.

Lose the target, probably.

The hill man had an escape route.

Everyone knew.

Option two:

Force the lower anchor.

Faster.

Higher certainty.

Known propagation risk.

There.

I read that sentence twenty times.

KNOWN PROPAGATION RISK TO LOWER CIVIL INFRASTRUCTURE.

Authority engineering note.

Written before the final break.

Not after.

Before.

I looked at the timestamp.

Then at the casualty timeline.

Then back.

Tracy sat across the table.

Not consulting.

Food.

Different relationship now.

She watched me stop eating.

“What?”

I pushed the page over.

She read.

Then again.

“Oh.”

“Yes.”

“Who wrote it?”

“Authority engineer attached to the perimeter.”

“Was Hale shown?”

“That is what I am trying to prove.”

She looked at the next page.

“Command receipt?”

“Missing.”

“Convenient.”

“Yes.”

Not enough.

I kept going.

Three weeks later I found a deposition from one of Hale’s own people.

Not Joren.

A woman named Dema Orr.

Healer.

Not combat lead.

Not demolition.

Field medical.

She had survived.

Never charged.

Authority cleared her early because she stayed outside the breach and treated civilians after the collapse.

That mattered.

She had testified once.

Then disappeared from the story.

I found her living in a coastal town.

Small clinic.

Good reputation.

She recognized my name.

Of course.

Peg-Leg Greg travels faster than Greg.

She did not want to talk.

Reasonable.

I went away.

Came back a week later.

Not with food.

Growth.

She still said no.

I left again.

Third time she asked:

“Why do you keep coming?”

“Because you were there.”

“So were many people.”

“You were close enough to Hale to know when the last decision happened.”

Her face changed.

There.

I knew.

She let me inside.

Clinic closed.

Night.

Rain.

Of course.

Stories love rain.

Reality also has weather.

Dema made tea.

I did not drink much.

She did.

Hands steady.

Good healer.

“You are going to kill him,” she said.

“Yes.”

No reason to lie.

She looked at the peg.

“He knows.”

“Yes.”

“He told us you would.”

“When?”

“After the first fight.”

Of course.

“What did he say?”

“That you were going to become a problem.”

Fair.

“I need the hill.”

She looked away.

“The last breach.”

Still.

“The lower anchor.”

Her hand tightened around the cup.

There.

“Did he know?”

“Yes.”

Immediate.

Everything stopped.

Not physically.

Internally.

I had spent six months preparing for uncertainty.

Maybe.

Probably.

Reports suggest.

Hale says yes to everything because Hale is broken in some particular way.

Dema gave me yes.

Clean.

“How?”

She looked at me.

“I told him.”

There.

Not engineer.

Her.

“Exactly what?”

“That the lower pressure route was carrying into town.”

“Did you know civilians were still below?”

“Yes.”

“Did he?”

“I told him responders were still in the market.”

Market.

Not even abstract civilians.

Responders.

People trying to help.

“What did he say?”

Dema closed her eyes.

Not memory problem.

Pain.

Then:

“How long?”

I frowned.

“What?”

“That was what he asked.”

How long.

“To what?”

“For the lower route to clear.”

“And?”

“I said I did not know.”

There.

Uncertainty.

Could wait.

Could not know.

Target might escape.

“What happened?”

“He asked Joren whether the upper guard was breaking.”

“And?”

“Joren said no.”

“Then?”

“Hale told Sera to cut the lower anchor.”

Silence.

Tea cooling.

Rain.

Everything stupidly ordinary.

“That was the order.”

“Yes.”

“His.”

“Yes.”

“After you warned him.”

“Yes.”

“After you said responders were still below.”

“Yes.”

“Did you tell him not to?”

Dema looked at me.

“Yes.”

“What did he say?”

Her mouth tightened.

“He said, ‘I know.’”

There.

Not rage.

Not accident.

Not failed map.

Decision.

I sat.

Dema looked at the peg again.

“I helped afterward.”

“I know.”

“I treated people.”

“I know.”

“I did not know the whole town would go.”

“I know.”

“Hale did not either.”

That mattered.

I waited.

“He knew it could spread.”

“Yes.”

“But not how far.”

“Yes.”

“You understand the difference.”

“Yes.”

She studied me.

“You still intend to kill him.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

There it was.

Not because he knew my house would collapse.

He did not.

Not because he targeted my children.

He did not.

Not because he wanted civilians dead.

He did not.

Because he was warned the lower route carried into a living town.

Responders were still below.

He had a slower option.

He chose certainty on his revenge target instead.

He accepted civilian risk as cost.

The exact thing he later said he would choose again.

“That was enough,” I said.

Dema looked tired.

“Was it?”

“Yes.”

“For death?”

“Yes.”

She did not argue.

That was worse.

“Do you think he deserves it?”

Long pause.

“I think I stopped knowing what deserve meant that night.”

Fair.

“Do you forgive him?”

“No.”

“Would you help me kill him?”

“No.”

Also fair.

“Would you stop me?”

She looked at me.

“I would stop you here.”

Clinic.

Civilians.

Good.

“Anywhere else?”

“I would call Authority.”

“Also fair.”

She almost smiled.

Then did not.

I left.

No threat.

No thanks.

What do you say?

Thank you for confirming the decision that got my family killed.

Bad.

I told Tracy.

All of it.

She read the notes.

Then asked:

“Does this change anything?”

“Yes.”

“How?”

“I know what I am killing.”

She hated that sentence.

I saw it.

Did not retract.

Hale was not the town.

Not the song.

Not revenge in human form.

Not every dead person condensed into one body.

He was a man who received a warning.

Asked how long.

Got uncertainty.

Saw his target might escape.

Then ordered the lower anchor cut anyway.

That was his guilt.

Specific.

Finite.

Enough.

Ithar listened.

Then asked:

“And if Dema had said Hale never received the warning?”

I hated the question.

Good dragon.

I thought.

Longer than I wanted.

“I would still hate him.”

“That was not my question.”

No.

It was not.

“Would you still kill him?”

I looked at the cave floor.

Maybe.

Bad answer.

Honest.

“I do not know.”

Ithar waited.

“That is why I asked.”

There.

Good.

The six months mattered.

Revenge did not become cleaner.

It became narrower.

Hale had made a decision.

So would I.
