## RECORD 014

## THE INSPECTOR

Inspection turned out to be mostly asking old people where doors used to be.

That disappointed me.

I had imagined instruments.

Plans.

Technical arguments.

Possibly a coat.

Halden did eventually give me a coat.

It was brown.

I gave it back.

The old people were more useful.

My tenth year at East Four began with district engineering admitting, very carefully, that East Four was not unique.

They did not say they had lost parts of the Line.

Institutions rarely use the interesting sentence first.

The official sentence was:

Potential discrepancies between legacy construction and current station documentation.

It took three meetings to produce that.

Dera read the notice at the pressure desk and translated it in less than a second.

“They don't know what's under anything.”

Halden, standing beside her with a folder under one arm, said, “That is not what it means.”

Dera looked at him.

“Do you know what's under East Two?”

“No.”

“South Three?”

“No.”

“Market Five?”

“We have drawings.”

“That wasn't my question.”

Halden rubbed his face.

I liked him best when Dera was helping.

The district started with four stations.

East Four because we already knew our foundation plans were fiction in at least one important place.

East Two because two construction records disagreed about how many foundation levels existed.

South Three because an operator there had been writing pressure drift in the same logbook margin for twenty-six years.

Market Five because somebody moved a shelving unit and found a door behind it.

Nobody could agree whether opening the door counted as maintenance or archaeology.

Halden asked me to join the survey two days a week.

“Why?”

“You notice old construction.”

“So do masons.”

“We have one.”

“Then you don't need me.”

“You understand station operation.”

“So does Dera.”

“She threatened to retire if I put another committee on her schedule.”

“That sounds like Dera.”

“She recommended you.”

“That does not sound like Dera.”

From the pressure desk, without looking up, Dera said, “I recommended sending you away two days a week.”

Nessa turned one page of a transfer sheet.

“Can we make it three?”

“Fuck both of you.”

Halden smiled.

The official title was provisional legacy inspector.

My father was impressed.

That should have warned me.

My mother asked whether it paid more.

Better question.

It did.

A little.

Not enough for the coat.

The coat came with the position anyway.

District brown. Brass buttons. INSPECTION stitched over the inside pocket in letters large enough to make theft embarrassing.

I tried it on at home.

Nessa looked up from the table.

“No.”

“I haven't said anything.”

“You look like a man who fines bakeries.”

“I inspect Line stations.”

“You look like you inspect bread weight.”

I took it off.

Halden was offended when I returned it the next morning.

“You need to be identifiable.”

“I have a badge.”

“You always have a badge.”

“Yes.”

“That identifies you as a Ranker.”

“Good.”

“You are not there as a Ranker.”

That stopped me.

Halden saw it and became irritatingly pleased.

“Exactly.”

“What am I there as?”

“Someone who asks questions and writes down the answers.”

“That sounds worse.”

“It usually is.”

My inspection partner wore the coat.

Of course she did.

Tova Renn was fifty-two, formally certified in structural survey, foundations, and station reconstruction. She carried three hammers and trusted none of my professional habits yet.

Small hammer.

Medium hammer.

One narrow-headed thing with a worn leather grip.

“The liar,” she said.

“Why?”

“Old mortar lies.”

“Mortar does not talk.”

“Then why do you stare at walls?”

I liked her less after that.

For about a week.

East Two changed my mind.

The current plan showed one foundation level.

The construction book showed two.

Pell, the station foreman, said there had always been one.

The retired foreman disagreed.

He lived above a cooper's yard two streets away and answered the door wearing one boot.

Tova asked about the lower service walk before I could.

He scratched his jaw.

“Used to carry lantern oil down there.”

“How many steps?” she asked.

“Six.”

Pell frowned.

“There is no stair.”

“There was.”

“What year?”

The old man looked at Pell.

“Before him.”

“That narrows it to thirty years,” Tova said.

“He was thin then.”

Pell straightened.

“I am still thin.”

The old man studied him.

“No.”

We went back to East Two.

The tool room smelled like oil, wet wool, and the sharp mineral dust people stop smelling after twenty years in the same building.

The current wall was plaster over older brick.

Pell stood with his arms folded while Tova moved slowly along it, tapping.

Tap.

Tap.

Tap.

Then a different sound.

Not dramatic.

Just hollow enough that once I heard it, I could not unhear it.

Tova tapped twice more.

Same place.

She took chalk from her coat pocket and drew a square.

“There.”

Pell stared at the mark.

“That's been wall since I started.”

Tova shrugged.

“Wall is a job. It can be reassigned.”

We opened it carefully.

That was disappointing too.

No ancient machine.

No hidden treasure.

No monster waiting patiently behind municipal plaster.

Six steps.

A landing.

Standing water.

Three dead rats.

One live rat that objected to our arrival and made Pell swear loudly enough to echo.

Then a passage running beneath the station.

Tova lifted her lantern.

The brick was older than East Two.

Much older.

I knew that before she did.

My stomach tightened before my mind caught up.

Ninth-station work.

Lower-channel access.

First-life places I had entered after something had already gone wrong.

By then the old construction had usually been broken open, burned, flooded, or excavated around by people trying to understand the failure backward.

Here it was intact enough to be boring.

That made it stranger.

Tova turned toward me.

“You know this brick?”

“Kind of.”

“From where?”

Bad question.

“Old contracts.”

She waited.

That answer had lasted for years.

It was aging badly.

Nessa's voice arrived in my head without permission.

Reduced mysterious bullshit.

I tried again.

“I've seen later Line work built over construction like this. North and west.”

“When?”

I looked at the wet floor.

“Years ago.”

Tova kept looking at me.

Not convinced.

Not her problem yet.

She turned back toward the passage.

Unlike Nessa, she was being paid to inspect walls, not me.

We went down.

The ceiling forced me to duck in two places.

Cold water soaked through one boot before we reached the first support.

Old marks showed where plaster had peeled away.

Half circles.

Short bars.

A hooked line beside every third support.

I knew the family of notation.

Not the meaning of every mark.

Important difference.

First-life Greg had treated marks like those as field knowledge. Stand here. Do not stand there. This section carries something. That wall is bad. Later, engineers explained mechanisms after rubble existed.

At twenty-nine in Life Two, I had less authority and more time.

It was an improvement.

Pell pointed at the hooked line.

“What does that mean?”

“I don't know.”

Tova looked over.

“You stared like you knew.”

“I know it matters.”

“Different statement.”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

She crouched beside the support and used the liar.

Tap.

Tap.

Then she put two bare fingers against the brick.

“Warm.”

I touched it.

She was right.

East Two's active feed ran thirty feet above us.

Nothing on the current plan should have warmed that wall.

Pell crouched beside us.

“Old drain?”

“Could be,” Tova said.

“Steam service?”

“Could be.”

I put my palm flat against the brick.

There.

A vibration.

Faint.

Broad.

Not the clean hum of a modern feed.

Something slower.

My body recognized it before my mind had language.

East Four.

Pressure through stone.

Wrongness under my hand.

“Active load,” I said.

Tova's eyes moved to me.

“Based on?”

“Vibration.”

She touched the wall again.

Pell did too.

He shook his head.

“I don't feel anything.”

“Neither do I,” Tova said.

I almost insisted.

The old reflex arrived cleanly.

I know.

They do not.

Therefore the world should move.

Then I remembered East Four and the wrong feed.

I had remembered the outcome and mistaken that for understanding the mechanism.

Feeling was not measurement either.

I took my hand off the wall.

“Fine. Let's test it.”

Tova smiled for the first time.

Not warmly.

Professionally.

Better.

We stayed underground most of the day.

Chalk dust along the support joints.

Weighted thread against the wall.

Marks on the floor where the weight settled.

Pell went upstairs twice to change station load while Tova and I watched a piece of string.

Very advanced work.

At fourth bell the upper station took more market load.

The weight shifted.

A finger width.

Returned.

Shifted again when Pell opened the east branch.

Tova stopped smiling.

“That is connected.”

“Yes.”

“Not necessarily carrying pressure.”

“No.”

Pell looked between us.

“What else moves when station load changes?”

Tova counted on her fingers.

“Foundation strain. Shared masonry. Water. Heat. Ten other things.”

She dusted chalk from her knee.

“We do not pick the interesting answer because it is interesting.”

That was when I started liking her.

A lot.

Two days later we opened another section of wall.

Inside was an old bronze sleeve.

Not a pipe exactly.

Too broad.

Flattened oval.

It crossed beneath the station and vanished east into stone.

The reconstruction book from sixty-three years earlier described that foundation as SOLID FILL.

It was not solid.

It was not fill.

Both words had survived three generations of copied plans.

Nobody had needed corruption.

Nobody had needed conspiracy.

One confident assumption and good handwriting had been enough.

That frightened me more.

Halden came down that afternoon.

His boots were too clean when he entered and ruined by the time he reached us.

Good.

Tova showed him the sleeve.

He stared at it.

Then at me.

“Don't.”

“I didn't say anything.”

“You have the face.”

“What face?”

Tova said, “He has several.”

Traitor.

Halden crouched beside the bronze.

“Active?”

“Responsive,” Tova said.

“Difference?”

“We can prove station load affects it. We cannot prove what it carries.”

Halden nodded immediately.

No ego.

Still one of his best qualities.

“What do you recommend?”

Tova looked at me.

I looked at her.

“Keep access open,” I said.

“Monitor,” she said.

“Do not isolate anything connected to it until we understand what else moves.”

“Agreed.”

Pell frowned.

“We have a live unknown under my station and the recommendation is leave it alone?”

Tova stood and wiped her fingers on the brown inspection coat.

“No. The recommendation is stop pretending touching something is safer than understanding it.”

Pell looked at the sleeve again.

Then nodded.

Competent people agreeing cautiously is terrible storytelling.

It is excellent maintenance.

The survey expanded.

Four stations became seven.

Then eleven.

Not all at once.

One bad drawing at a time.

I stayed at East Four through all of it.

That mattered.

Two days inspecting most weeks. Three or four doing ordinary station work. Sometimes nights when schedules went wrong because the Line did not care about my title.

I still changed seals.

Still cleared grit traps.

Still got yelled at by Dera for returning tools to the wrong cabinet.

The inspection work made the ordinary work look different.

At South Three, Kel Arno met us with twenty-six years of irritation already prepared.

She was sixty-four and had been recording pressure drift in the same logbook margin since before I started at East Four.

Halden brought six reports.

Kel looked through them.

“I sent six.”

Halden checked his list.

“Eight.”

She looked offended.

“Then why did you only bring copies of six?”

Halden stared at her.

I laughed.

That did not help.

We found the missing two later.

Her first report had been filed under meter calibration.

The second under seasonal variance.

Another became an attachment to a budget request.

Another was marked resolved after somebody replaced the meter.

The drift remained.

Nothing had disappeared.

The institution had remembered almost everything.

Just not in one place where a person looking for pressure drift would find it.

Kel stood beside the old station book while Halden read the filing trail aloud.

“So I wasn't ignored,” she said.

Halden hesitated.

“Not exactly.”

“That is worse somehow.”

He looked at me.

I had nothing.

She was right.

At Market Five, the hidden door produced less philosophy.

It opened into a dry room full of retired gauges.

Seventy years of them.

Round brass faces. Cracked glass. Missing needles. One still attached to a length of pipe that went nowhere.

I stood in the doorway.

“This is it?”

Tova looked delighted.

“You wanted treasure.”

“No.”

“You look disappointed.”

“I was promised archaeology.”

“Old gauges are archaeology.”

“They are old gauges.”

“That is what archaeology mostly is. Old versions of things people still complain about.”

We spent six hours cataloging them.

One still worked.

Badly.

Tova mounted it over her desk.

I did not understand that either.

While my work widened, Nessa's did too.

Not because mine did.

That distinction became important enough that I remember correcting myself about it even then.

She had earned senior transfer qualification the year before. Now she was training junior pressure hands and taking more scheduling responsibility from Dera.

Nessa hated scheduling.

People lied about weddings.

People invented sick cousins.

One man apparently lost three grandmothers in the same winter.

“Could be complicated family structure,” I said over dinner.

“He has one grandmother.”

“How do you know?”

“I asked his mother.”

“That feels excessive.”

“He asked for sixth bell off three times.”

“Carry on.”

For a while we saw less of each other.

Not badly.

Just less.

Two people gaining responsibility do not become more available because the relationship is going well.

That would be convenient.

I came home late from East Two and found a note beside cold food.

SOUTH SIX TOMORROW. DON'T WAIT FOR DINNER.

I left before she woke two mornings later and wrote beneath it:

MARKET FIVE. PROBABLY HOME BY EIGHTH.

Then, because we had learned something from Record Twelve:

I HAVE MONEY. COAL IS PAID.

Nessa added later:

ROMANCE.

We moved ledger entries without explanation and argued about them the next morning.

She learned that the aunt I still forgot to ask about preferred plum preserves.

I learned nothing from that except that I remained bad at tracking her family.

My father worked fewer mill shifts.

My mother stopped pretending stairs did not hurt her knees.

My nephew learned to read and immediately became intolerable because he discovered signs could be corrected.

I turned thirty.

Thirty had felt old when I was nineteen the first time.

The second time it felt like Thursday.

Probably healthier.

By the end of the survey's first year, district engineering had a new map.

Not complete.

I had become suspicious of that word.

Better.

Known modern feeds.

Known retired feeds.

Suspected legacy works.

Observed responsive structures.

Unverified spaces.

Tova gave every category its own line style.

I complained until she told me confusion was what happened when reality refused to fit four kinds of ink.

I stopped complaining.

Mostly.

East Two's bronze sleeve remained unexplained.

So did three related structures beneath other stations.

They did not form a pattern I trusted.

Old Greg can see more shape in those marks now.

Young Greg could not.

At thirty, I had eleven stations on a district map, three warm walls, two buried structures that reacted to load, and a growing suspicion that modern stations were not separate machines sitting on dead foundations.

That was enough to change how I worked.

It was not enough to tell me why.

At the end of the year, Halden called me into his office and put a new badge on top of a packet.

I picked up the badge first.

“Permanent?”

“Yes.”

“Does this mean I have to wear the coat?”

“No.”

“Good.”

“It means you can sign survey findings without me countersigning every page.”

I looked at him.

“That sounds worse.”

“It is more responsibility.”

“Same thing.”

He smiled and pushed the packet toward me.

“What is this?”

“East Two operating review.”

“I thought Tova was doing it.”

“She is.”

“Then why do I have one?”

“Because she disagrees with you.”

I opened the packet.

The old bronze sleeve under East Two had begun moving more under winter load.

Not much.

Enough.

Tova's recommendation sat on the first page.

IMMEDIATE ISOLATION OF EAST MARKET BRANCH PENDING LEGACY STRUCTURE IDENTIFICATION.

I read it twice.

Then I looked at Halden.

I did not agree.

Not yet.
