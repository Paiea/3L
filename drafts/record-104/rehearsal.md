## RECORD 104

## THE SAME HANDS

The eastern aqueduct station had already lost one wall.

That was the first thing I saw.

The second was Hale.

Of course.

He stood waist-deep in runoff inside the lower transfer court, holding a cracked support beam in place with one shoulder while six workers crawled under it.

Same man.

Different day.

Missing finger.

Blood on one sleeve.

Calm.

I hated him immediately.

Useful.

The station moved water through three districts.

Most of the machinery was below street level.

The failure had begun in a pressure chamber under the residential slope.

Too much transfer.

Bad valve.

Old reinforcement.

Nobody knew which yet.

Did not matter.

Water had found the weak route and started removing everything that had once made the building stable.

Stone.

Mortar.

Soil.

Patience.

The outer yard had become mud.

The lower chamber had become a throat.

Every few seconds the whole structure made a sound you could feel in your teeth.

Authority workers were evacuating houses above.

Not fast enough.

Hale saw me.

His face changed.

Not surprise.

Recognition.

Then work.

“Support?”

“Yes.”

He looked at Tracy.

“Healer?”

“Yes.”

She said, “Tracy.”

Hale nodded once.

No history.

Good.

Stone groaned.

“Twenty seconds.”

I looked at the load.

“No.”

His eyes moved to me.

“Thirty.”

“Forty-five.”

He almost argued.

Then stopped.

Good.

Maybe he had learned too.

I went into the water.

Cold.

Fast.

Bad footing.

The peg hated all of it.

Tracy grabbed my arm.

“Surface?”

“Stone under silt.”

“Loop?”

“Low.”

“Keep it low.”

Current consent.

Good.

I set the first Barrier under the failed beam.

Not to hold it.

Too much.

Define the edge.

Stop the slipping face from grinding wider.

Second line to the standing column.

Third through the broken stair base.

The old support language came back quickly.

Structure.

Transfer.

Failure.

Hale felt it.

Of course.

His shoulder settled.

Not relaxed.

Better.

“What do you need?” he asked.

I hated that question.

Because it was correct.

“Move six inches right.”

He moved.

No ego.

No hesitation.

The workers underneath kept crawling.

One injured man.

Two conscious.

One screaming.

Good.

Screaming meant breathing.

Usually.

“Left hand higher.”

Hale adjusted.

His missing finger changed his grip.

I noticed.

Again.

Always.

“Now.”

He drove reinforcement through the beam.

Not maximum.

Exactly enough.

That surprised me.

People think strong fighters solve structural problems with more.

Hale did not.

He knew the difference between holding and breaking.

Of course he did.

He had destroyed a town.

He had also held a river wall.

Same hands.

I linked to him.

That was the worst part.

Not physically.

Emotionally.

First-Life support had always been intimate in a practical way.

You learn people through load.

Where they carry.

Where they waste.

Where they lie about fatigue.

Where the body gets clever.

Hale’s body was disciplined.

Very.

His reinforcement entered clean.

Spread clean.

Returned clean.

He wasted almost nothing.

A-class.

Named.

The hand without the finger compensated automatically.

Not perfectly.

Good enough.

I hated knowing that.

“Less shoulder,” I said.

He adjusted.

“You’re overloading the right hip.”

Adjusted.

“Stop correcting before I finish.”

He looked at me.

Then did not.

Good.

The last worker cleared.

“Out,” I said.

Hale moved.

Beam dropped.

Barrier failed.

Stone hit water.

The whole court jumped.

We both went down.

My peg slipped.

Loop caught.

Too much.

Pain through the socket.

Tracy shouted my name.

I got up.

“Hale?”

“Here.”

Of course.

He surfaced three feet away.

Cut over one eyebrow.

Looked at me.

“You held too long.”

“I know.”

Tracy reached us.

“No, he does not.”

Good.

Not now.

She checked the socket by touch.

Then my shoulder.

“Still intact.”

“Encouraging.”

“Not praise.”

She turned to Hale.

“Your side.”

“I’m fine.”

She stared at him.

I almost laughed.

Same mistake.

“Shirt.”

Hale looked at me.

“No help.”

He removed the outer layer.

Deep bruise along the ribs.

Not broken.

Probably.

Tracy pressed.

He did not react.

She pressed harder.

He did.

“Good.”

Hale frowned.

I smiled.

Tracy looked at me.

“Do not.”

Fair.

A runner came down the remaining stairs.

“Upper slope is moving.”

Of course.

The station had not failed once.

Systems rarely do.

The lower chamber collapse had started undermining the residential retaining wall.

Houses above.

Families.

People still moving.

Hale put the shirt back on.

“Lead?”

The runner said, “Engineering says ten minutes.”

I looked at the wall.

“No.”

Hale looked at me.

“How long?”

“Three.”

The runner went pale.

Good.

Useful fear.

Hale said, “Evacuation route?”

“East street.”

“Too slow.”

He looked at me.

“Can you hold the wall?”

“No.”

Immediate.

He nodded.

No insult.

Better question.

I pointed.

“Not wall. Load path.”

Old transfer arch.

Drain channel.

Collapsed lower chamber.

The retaining wall was not failing uniformly.

Could maybe make the missing support exist somewhere else for a few minutes.

Not alone.

I hated where the answer went.

“Hale.”

“Yes.”

“I need you under it.”

Tracy looked at me.

Then at him.

Hale did not.

He was already looking at the route.

“Where?”

I showed him.

“Bad position.”

“Yes.”

“If it goes?”

“You die.”

He nodded.

Normal sentence.

I hated that too.

“Anything better?”

“No.”

“Then move.”

No drama.

He went.

Tracy grabbed my coat.

“Greg.”

“I know.”

“No, you don’t.”

“You are opening outward.”

“Yes.”

“Full?”

“No.”

“Do not close the loop at the same time.”

“I may need it for footing.”

“No.”

“Tracy.”

“If you need footing, move.”

“I cannot move and hold Hale.”

There.

Her eyes changed.

Hale.

The enemy.

Now part of my architecture.

Disgusting.

Necessary.

Tracy understood.

“Then I anchor you.”

“What?”

She pointed at the intact stair rail.

“Mechanical.”

Of course.

Not elegant.

Better than channel compromise.

“Do not make magic solve a rope problem.”

Old Tracy could have said that.

Current Tracy did.

Good.

Water to his knees.

Stone dust falling.

People still running above.

I linked.

First to him.

Then arch.

Then lower floor.

Then drain edge.

Support moved outward.

Real outward.

Not the loop.

For the first time since Nessa died, I opened more than I wanted.

Hale felt it.

His head turned.

“Greg.”

“Shut up.”

He did.

Good.

I reinforced his stance.

Not strength.

Release.

His body had more available than it was safely using.

A-class bodies often do.

The limiter is not power.

It is what joints, organs, channels, and ground can survive when power arrives.

I gave him route.

Hip to floor.

Shoulder to arch.

Hand to wall.

Missing finger compensated through the wrist.

Load moved.

Not stable.

Paused.

Good enough.

No speeches.

No heroism.

Shoes.

Children.

Bags.

Life.

I felt it before I saw it.

“Not yet.”

“It’s moving.”

“I know.”

“I can hold more.”

“Your right knee can’t.”

Silence.

He lowered slightly.

That was support.

Not telling stronger people what they could do.

Telling them what their body could survive doing twice.

Tracy watched my hands.

“Shoulder.”

“I know.”

“Greg.”

The strap took some.

Good.

Then five.

Bad.

Hale’s breathing changed.

I noticed.

Of course.

“Slow.”

“Do not lock the left wrist.”

“Your hand.”

“I know.”

“No, the missing side.”

From himself maybe.

“Shift grip.”

“There is nowhere.”

More dangerous because Greg was attached.

Not Hale.

Support.

Old self.

Outward.

Useful.

People alive because connection worked.

Later.

Not now.

“Clear!”

“Check.”

“Check every house again.”

Good.

Of course.

Two workers ran.

The wall moved.

I felt the limit.

“Hale.”

“Wait.”

“No.”

“One more.”

“No.”

“Greg.”

I almost laughed.

He had learned my name as an argument.

“Thirty seconds.”

“No.”

“Twenty.”

Same disease.

Different man.

“Ten.”

Ten.

Not twenty.

Ten.

Of course.

Not completely.

Enough.

Show-off.

Contained.

Enough for engineers to work.
