## RECORD 126

## THE GRAMMAR

Ithar made me repeat Nhal’s exact words three times.

Not the meaning.

Words.

That mattered.

Ancient beings are irritatingly specific about language when the language is old enough.

I gave him what I remembered.

Not perfect.

Memory never is.

Pava.

The pressure shift.

Nhal watching.

Her warning before the lower system claimed the route.

Her refusal to describe the containment as simple imprisonment.

The way she spoke about release like it was a structural event.

The hidden name.

The route.

The conditions.

Ithar interrupted constantly.

“What word?”

“That one.”

“Not the modern word.”

“It is the word I remember.”

“Sound?”

I repeated it.

His eyes narrowed.

“Again.”

I did.

Then he asked where Nhal had been looking when she said it.

That annoyed me.

“How does that matter?”

“Everything matters when you have only one witness.”

Fair.

I remembered.

Not at me.

At the lower wall.

No.

Behind it.

Maybe.

I told him.

He went still.

Good.

Bad.

“What?”

“She was not warning you about herself.”

I stared.

“What?”

“The sentence.”

“I know the sentence.”

“You know the translation.”

Same problem.

Ancient language compressed things badly into modern speech.

I hated this.

“What was she warning us about?”

Ithar looked at me.

“The load.”

There.

Simple.

Of course.

Not power.

Not monster.

Load.

He shifted one claw across the cave floor.

Stone scratched.

Then he drew three lines.

Dragon diagram.

Good.

First line.

“Nhal.”

Second.

“Containment.”

Third.

“Foundation.”

I looked.

“That is too simple.”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

He stared.

I stopped.

He pointed to Nhal.

“Humans at Vey believed this was the problem.”

“Yes.”

“Containment around her.”

“Yes.”

“Therefore remove containment.”

“Yes.”

“Wrong.”

“Obviously, in hindsight.”

“No.”

His claw tapped the third line.

“You still think in hindsight.”

I frowned.

“What?”

“You believe the danger was that removing the containment would release Nhal.”

“That was part of it.”

“Yes.”

“Not all.”

“No.”

The claw moved from containment to foundation.

“The system did not merely hold her.”

“What did it hold?”

“Load.”

“From what?”

He looked at me.

“No.”

Fuck.

“Because you do not know?”

“I know possibilities.”

“Then say them.”

“No.”

“Ithar.”

He ignored me.

“The distinction is enough.”

Fine.

For now.

He drew another set of lines.

More.

Longer.

Branches.

“The Line.”

I watched.

Human maintenance layer.

Transfer bands.

Deep anchors.

Ground paths.

Adjacent stations.

Not exact map.

Conceptual.

Then he drew a loop.

“There.”

My stomach changed.

“That is not supposed to happen.”

“No.”

“The Line should vent load down.”

“According to humans.”

“Yes.”

“According to builders?”

I stopped.

There.

We did not know.

Ithar’s claw tapped the loop.

“You assume return is failure because return is new to you.”

“Yes.”

“It may be.”

“Helpful.”

“It may also be fallback behavior.”

Worse.

“Built in?”

“Possibly.”

“Why?”

He looked at me.

“What does your loop do?”

There.

I hated where this was going.

“It keeps load inside a controlled route.”

“When outward transfer is unavailable?”

“Yes.”

“When ground cannot take more?”

“Yes.”

“When a partner does not exist?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“To survive.”

Ithar nodded.

The Line diagram remained.

No.

No.

“You think the Line is closing.”

“I think part of it may be.”

“On purpose.”

“Do not give machines purpose casually.”

Fair.

“By design.”

“Possibly.”

That word should also be illegal.

“What would force fallback?”

“Loss of sinks.”

“Deep anchors.”

“Perhaps.”

“Failure below.”

“Perhaps.”

“Then we repair the anchors.”

Ithar looked at me.

“Do you know what they anchor into?”

No.

There.

Problem.

Humans called them ground anchors.

That did not mean ground was the final destination.

Names are often where ignorance becomes furniture.

“What if the anchor is not an endpoint,” I said.

Ithar said nothing.

Good.

“What if it transfers deeper.”

Still.

“What if all we call grounding is just another route.”

His eyes narrowed.

Better.

Then load had never disappeared.

Of course.

It went somewhere humans did not monitor.

Maybe for centuries.

Maybe longer.

“How long can a system do that?”

“Depends on the system.”

“Useful.”

“You asked a useless question.”

Fair.

I looked at Nhal’s three lines.

Containment.

Foundation.

“Same grammar.”

“Yes.”

“Not same machine.”

“No.”

“But same idea.”

“Closer.”

I hated closer.

“What idea?”

Ithar looked at the diagrams.

Then at me.

“Failure is allowed.”

That surprised me.

“What?”

“Humans build many systems to prevent failure.”

“Yes.”

“Older systems often assume failure.”

“Why?”

“Because everything fails.”

Good dragon.

Bad news.

“So they route it.”

“Yes.”

“Stage it.”

“Yes.”

“Trade one failure for another.”

“Yes.”

There.

Support.

Of course.

Contain.

Stabilize.

Repair.

Recover.

Tracy’s medicine.

First-Life Greg.

Nhal.

Line.

Same logic at different scales.

No system avoids cost.

Good systems choose where cost lands.

I looked at the loop.

“The Line is running out of cheap failures.”

Ithar’s head shifted.

There.

Better.

“Maybe.”

Fuck maybe.

“If the lower anchors stop taking load, it returns upward.”

“Yes.”

“And human maintenance keeps pushing it back down.”

“Yes.”

“So we may be forcing it into fewer remaining sinks.”

“Yes.”

“Making them fail faster.”

“Possibly.”

I closed my eyes.

Great.

Human maintenance might be accelerating the deep problem while keeping the visible Line stable.

Not because people were stupid.

Because they were repairing what they could measure.

Classic.

Tracy would hate it.

Then love the data.

“What about Nhal?”

I asked.

Ithar’s attention changed again.

Personal.

“She may be inside a system that made the same bargain.”

“What bargain?”

“Local containment in exchange for deeper stability.”

I stared.

“Nhal is a sink?”

“No.”

Hard.

Good.

“Do not reduce a person to a function because a system uses them.”

That landed.

Kelan.

Greg.

People.

Good.

“Then?”

“She may be one component.”

“Component is not better.”

“No.”

He looked irritated.

Good.

“She may be held at a junction.”

That was different.

“Between loads.”

“Perhaps.”

“Her presence matters structurally.”

“Perhaps.”

“Removing her changes the route.”

“Yes.”

There.

That fit Vey.

Why the containment could not just break.

Why Pava’s test was claimed by the lower system.

Why Nhal knew Ithar’s name.

Why she gave route instead of asking Greg to free her.

She knew.

Of course.

“She never asked us to break it.”

“No.”

“She could have.”

“Yes.”

“Why didn’t she?”

Ithar looked at me.

“You tell me.”

Because Nhal was not stupid.

Because she understood enough.

Because release might kill her.

Or others.

Or something below.

Because freedom without a replacement load path is just collapse.

I said:

“She needed someone who could understand the structure first.”

Ithar’s eyes stayed on me.

“Perhaps.”

I hated perhaps.

Then:

“That is why she sent you here.”

Silence.

I had always treated Nhal’s gift as access.

Name.

Route.

A future option.

Maybe it had always been a referral.

Ancient beings.

Terrible communication.

“She knew you would understand.”

Ithar did not answer.

Personal again.

Good.

“What do we do?”

I asked.

“Nothing.”

I stared.

“No.”

“Yes.”

“The Line is failing.”

“Yes.”

“Nhal may be trapped.”

“Yes.”

“And your plan is nothing.”

“For now.”

I hated him.

Good.

“Why?”

“Because action without model is how your Pava died.”

There.

Clean.

No argument.

I looked at the stone diagrams.

Nhal.

Containment.

Foundation.

Line.

Loops.

Load.

Everything.

“What model do we need?”

Ithar pointed at me.

“Yours first.”

“What?”

“Your loop.”

I laughed.

“No.”

His eyes narrowed.

“You built a small human version of forced return architecture inside your own channels.”

“That is not the same.”

“No.”

“Then why?”

“Because you can describe adaptation from inside it.”

There.

Useful.

Horrible.

“What did the body do first?”

“Resisted.”

“Then?”

“Compensated.”

“Then?”

“Preferred.”

His claw tapped the Line loop.

Fuck.

“What happened when you tried to reopen outward flow?”

“Slow.”

“Why?”

“Architecture had changed.”

“Broken?”

“No.”

“Changed.”

“Yes.”

There.

The Line.

Maybe.

Not broken.

Adapted.

“What if the Line is not failing?”

I asked.

Ithar’s eyes narrowed.

“What if it is adapting to a failure somewhere else?”

Silence.

The cave felt colder.

Not magic.

Probably.

Ithar looked at the diagram.

Then toward the west.

Far beyond stone.

Humans.

Stations.

Kelan.

Tracy.

Everything.

“That,” he said, “is a better question.”

Good.

I hated it.
