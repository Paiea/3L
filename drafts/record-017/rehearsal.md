## RECORD 017

## THE ABSENT

The first job Lorn did without me cost us a client.

I still paid him.

That surprised both of us.

The client was a landlord named Senn.

Three buildings on Rope Street.

Nothing elegant.

Narrow rooms.

Shared wash yards.

Too many people using too few stairs.

The middle building had a wall that knocked every evening when the heat came up.

Senn had already paid a plumber.

The plumber blamed the Line.

District service blamed the building.

That left the traditional final authority.

Somebody willing to take six silver.

Me.

I scheduled the inspection for sixth bell on a Tuesday.

Then my father fell in the mill yard.

Not far.

That detail mattered to him.

He repeated it three times while I helped him sit up.

“Wasn't far.”

“You hit your head.”

“Barely.”

“You are bleeding.”

“Head bleeds.”

“Yes. When you hit it.”

My mother stood over both of us.

“Take him to the healer.”

My father looked betrayed.

“I am sitting here.”

“That is not medical evidence,” she said.

Nessa had arrived carrying bread.

She looked at me.

Then at my father.

Then back at me.

I hated when evidence took her side before she even spoke.

“I have a contract.”

My father said, “You promised today.”

Not about the healer.

About Senn's building.

He had heard me mention it over dinner.

“I can move it.”

My mother said, “Or somebody else can work.”

Rude in several directions.

Lorn was already supposed to meet me at Rope Street.

Second pair of hands.

I sent a runner.

WAIT FOR CLIENT.

START VISUAL ONLY.

DO NOT OPEN WALL.

I added the last line because Lorn enjoyed opening things.

The runner left.

My father tried to stand.

He swayed.

My mother looked at me.

“He is fine,” my father said.

We took him to the healer.

Concussion.

Not severe.

Severe enough that the healer told him not to work for a week.

My father laughed.

The healer did not.

I liked her.

By the time we finished, seventh bell had passed.

I expected Lorn to be waiting on Rope Street with Senn angry beside him.

Senn was there.

Angry.

Lorn was inside.

I could hear hammering from the pavement.

I stopped walking.

Senn saw me.

“There he is.”

That tone rarely introduces praise.

“What happened?”

“Your man shut my heat off.”

I looked at the building.

Branch indicator down.

All three buildings shared the same house feed.

“Why?”

“You tell me.”

“I just got here.”

“Exactly.”

Fair.

Lorn came out carrying a strip of plaster.

I looked at it.

Then at him.

“I said do not open the wall.”

“You said visual only.”

“Yes.”

“I visually found a crack.”

“Behind plaster.”

“It became visible after I removed the plaster.”

Senn pointed at him.

“This is what I have been dealing with.”

I rubbed my forehead.

“Why is the feed off?”

Lorn stopped smiling.

“Come inside.”

That changed things.

Second floor.

Rear hall.

The knocking wall.

Lorn had removed a square of plaster about the size of a serving tray.

Behind it was an old copper return sleeve.

House-side.

Legal for us to inspect.

The sleeve had rubbed against a brick edge long enough to polish a bright line into the metal.

Every time the heat came up, expansion pushed it sideways.

Knock.

Then back.

Knock.

Simple.

Except the bright line had become a groove.

Deep one.

I reached toward it.

Lorn said, “Don't.”

I looked at him.

He pointed below the groove.

A bead of moisture sat there.

Not water.

Condensate around a pressure leak.

Tiny.

The kind of tiny that becomes larger if everybody agrees to wait.

I took my hand away.

“You shut the building feed.”

“Yes.”

“Immediately?”

“Yes.”

“Before Senn agreed?”

“Yes.”

Senn said, “There. Tell him.”

I looked at Senn.

“He was right.”

Senn stared at me.

“You work for me.”

“Not exactly.”

“I hired you.”

“You hired us to inspect a pressure fault.”

“And he shut off three buildings.”

“Because you have a leaking pressure sleeve inside a wall.”

“It has probably been leaking for years.”

“No.”

“How do you know?”

“The wall would be worse.”

“Probably.”

“Yes.”

Senn folded his arms.

“How long?”

“Repair?”

“Heat.”

I looked at Lorn.

He answered.

“Tomorrow if the sleeve is standard.”

Senn looked back at me.

“Tonight?”

“No.”

“People are paying rent.”

“I know.”

“Children live here.”

“I know.”

“It is cold.”

“I know.”

“Then turn it back on until morning.”

There are requests that sound more reasonable the less you understand the mechanism.

Senn's was one.

I crouched beside the sleeve.

The groove was worse than I wanted.

I would have shut it too.

Maybe after another measurement.

Maybe after checking whether only the middle building could be isolated.

That was the uncomfortable part.

I was not sure I would have done it as quickly as Lorn.

I had been thinking client.

Schedule.

Scope.

He had been standing beside a leaking sleeve.

“I am not turning it back on,” I said.

Senn's face closed.

“Then I am not paying you.”

“Okay.”

Lorn looked at me.

Senn pointed at the open wall.

“You damaged my property.”

“We'll patch the plaster after repair.”

“Not if you are off the job.”

“That is your choice.”

“It is.”

He walked out.

The front door slammed hard enough to shake dust from the hall trim.

Lorn waited.

Then said, “Six silver.”

“Yes.”

“Gone.”

“Yes.”

“You are terrible at business.”

“I have heard.”

He looked at the sleeve.

“I should have waited.”

“No.”

“You told me visual only.”

“I told you do not open the wall.”

“I opened the wall.”

“Yes.”

“Client is gone.”

“Yes.”

“So I fucked it.”

“No.”

Lorn looked at me.

The answer was harder than it should have been.

“You found a condition that made the existing scope unsafe.”

“By breaking scope.”

“Yes.”

“That sounds convenient.”

“It is.”

He almost smiled.

I shook my head.

“You should have sent for me before opening.”

“You were with your father.”

“You could have waited.”

“The knock was active.”

“Yes.”

“I could hear metal contact.”

“Yes.”

“I thought waiting was stupid.”

I looked at the groove.

It probably was.

“Next time,” I said, “send a runner before opening.”

“Next time, write that.”

I looked at him.

“What?”

“Your work sheet said visual only. It did not say what I can do if visual finds hazard.”

I opened my mouth.

Closed it.

He was right.

Again.

I disliked this arrangement.

“What would you write?”

Lorn shrugged.

“Stop unsafe condition. Notify client. Notify you. Open only enough to verify if delay increases risk.”

“That is a lot for a work sheet.”

“So write smaller.”

I stared at him.

“Have you been hiding literacy too?”

“Fuck you.”

Better.

Senn hired another fitter that afternoon.

The fitter kept the feed off and replaced the sleeve the next morning.

Three buildings spent one cold night with blankets and borrowed coal heaters from district stores.

Nobody died.

Nobody was happy.

Senn sent me a written complaint anyway.

Not to the Guild.

To me.

Almost worse.

UNAUTHORIZED DAMAGE.

UNAUTHORIZED SERVICE INTERRUPTION.

FAILURE TO COMPLETE CONTRACTED INSPECTION.

He was not entirely wrong.

That mattered.

Lorn read the complaint at our table.

Nessa read it after him.

Then she looked at me.

“Well?”

“What?”

“Are you paying Lorn?”

Lorn said, “I told him not to.”

Nessa ignored him.

I had already decided.

“Yes.”

Lorn frowned.

“For what?”

“The job.”

“We did not get paid.”

“You worked.”

“I cost the contract.”

“You found the leak.”

“I opened the wall.”

“You found the leak.”

Lorn looked at Nessa.

She shrugged.

“He has become strange about accounting.”

“I have always been strange about accounting.”

“No. You used to be bad. Now you are bad with principles.”

That sounded worse.

I paid Lorn two silver from PRIVATE WORK.

The ledger went negative for the month.

I hated the red mark.

Nessa drew a box around it.

“Why?”

“So you remember.”

“I remember losses.”

“Do you?”

She pointed at Lorn.

“What did you buy for two silver?”

I knew what she meant.

Not labor.

Judgment.

The ability for a job carrying my name to become safer while I was somewhere else.

Expensive.

Also cheap.

The next week I changed the private work sheet.

STOP AUTHORITY.

Any worker on the job could isolate service for an observed immediate hazard.

OPEN AUTHORITY.

Limited opening allowed if delay increased risk, with client notice where practical.

CALL AUTHORITY.

If I was not present, Lorn could request district assistance under the contract number.

Lorn read it.

“You made me paperwork.”

“Yes.”

“Can I quit?”

“No.”

“That is probably illegal.”

“Then file a complaint.”

“I know where you live.”

“So does Senn.”

That complaint reached Guild Hall two weeks later.

Of course it did.

Senn had reconsidered.

Or somebody told him formal complaints could recover repair costs.

The Guild notice required testimony.

PRIVATE WORK REVIEW.

CLIENT DISPUTE.

WORKER CONDUCT.

Lorn read it over my shoulder.

“You getting fined?”

“We.”

His face changed.

“No.”

“Yes.”

“You signed the contract.”

“You opened the wall.”

“You told me I was right.”

“You were.”

“Then why am I going?”

“Because being right apparently does not eliminate procedure.”

He looked at the notice.

Then at me.

“You going to tell them I did what you said?”

“No.”

“Good.”

The answer came too fast.

Lorn heard it.

So did I.

There had been a time I would have protected someone by taking their decision away from them.

My contract.

My worker.

My responsibility.

All true.

Incomplete.

Lorn made the call.

If I spoke over him, trust became ownership again.

“I'll tell them what I wrote,” I said.

“And I tell them what I did.”

“Yes.”

“What if they decide I fucked it?”

“Then we learn something expensive.”

“That is a terrible speech.”

“I was never good at speeches.”

From the wash basin, Nessa said, “True.”

I threw a pencil at her.

Missed.

We were losing fewer pencils by then.

Practice.

The hearing was set for Monday.

I spent the weekend wanting to prepare Lorn.

Questions.

Answers.

Order of events.

Words to avoid.

He refused.

“I know what happened.”

“I know.”

“Then stop trying to put your mouth on it.”

Disgusting sentence.

Effective.

I stopped.

Mostly.

The night before the hearing, my father asked how business was going.

We were at his house.

He had returned to the mill after the concussion.

Fewer hours.

My mother had won that argument by hiding one of his boots.

“Lost money,” I said.

“How?”

“Paid someone for a job the client refused to pay.”

My father nodded.

“Good.”

I looked at him.

“What?”

“Means you had someone worth paying.”

“That is not how profit works.”

“Never said profit.”

He went back to sharpening a kitchen knife badly.

I took it from him.

He let me.

That worried me more than the hearing.

I sharpened it properly.

He watched my hands.

“You always do too much yourself,” he said.

“I am literally doing your knife.”

“Yes.”

I stopped.

He smiled.

Old bastard.

The next morning, Lorn and I went to Guild Hall together.

Neither of us talked much.

He bounced one knee while we waited outside the hearing room.

I told him to stop.

He switched knees.

Then the door opened.

Bad chairs waited inside.
