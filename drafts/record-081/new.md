## RECORD 081

## THE WOUND

We did not all go below.

That would have been stupid.

Stories like to move the important people together because it is easier to describe.

Expeditions split because someone has to keep the important people from dying while they investigate the thing trying to kill them.

The healer stayed with Nhal.

Two technical staff stayed with the chamber instruments.

One survey assistant stayed on the upper tension cord.

Mael took Pava, the survey lead, Arcrutus, me, and one structural specialist whose name I remembered for three days and then apparently discarded forever.

Sorry.

He had excellent handwriting.

That survived.

The route below was not obvious until the floor moved again.

A hairline seam opened twenty feet behind Nhal's tail anchor.

Not enough for a person.

Enough for cold air.

The survey lead found an older access line buried under black mineral growth.

She scraped at it with a tool.

Mael said, "Stop."

She stopped.

Arcrutus said, "You people have made stopping very fashionable."

Nobody rewarded him.

The seam ran along the floor, then disappeared under one of the six pillars.

Pava checked pressure.

"Different from chamber load."

"How?" Mael asked.

"Deeper. Slower. Larger."

Arcrutus said, "Those are not numbers."

Pava looked at him.

"Would you like me to write very large on the slate?"

I liked her too.

We found the access opening behind the sixth pillar.

Human-sized only because someone had widened it later.

That became obvious once we were inside.

The first twenty feet had cut steps.

Then the steps ended against older stone that had never cared about feet.

We rigged rope.

Two independent anchors.

Not the expedition pressure kind.

Ordinary fall protection.

Important distinction when everything magical is trying to become metaphor.

Mael descended first.

Again.

Pava second.

Survey.

Structural specialist.

Me.

Arcrutus last this time because Mael wanted him between us and anything that came from above.

Arcrutus objected.

Mael ignored him.

Leadership is often deciding which S-class person's feelings are cheaper than the alternative.

The shaft dropped sixty feet.

Then turned sideways.

The chamber above had been warm.

Below it, the air became cold enough that my fingers hurt through my gloves.

Not weather cold.

Deep stone cold.

The walls changed too.

No blue paths.

At first.

Just black material and pale mineral lines.

The black looked older here.

Less polished.

More like something poured into cracks and allowed to become whatever shape the crack demanded.

The survey lead touched nothing.

Good woman.

She held a lamp low.

"Layering."

The structural specialist crouched.

He scraped loose dust beside one black seam without touching the seam itself.

"At least three phases."

Mael said, "How sure?"

"Different aggregate. Different cure texture. Different mineral inclusion."

Arcrutus said, "Old, older, oldest?"

The man thought.

"Crude, but yes."

"Excellent. I am a surveyor now."

The survey lead said, "You are not."

We continued.

The first blue line appeared thirty yards in.

Thin.

Barely visible.

It ran through the wall instead of along it.

Then another.

Then five.

All of them angled toward the chamber above.

Pava checked one with passive instruments.

"Tail."

I looked at her.

"You sure?"

"Same pressure signature."

Survey checked.

"Same timing too."

We followed them.

The passage narrowed.

Arcrutus had to turn sideways once.

He complained that ancient builders lacked respect for shoulders.

The passage opened twenty feet later.

Nobody joked after that.

The room below Nhal was not a room.

It was a break.

A fracture maybe thirty feet across split something enormous under the chamber.

Not a wall.

Not a beam.

The scale made those words silly.

Two black masses faced each other across a gap wide enough for me to fall through.

Pale mineral deposits covered the edges.

Blue relations crossed the gap without visible material beneath them.

Some went dark halfway.

Some bent upward.

Most converged on one thing.

Nhal's tail.

The anchoring structure did not end at the chamber floor.

It passed through it.

Down another forty feet.

I had thought the hard ridged end was embedded a little way into the black plane.

Wrong.

The tail anchor entered the fracture like a driven spike.

Except nobody had driven it from outside.

The geometry was backwards.

The ridges had opened inside the break.

Natural structures along the tail had seated against both sides of the fractured mass.

Black material had grown around them afterward.

Not grown naturally.

Added.

Layer after layer.

The structural specialist walked closer.

Mael caught the back of his coat.

"Distance."

"I need to see the interface."

"See from here first."

He did.

We all did.

The oldest black material ended before the tail.

The next layer wrapped around it.

The newest layers climbed upward toward the chamber restraints.

The survey lead said, very softly, "Oh."

Pava looked at the traces.

"She is bridging the fracture."

Mael said, "Evidence."

Pava pointed.

"Deep compression crosses through the tail structure. Lateral motion too. The two sides are not mechanically continuous anywhere else in this section."

Survey added, "And the blue relationships across the gap all use the tail interface or route around through later material."

Mael looked at me.

I hated when that happened.

I lowered my aura.

Slowly.

The fracture appeared in a way my eyes could not show.

Not as space.

As disagreement.

One side wanted to move down and inward.

The other wanted to rotate away.

The structure above required them not to.

Nhal's tail did not merely hold both sides.

It maintained the relation that made the separation behave like continuity.

I raised my aura.

"She is not plugging the crack."

Arcrutus said, "Looks like plugging."

"Force, yes. More than that."

Pava looked at me.

"Say it badly."

"The two sides are broken apart. Her tail is making them behave like they still belong to the same thing."

Nobody spoke.

That sentence was worse than plugging.

The structural specialist walked the safe edge of the fracture.

"This material around her anchor was added after the break."

Survey said, "How sure?"

"Very."

"Why?"

He pointed with the lamp.

"See the mineral skin on the old fracture face? It continues behind the first encasement layer. The fracture weathered before they packed around the tail."

Mael said, "Meaning the break predates the reinforcement."

"Yes."

Arcrutus looked up at the tail disappearing through the ceiling.

"And the tail predates the reinforcement."

The specialist hesitated.

"It appears so."

Mael said, "Appearances are enough to ask."

We used the tension cord.

One pull from below meant question ready.

The assistant above relayed it to the healer.

The healer asked Nhal at the rods.

Left for yes.

Right for no.

Both for not like that.

Primitive.

Worked.

Mael chose the first question.

"Was this fracture already here when you placed your tail into it?"

The cord stayed still.

Then one pull came down.

Yes.

Nobody reacted much.

We had expected it.

Expected is different from known.

The survey lead asked the second.

"Did you place it here to stop the two sides from separating further?"

One pull.

Yes.

Arcrutus closed his eyes.

Just for a second.

Then opened them.

Pava looked at the encasement layers.

"Were the first structures around your tail added with your agreement?"

The cord pulled twice.

No.

I looked at her.

"Careful. First structures around the tail could mean reinforcement she did not choose even if useful."

Pava nodded.

Good.

No defensiveness.

She changed the question.

"After you anchored here, did humans add structures intended to keep you from removing your tail?"

One pull.

Yes.

Arcrutus turned away from us.

Walked three steps.

Stopped at the safe line.

His hands opened and closed.

Mael let him have that.

The structural specialist was staring upward.

"This is not a containment chamber built to hold a creature."

Survey said, "Not originally."

He nodded.

"It is a repair site."

That landed harder.

Not because prisoner was better.

Because repair had chronology.

Something broke.

Nhal intervened.

It worked.

Then people built around the intervention.

We did not know why.

We did not know how quickly.

We did not know whether the first builders intended temporary support and failed to replace it, or immediately saw a living solution and decided they preferred it.

History is often where motive goes to become convenient.

We had material.

Material was enough.

Pava crouched near the fracture trace.

"The failed path from above."

She pointed into the gap.

One blue relation ended against nothing twenty feet below us.

The far end had sheared away from a black support spur.

Fresh pale dust coated the edge.

"There."

The survey lead moved her lamp.

"That's new."

"Yes."

Mael said, "How much did it carry before failure?"

Pava checked the slate.

"Not much force."

I felt the next sentence coming.

I hated it already.

"Condition?" I asked.

Pava looked at me.

"Probably."

We tested from distance.

No direct contact.

A small pulse through a surviving adjacent relation.

The broken spur did not respond.

The fracture did.

Both sides shifted less than a fingernail.

Nhal's tail took the difference immediately.

Forty feet of natural anchor flexed.

Not visibly at first.

Then the ridges nearest us compressed into the black encasement.

Pale dust fell.

Above, the tension cord snapped once.

Change.

Pava shouted, "Stop."

Survey stopped the pulse.

The movement settled.

I looked at the tail.

A fresh line had appeared where dark natural material met ancient black reinforcement.

Not a crack.

Wear.

The healer relayed another change from above.

Nhal's breathing had increased.

Mael said, "No more tests."

Nobody argued.

We had found the active failure.

Not one path.

The entire fracture was still trying to become more fractured.

The old system had spent branches around it for centuries.

When branches failed, Nhal compensated.

When compensation changed, restraints and later control structures redistributed again.

Every generation of repair had inherited the previous generation's assumption that Nhal would remain where she was.

Pava said, "We cannot free the tail until this has continuity without her."

Arcrutus turned back.

"Can we build it?"

Survey looked at the structural specialist.

He looked at Pava.

Pava looked at me.

I considered pretending not to notice.

Mael said, "Greg."

Bastard.

I looked across the fracture.

"Temporarily?"

"Yes."

"Probably."

Arcrutus swore.

I held up a hand.

"Temporarily is not useless."

Pava said, "How temporary?"

"With one relationship like this? Seconds alone. Longer with help."

"Minutes?"

"Maybe."

Mael said, "Enough to install a physical bridge?"

The structural specialist answered.

"Not one we brought."

Of course.

Arcrutus said, "Use me as the bridge."

The structural specialist stared at him.

"You are not shaped correctly."

"I can become very persuasive."

"Still wrong shape."

I almost laughed.

Mael did not.

"We have no fabrication capacity for a permanent replacement at this scale."

Survey said, "We may not need permanent before extraction."

Everyone looked at her.

She pointed upward.

"We need enough continuity to unload the tail safely. Once Nhal is free, she may be able to maintain the relation externally instead of through her own body."

Arcrutus said, "You want her to keep carrying it after we free her."

Survey did not back away from the sentence.

"If she chooses to, temporarily."

That word mattered.

Pava said, "Then we replace captivity with distance."

I looked across the gap.

Maybe.

Support is often moving a cost somewhere better, not making it disappear.

The difference here was that the person paying it finally got a vote.

Mael looked up at the tail.

"Ask her."

We relayed the question carefully.

Not would you keep holding the system.

Too broad.

Not will you help us.

Meaningless.

The actual question took three attempts.

Finally:

"If we create enough temporary continuity to release your tail from the fracture, can you maintain this relation from outside the restraint without placing your tail back into the break?"

We waited.

One pull came down.

Yes.

Arcrutus sat on the floor.

Just sat.

Pava looked at him.

"You okay?"

"No."

Good sentence.

Mael looked at the fracture.

"Then we have an extraction condition."

The structural specialist shook his head.

"We have one extraction condition."

Mael nodded.

"Correct."

Pava looked at the dead support spur.

"And a system degrading while we prepare."

"Correct."

I stared at Nhal's tail.

The natural anchor was scarred where later black material had been packed around it.

Some scars were old enough to have mineral deposits inside them.

Centuries of microscopic movement.

Centuries of correction.

Centuries of the structure getting to treat a living decision as permanent infrastructure.

The first connection had not been a restraint.

Nhal had put herself into the break.

Then somebody had built a prison around the fact that it worked.
