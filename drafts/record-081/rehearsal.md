## RECORD 081

## THE SCENERY

The north bridge was still standing.

That was the first good thing.

I hated it later.

Not the bridge.

The fact that my mind kept finding good things.

Bridge standing.

Road passable.

Wind pushing smoke east instead of south.

Water pressure still present in the lower mains.

Useful facts.

I crossed with my bag hitting my hip and somebody behind me shouting that the road was closed.

It was.

I continued.

The first people coming the other direction were walking.

Then running.

Then carrying other people.

A man had blood down one side of his face and no idea it was there.

A woman held a little girl who was old enough to walk.

The girl was awake.

Good.

There it was again.

Good.

I moved around them.

Closer to town, the road had split.

Not cracked.

Split.

The stone had lifted on one side and dropped on the other like something underneath had tried to turn over.

No ordinary fight did that.

The administrative house sat above town on the old hill.

The people in the song had come for someone connected to that house.

I remembered that now.

Of course I did.

Memory is generous after it becomes useless.

A Guild cordon had gone up near the market road.

Temporary posts.

Rope.

Three exhausted workers.

One Authority coat.

I went under the rope.

The Authority man caught my shoulder.

“Sir.”

I turned.

He let go.

Not because he recognized me.

Because of my face.

“Family,” I said.

He looked past me toward town.

“Where?”

I gave the street.

His expression changed.

Small.

Enough.

“I need to go.”

“They’re still clearing that section.”

“I need to go.”

“There are unstable structures.”

“I know.”

“Secondary contamination in the upper blocks.”

“I know.”

“Sir.”

I looked at his hand.

Still between me and the road.

He moved it.

Good decision.

I ran.

The market was gone.

Not completely.

That would have been easier to understand.

Half the stalls were still there.

One fruit awning had survived.

The cloth was red.

Everything around it was gray.

A man stood beside a broken fountain yelling the same name over and over.

I did not know if it belonged to a wife.

Child.

Brother.

Dog.

Did not matter.

Three buildings had folded inward.

Another had lost its front wall and kept the rooms.

I could see a bed on the second floor.

Made.

Nobody had slept in it yet.

A table remained set behind a missing wall.

Four bowls.

I looked away.

The song did not mention bowls.

I reached our street.

Stopped.

Wrong street.

No.

Correct street.

Wrong shape.

Our neighbor’s roof was in the road.

The little stone drainage channel our son liked to walk along had disappeared under brick.

A tree I had complained about pruning lay across two properties.

Our house was still there.

Enough of it.

That was worse.

The front room had opened.

The wall facing the street was gone.

I could see the table.

One chair tipped sideways.

One standing.

The pantry door hung open.

I remember thinking about onions.

Of course I did.

I dropped my work bag.

The sweet bread stayed inside.

I climbed over the front stones.

“Nessa?”

Too quiet.

“Nessa!”

Someone answered from behind me.

Not her.

A rescue worker.

“Don’t go upstairs.”

I turned.

“Where are they?”

He looked at me.

“Who?”

“My wife. Two children.”

He asked their descriptions.

I gave them too quickly.

Hair.

Height.

Clothes that morning.

No.

Three mornings ago.

I corrected myself.

Our daughter might have changed clothes.

Obviously.

Our son had the repaired left boot.

Blue stitching near the heel because I only had blue thread when I fixed it.

Nessa had been wearing the brown work coat when I left.

Maybe not today.

I did not know what she was wearing today.

That became suddenly unacceptable.

The rescue worker listened.

His face did not change.

Professional.

I hated him for that.

Then loved him for it.

“Some people were moved to the school hall,” he said.

“Alive?”

“Some.”

Wrong answer.

“Some?”

He pointed.

“Go there first.”

I started to move.

Then heard something inside the house.

A shift.

Stone against wood.

I stopped.

The rescue worker said, “Don’t.”

I went back in.

Not because I thought it was them.

Because once you hear a structure move, your body wants to know why.

Mine did, anyway.

The back room had partially collapsed.

The ceiling beam rested on what had been the kitchen partition.

Under it, a hand moved.

Not Nessa.

I knew before seeing the face.

Wrong hand.

Too large.

A man from two houses down.

I knew him enough to nod at.

Not enough to remember his first name immediately.

He was conscious.

Barely.

The beam had him pinned through the hips.

The rescue worker came in behind me swearing.

“Do you have lift?”

“Some.”

“Support?”

“Some.”

“Good enough?”

“No.”

He stared.

I put my hands on the beam.

“Probably.”

We worked.

This became important later because I hated him for being there.

The trapped man.

Not personally.

I hated that somebody else’s life could demand anything from me while I did not know where my family was.

Then hated myself for hating him.

Then stopped having enough attention for either.

The beam had taken load from the remaining wall.

Lift it wrong and the room followed.

So we did not lift it.

We supported the wall first.

Moved loose stone.

Built a temporary brace.

Shifted load six inches at a time.

The rescue worker understood enough to listen.

The trapped man screamed once.

Then apologized.

That nearly broke me.

“Don’t,” I said.

He thought I meant do not scream.

I meant do not make me care about you right now.

He survived.

Good.

There it was again.

Good.

We got him onto a door somebody had turned into a stretcher.

The rescue worker looked at me.

“School hall.”

I left.

I did not take my work bag.

I remembered that later too.

Four pieces of sweet bread sat in the wreckage until somebody moved them or rain got them or an animal found them.

I do not know which.

The school hall had become a place for lists.

Living.

Injured.

Missing.

Dead.

Human beings made into columns because columns are how you survive too much information.

I understood that.

I still hated it.

A clerk asked me for names.

I gave them.

He checked living first.

Good man.

Not there.

Injured.

Not there.

Missing.

Two of them appeared.

My son.

My daughter.

Nessa did not.

For approximately three seconds I experienced hope.

Then the clerk checked another sheet.

His finger stopped.

He looked at me.

There are expressions people learn in jobs they should not have to do.

His was very practiced.

“Your wife?”

“Yes.”

He asked me to come with him.

“No.”

He stopped.

“Check the children again.”

“I did.”

“Again.”

He did.

Living.

Injured.

Missing.

Same.

“Again.”

He looked at me.

Then checked again.

Good man.

Same.

Someone had written the children’s names onto the missing sheet because identification had not been complete when the first rescue teams moved bodies.

That was all.

Not hope.

Administrative lag.

We went to another room.

I will not tell you much about that room.

Not because I forgot.

Because I did not.

Nessa was there.

The twins were there.

My son’s left boot had blue thread at the heel.

My daughter did not have the blue cup.

I remember being grateful for that.

I do not know why.

Nessa’s hand was visible.

I knew it.

Hands become very specific after years.

The clerk asked if I could confirm.

I said yes.

He asked each name separately.

I answered each one.

He did not group the children together.

I remember that.

Good man.

My son was my son.

My daughter was my daughter.

Nessa was Nessa.

Three confirmations.

Three lines corrected on paper.

Then somebody asked whether there was anyone they could contact for me.

I laughed.

Not because it was funny.

The sound just happened.

I had people.

Parents.

Lena.

Nessa’s family.

Friends.

Work contacts.

Neighbors.

An entire life.

I had no idea which of them were alive.

“Not yet,” I said.

The clerk nodded.

He did not touch me.

Another good decision.

Outside, somebody was singing.

Quietly.

Not the whole song.

One line.

Then another person told him to shut the fuck up.

He did.

I stood in the doorway of the school hall and finally understood the line I had sung two nights earlier.

In First Life the verse had always sounded like culmination.

The avengers reached the house.

The debt was answered.

The guilty man finally faced what he had done.

There was fire.

There was collapse.

There was a line about the hill burning red.

Good line.

Very singable.

The town lived underneath the hill.

The song never said how many.

Why would it?

That was not what the song was about.

People like clean stories.

Wrong.

People like stories with a foreground.

The avengers had one.

Their grievance was real.

Their dead were real.

Their rage was real.

The man they came for had done what the song accused him of.

None of that made Nessa less dead.

None of it made my children more relevant to the verse.

We were scenery in somebody else’s revenge.

Then memory finally became useful.

Not kind.

Useful.

The song had a road in it.

Most versions called it the king’s road.

There had not been a king on that road for two hundred years.

The real road was an old quarry route northwest of town.

The lyric about three stones meant nothing to singers.

It meant something if you knew the district.

Three standing markers at the split.

One route toward the river.

One toward the abandoned lime works.

One toward the ridge.

In First Life I had heard the song hundreds of times.

I had never cared enough to ask where any of it actually was.

Now I knew.

I found the Authority man from the cordon.

“Hale Rusk.”

He looked at me.

“What?”

“Hale Rusk was here.”

His face gave me the answer before his mouth did.

A-class.

Named Ranker.

Famous before the song.

More famous after.

One of the people the story had turned into something larger and cleaner than a man.

“Sir, you need to stay here.”

“Which way did he go?”

“We don’t know.”

That was a lie.

Not a good one.

“Northwest.”

He said nothing.

“Quarry road.”

His eyes changed.

There.

I turned.

He caught my arm.

“Do not.”

I looked at his hand.

He let go again.

Learning.

“They have field teams moving.”

“How long?”

“What?”

“How long since he left?”

“Sir.”

“How long?”

He hesitated.

“Maybe three hours.”

Three hours.

Not gone.

Moving.

Probably tired.

Probably injured.

Almost certainly not expecting me.

The thought arrived complete.

I hated myself later for how normal it felt.

Not whether I would go.

How.

Water.

Food.

Shortest crossing.

Whether the north ferry still ran after dark.

Where the quarry road narrowed.

How much current magic I could actually execute after the run home.

What I could use instead.

The clerk from the school hall found me near the door.

“Sir?”

I looked at him.

He had been good to me.

That mattered.

“Someone should contact my family.”

His face softened.

“Of course.”

“My parents. Lena. Nessa’s family if they haven’t already.”

“We can.”

I nodded.

Then left.

He called after me.

I kept walking.

I had confirmed my wife and children dead less than twenty minutes earlier.

That sounds impossible when I say it now.

It was not.

Grief did not make me collapse.

Not yet.

It made the world very small.

Hale Rusk had done this.

Hale Rusk was three hours away.

Everything else could wait.

Ithar moved in the cave.

A slow flex of one foreclaw.

“You pursued him immediately.”

“Yes.”

“You had just identified your family.”

“Yes.”

“You were not remotely his equal.”

“No.”

“Did you know that?”

I looked at him.

“Of course.”

Ithar waited.

That irritated me.

“I wasn’t stupid.”

“No,” he said. “That is why I asked.”

Fair.

I looked at the stone between my hands.

“I knew I couldn’t fight an A-class Named Ranker.”

“And yet.”

“And yet.”

I had lived another life.

The body was wrong.

The channels were wrong.

The rank was wrong.

The knowledge was not.

Hale Rusk had three hours.

I had forty years.

So I went hunting.
