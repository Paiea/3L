## RECORD 041

## THE TRAVELER

Regional work taught me that every town believes its problem is unique.

Sometimes it is.

Usually the bakery is still cold.

My first contract outside the district after East Four began with onions.

Not metaphorically.

A cart full of them.

The tram took four hours north to Bellwater, which was long enough for me to finish the inspection notes I had promised Nessa I would not bring into our protected half-day and short enough that sleeping would have guaranteed a bad neck.

I chose reading.

My shoulder clicked once before the river crossing.

Rain somewhere ahead.

Useful damage.

Still annoying.

Bellwater's station runner met me at the tram circle with a two-wheeled cart and no sign bearing my name.

Good.

The cart already contained three sacks of onions, a box of lamp wicks, and a woman who said she had paid for the seat first.

The runner pointed at the back.

“You can sit by the onions.”

“I am district inspection.”

“Not our district.”

Fair.

I sat by the onions.

Forty minutes later I arrived at Bellwater smelling like soup.

The city sat where the river widened enough for warehouses and narrow enough for people to argue about bridges.

First thing I noticed was wood smoke.

Second was that the main station was smaller than I expected.

Third was that almost nobody seemed worried about household heat.

That was strange enough that I asked.

Bellwater's foreman was named Hedd Marr.

He had a beard with more gray than black and the tired patience of a man who had already answered every visitor's first question incorrectly for them.

“Most houses burn wood,” he said.

“Line doesn't heat residential?”

“Some. Not enough to matter first.”

“What matters first?”

Hedd pointed through the station window toward the river warehouses.

“Flour stops moving.”

That was better.

Not morally.

Informationally.

Bellwater used the Line for grain lifts, wash pumps, and the river icebreakers that kept loading channels open in winter. Same Line. Different dependency.

I had arrived carrying a home-district model without noticing.

Good start.

The fault itself was ordinary.

Return imbalance after a replacement valve.

New valve closed faster than the old one. Pressure rose briefly on the shared return, then settled. Nothing dramatic. No old bronze hidden behind a wall. No ancient mark waiting to become my personality for three weeks.

Hedd looked almost apologetic.

“You disappointed?”

“No.”

“You have a face.”

Apparently the problem had spread regionally before I did.

We reduced service to reproduce the surge.

The first grain lift stopped.

Then another.

Warehouse crews did not panic.

They swore, dragged out hand trucks, and started rerouting sacks by muscle.

I watched six men move flour manually while Hedd adjusted the station.

“Do they have a procedure for this?” I asked.

He looked at me.

“They have backs.”

“Those are not procedures.”

“They're older than procedures.”

Across the river yard, one of the icebreaker crews started shouting at us.

Hedd shouted back.

I could not hear the words over the grain lift chains.

“Problem?”

“They want pressure back.”

“Grain crew seems fine.”

“Grain can move slow. Ice freezes at the speed it likes.”

There it was.

Local priority.

Not on the regional load sheet.

The sheet marked both as INDUSTRIAL SERVICE.

Same box.

Different consequence.

I wrote that down.

Not the profound version.

Just:

ASK WHAT STOPS FIRST.

Hedd leaned over my shoulder.

“That your whole report?”

“Beginning.”

“Shame.”

We fixed the return balance before evening.

No one thanked me because the grain lifts had only been stopped forty-three minutes and the icebreaker foreman believed I had personally delayed winter.

Correct response to maintenance.

At the inn I emptied onion skins out of my tool roll.

One had worked under the buckle somehow.

I sent Nessa a slate.

BELLWATER DOES NOT CARE ABOUT HEAT FIRST.

Her reply came after I had eaten.

PEOPLE THERE HAVE INVENTED FIRE?

WOOD.

REVOLUTIONARY.

I considered telling her about the grain lifts.

Instead I wrote:

I SMELL LIKE ONIONS.

She replied:

THAT PART IS NOT REGIONAL.

Marriage remained supportive.

Except we were not married yet.

That sentence still mattered then.

The next town was South Fen.

Flat.

Wet.

Built around dye houses and cloth finishing.

Colored runoff stained the gutters despite regulations everyone swore they followed.

The station smelled like wet wool, hot metal, and whatever purple was made from before it became purple.

Nessa would have hated it.

I missed her enough to think that three times.

South Fen complained about a pressure dip at sixth bell.

I assumed shift start.

Reasonable.

Wrong.

The operator on duty corrected me before I finished asking.

“Rinse.”

“What?”

“Sixth bell is rinse discharge.”

Every dye house emptied hot process water into recovery tanks at nearly the same time because a trade schedule written fifty years earlier had standardized lunch very efficiently and everything around lunch very badly.

The recovery pumps pulled together.

The Line sagged.

Engineers had been adjusting station pressure for years.

No machine was broken.

The town needed staggered rinse times.

That was offensive to everyone because scheduling problems do not justify new equipment budgets.

A dye-house owner told me his workers had rinsed at sixth bell since his grandfather's time.

I asked whether his grandfather had shared a recovery loop with eight other dye houses.

He said no.

“Then your grandfather sounds innocent.”

He did not laugh.

We staggered three houses first as a test.

Pressure dip shrank.

Staggered the rest over forty minutes.

Nearly disappeared.

No new regulator.

No old Line.

No excavation.

A scheduling problem wearing machinery clothes.

I sent Nessa another slate from the inn.

YOU WOULD HATE THIS PLACE.

WHY.

EVERYONE SCHEDULES BADLY.

A pause.

Then:

I WOULD BE EMPLOYED.

Fair.

By the third trip, packing became less ceremonial.

Two shirts.

Tool roll.

Inspection hammer.

Shoulder wrap if the weather looked dishonest.

Ledger pages.

Separate coin purse for reimbursable expenses because Nessa had strong opinions about soup.

The first time I submitted a travel ledger, I had written FOOD: TWO SILVER.

She returned it.

WHAT FOOD.

“Food.”

That was apparently not accounting.

Now I kept receipts when merchants gave them and wrote descriptions when they did not.

Hillcross cost me three pages because the innkeeper refused paper on moral grounds I never understood.

Hillcross had no river.

Cold wind.

Steep streets designed by someone who hated knees.

The Line drove public pumps because water had to climb from lower wells.

Heating existed.

Secondary.

When pressure dropped, people still had stoves.

They did not have water upstairs.

A station operator named Noll said, “You can tell visitors because they complain about cold first.”

I had complained about cold first.

He enjoyed this for the entire week.

The fault took long enough that the innkeeper learned how I took tea and continued making it wrong anyway.

Current map showed one pump feed.

Physical line split into two under a street rebuilt after a fire.

Not ancient infrastructure.

Only forty-year-old bad records.

Important distinction.

We found the second branch because a retired plumber remembered his father cursing it.

His father had been dead eleven years.

The curse survived longer than the drawing.

Generational engineering.

I wrote another note:

ASK RETIRED TRADES BEFORE DIGGING.

Noll read it.

“You need paper to know that?”

“I travel.”

“That your excuse?”

“Currently.”

He nodded like this explained something unfortunate about me.

When I came home from Hillcross, Nessa was asleep at the table with a transfer sheet under one arm.

I stood in the doorway longer than necessary.

Travel made home visible differently.

Not prettier.

The floor still sloped.

The third stair still complained.

The table still leaned toward the window because Nessa continued defending mechanical failure as personality.

But I had spent four nights in an inn where the blanket smelled faintly of somebody else's soap.

Our bad table looked excellent.

I took the transfer sheet out from under her arm.

She woke.

“Home?”

“Apparently.”

“What time?”

“Late.”

“That is not a time.”

We had not built that rule yet.

We were already rehearsing it.

Cask came after.

Actual name.

I thought someone was joking.

They were not.

Brewery town.

The Line powered refrigeration and cooperage steam. Every public meeting smelled like beer. This improved attendance and worsened minutes.

I did not solve anything there.

Tova did.

That mattered.

We were hired together for a foundation survey after a recurring vibration appeared near a legacy wall.

I spent half a day convinced the vibration came from an old buried support.

The frequency changed with load.

The wall carried it beautifully.

I had theories.

Tova listened to three.

Then walked outside.

Five minutes later she returned.

“Come here.”

I followed her into the alley.

A brewery compressor sat against the shared wall on four bolts and an expression of innocence.

It kicked on.

The wall vibrated.

Tova looked at me.

“Ancient?”

“Fuck you.”

The town bought us dinner anyway.

That may have been the first place outside home where someone refused my money because of work.

Not one of the three cities from later stories.

Do not count it.

One dinner is not a civic identity.

The smallest place taught me the lesson I deserved most.

Seven hundred people.

One station room attached to the mill.

No dedicated foreman.

The mill mechanic kept the Line equipment because nobody else wanted the paperwork.

His name was Corren.

One good eye.

No patience for badges.

“What are you ranked?” he asked before saying hello.

“C.”

“Useful?”

“Sometimes.”

“Good.”

Their relief valve opened during storms.

Only storms.

I wanted atmospheric pressure.

Corren said roof runoff hit the vent hood.

I laughed.

He did not.

Then it rained.

Water came off the mill roof in one hard sheet, found the poorly placed vent hood, and filled it enough to raise backpressure.

The relief opened inside thirty seconds.

Corren looked at me with his one good eye.

I looked at the water.

“Fine.”

“Atmosphere?”

“Technically weather.”

He charged me for lunch because I had been condescending.

Fair.

We extended the vent.

Problem gone.

Regional work did not make me cosmopolitan.

I still preferred my own bed.

I learned which tram stations had edible food.

Which inns washed blankets.

Which towns overcharged visitors.

Which Guild halls had clerks willing to stamp private work after fourth bell.

I learned Bellwater measured urgency in grain and ice.

South Fen measured it in rinse schedules.

Hillcross measured it in whether water reached the upstairs tap.

Cask measured it in beer, which was at least honest.

Corren's village measured it in whether rain fell into the wrong piece of metal.

Same Line.

Different lives arranged around it.

First Life, travel had meant movement toward danger or advancement.

New contract.

New province.

Next climb.

I carried what I needed because there was rarely a reason to imagine the room after me.

Second Life acquired return tickets.

That changed the geometry.

I went somewhere because work was there.

Then came home because home was somewhere else.

The regional map above our desk grew marks.

Not conquest.

Clients.

Contacts.

A mechanic in Hillcross.

A foreman in Bellwater.

A dye scheduler who eventually sent Nessa three pages complaining about everybody else's schedules.

Corren's village with a note saying CHECK ROOF FIRST.

Tova drew a small barrel beside Cask.

I objected.

She added another.

The map widened.

The address above Mina's bakery stayed the address.

Sometimes I returned after Nessa had already left for work.

Sometimes she came home after I had gone again.

Sometimes both bags sat by the door and neither of us did.

That problem would become its own chapter later.

For now, there was still a reliable thing beneath all the travel.

A ticket with two directions printed on it.

Out.

Back.

First-life Greg had known how to leave.

Life Two was teaching me that travel could be defined by the return.
