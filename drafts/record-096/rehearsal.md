## RECORD 096

## THE UNMET

I found Tracy in the same building.

Different counter.

That hurt more than it should have.

Not because anything was wrong.

Because enough was right.

Same Guild branch.

Same downstairs medical floor.

Same smell of alcohol, dried herbs, waxed cloth, and sealed cabinets.

Different paint.

Different shelving.

Different clerk at the first desk.

Twelve years earlier, approximately.

That mattered.

I had known Tracy after Vey.

After S-class.

After enough time for her to become herself in the particular way I remembered.

This Tracy had not lived those years yet.

Neither had I.

Not in this life.

I asked for her anyway.

The desk clerk looked at me.

“Tracy?”

“Yes.”

“Which one?”

That was a problem I had not anticipated.

“Medical maintenance.”

“We have two.”

“Poison.”

He thought.

“Oh.”

Then pointed.

“Back stock.”

I went.

My peg made the stairs annoying.

Not impossible.

Annoying.

That was the body then.

Still learning.

Still compensating.

Still getting angry at architecture.

The back stock room had three people.

One older man counting sealed kits.

One woman labeling drawers.

And Tracy.

Younger.

Of course.

That should not have surprised me.

It did.

Hair shorter.

No B-class mark.

No A-class specialty line.

No authority in the room.

Just a healer credential.

D-class.

Barely, judging by the fresh notation.

She was arguing with a shelf.

Not metaphorically.

“This is wrong.”

The older man did not look up.

“It’s on the list.”

“The list is wrong.”

“Then write a correction.”

“I did.”

“Then leave it.”

“It’s still wrong.”

There.

Same person.

Earlier.

I stood in the doorway too long.

She noticed.

“Can I help you?”

I almost said yes in the old way.

Obviously.

Instead I said, “Probably.”

She frowned.

Good.

Still her.

“What do you need?”

I had rehearsed this badly.

Poison consultation.

Employment.

Mentorship.

Maintenance.

All true.

None explained why seeing her had made my throat close.

“I’m looking for a poison specialist.”

“You found inventory.”

“Are you Tracy?”

“Yes.”

“Then I found both.”

She looked at the peg.

Then back at me.

Not rudely.

Professionally.

“Exposure?”

“No.”

“Recent?”

“No.”

“Medication?”

“No.”

“Then what poison?”

“None yet.”

She stared.

“Excellent use of my time.”

There.

I almost smiled.

That was my first mistake.

Not smiling.

Recognizing the sentence.

She saw something in my face she did not understand.

Her posture changed.

Small.

Guarded.

Right.

Of course.

I knew her.

She did not know me.

I had arrived already carrying ten years of reactions.

To her, I was a stranger with one leg staring too hard.

“Sorry,” I said.

“For?”

Good question.

I did not answer.

She waited.

Then looked toward the older man.

Not asking for rescue.

Checking distance.

That hurt too.

I stepped back.

“Professional question.”

“Ask.”

“Known toxic environment. Repeated exposure. Parties currently rely on late antidote response. What would you change?”

She blinked.

“That is not a question.”

“It is.”

“No. That is a planning meeting.”

Fair.

“What would you look at first?”

“Exposure route.”

Immediate.

Good.

“Then?”

“Dose pattern.”

“Then?”

“Available prophylaxis.”

There.

I knew the next sentence before she said it.

“Then whether anybody is actually following it.”

Not the same sentence.

Close enough.

I smiled again.

She stopped.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“No.”

I had forgotten that too.

She hated unexplained reactions when they involved her.

“What?”

“You think ahead.”

She looked unimpressed.

“That is the job.”

Not yet.

Not fully.

Already.

I wanted to tell her everything.

The marsh.

The half.

The yard.

The washhouse.

Marrin’s.

Arcrutus calling her Clerk.

The fish dinner after A-class.

The day she became Number Two without anybody deciding it.

I wanted to give her the proof of what she would become.

That was dangerous.

I knew it.

Did it anyway.

“You’re good at this.”

Her face closed slightly.

“You asked three questions.”

“I know.”

“No, you don’t.”

I stopped.

There it was.

Not the words.

The boundary.

I had crossed it.

She looked at me more directly.

“Do I know you?”

“No.”

“Have we met?”

“No.”

“Then stop looking at me like I forgot you.”

Fuck.

I looked away.

The older man in the room kept counting.

Professional saint.

I said, “You didn’t.”

“What?”

“Forget me.”

She waited.

“I knew someone like you.”

Wrong.

Her expression hardened.

“Someone like me.”

“No.”

“Better.”

“I mean you.”

Silence.

That was worse.

I rubbed my face.

“I am doing this badly.”

“Yes.”

Progress.

“Can I start over?”

“You can leave and come back.”

Same person.

I laughed.

Could not help it.

She did not.

I left.

Actually left.

Walked upstairs.

Bought food.

Almost bought lamb.

Stopped with my hand halfway toward the order.

No.

That was hers from another life.

Maybe she hated lamb now.

Maybe she had not learned to like it.

Maybe she only liked it from Marrin’s because Mara seasoned it differently.

Maybe this Marrin’s did not exist yet in the same way.

People were not save files.

I knew that intellectually.

My body did not.

My body expected patterns.

I bought my own lunch.

Ate it.

Waited an hour.

Went back.

Tracy looked up.

“You actually came back.”

“You told me to.”

“I was hoping you would understand the second half.”

“There was no second half.”

“Implied.”

“I’m bad at implication.”

“I noticed.”

Better.

I stayed farther from the counter.

That helped.

“My name is Greg.”

“I know.”

I froze.

She pointed at the visitor slip clipped to my coat.

Of course.

“Right.”

“You are having a difficult day.”

“Yes.”

That softened her slightly.

Not trust.

Room.

“What do you want, Greg?”

I thought carefully.

“Help learning how to live in this body.”

Her eyes moved to the peg.

Then the shoulder.

Then my stance.

Healer now.

Good.

“You need rehabilitation.”

“I have rehabilitation.”

“Then use it.”

“I do.”

“Poorly?”

“Probably.”

She almost smiled.

There.

Not history.

Current.

Earned.

“I need someone who looks at problems before they become expensive.”

That got her attention.

Not because she recognized herself.

Because the sentence interested her.

“What kind of problems?”

“Body. Work. Equipment. Eventually people.”

“That is too broad.”

“I know.”

“Then narrow it.”

I almost laughed again.

Did not.

Progress.

“Body first.”

“Good.”

“Channels.”

“Who is treating them?”

I told her.

She knew the clinic.

Good clinic.

Better healers than her.

She said so.

“I don’t need you to replace them.”

“Then why me?”

Because you taught me how to survive myself.

Could not say that.

Not yet.

“Because they treat damage.”

She waited.

“I want someone interested in the part before damage.”

That landed.

Tracy looked at me differently.

Not personally.

Professionally.

Better.

“Prevention.”

“Yes.”

“Maintenance.”

“Yes.”

“Compliance.”

I sighed.

“Yes.”

“Why did you sigh?”

“Future disagreement.”

“What?”

“Nothing.”

Her eyes narrowed.

I corrected.

“I am bad at compliance.”

“That is not future disagreement.”

Fair.

She asked what happened to the leg.

I said, “A man took it.”

She stared.

Not the answer people usually gave.

“Deliberately?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“I took his finger.”

Long silence.

“This is becoming less medical.”

“Yes.”

“Is he going to take anything else?”

“Not if I can help it.”

There.

Too much.

She heard it.

“What are you planning?”

“Nothing today.”

“That was not my question.”

Same person.

Damn it.

I looked at her.

“I am going to need to become much harder to kill.”

She looked at the peg.

Then at me.

“That is not the same as becoming healthy.”

There.

First lesson.

Second time.

Different life.

I had to sit with that.

She noticed.

“Why did that bother you?”

“It didn’t.”

“Bad answer.”

“Fine.”

I looked toward the shelves.

“I have known people who confuse capability with health.”

“Most field people do.”

“Yes.”

“You?”

“Yes.”

“Then don’t.”

Simple.

Impossible.

Useful.

I asked what consulting would cost.

She named a small number.

Small to First-Life Greg.

Not small to current Greg.

Still affordable.

I almost offered ten years.

Stopped.

Good.

One session.

That was what she offered.

One.

No guarantee.

No half.

No relocation.

No lamb.

One paid consultation about rehabilitation maintenance and poison-risk planning if relevant.

I said yes.

She wrote the appointment.

Then looked at me.

“Anything else?”

A thousand things.

“No.”

“Good.”

I left.

Outside, the stairs were still annoying.

My socket hurt.

My shoulder hurt.

Hale was still alive.

Nessa was still dead.

The children were still dead.

Tracy existed.

That was not the same as having Tracy.

I had found the person.

I had not found the relationship.

Those ten years were gone too.

That loss was smaller.

It still counted.

Ithar said nothing for a while.

Then:

“Did you tell her?”

“Eventually.”

“About the first life.”

“Yes.”

“Did she believe you?”

“Eventually.”

“Did she become your Number Two again?”

I looked at him.

“No.”

That answer mattered.

“Why not?”

“Because she was not obligated to become the woman I remembered.”

Ithar lowered his head.

“And did you understand that then?”

I thought about the first consultation.

The arguments.

The times I pushed too hard because I knew where we had once ended.

The times she pulled away because I acted like a promise had already been made.

“No.”

Honest.

“Not yet.”
