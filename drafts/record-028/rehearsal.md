## RECORD 028

## THE BIDDER

I lost my first large contract to a man who owned twelve ladders.

That was not the only reason.

It was the one I complained about.

The contract covered winter maintenance for six public buildings and two market branches.

Not stations.

Building-side systems.

Heat exchangers. Pressure regulators. Emergency shutoffs. Scheduled inspections before winter load. Call response afterward.

Ordinary work multiplied until it became money large enough to frighten me.

Lorn found the notice at Guild Hall and brought it to our new room.

He put the sheet on Nessa's desk.

She looked at him.

“Wrong desk.”

“They are touching.”

“That does not make his paper mine.”

“Household precedent says otherwise.”

I looked up.

“What?”

Lorn pointed at the contract.

“Bid.”

I read it.

Eight sites.

Five-month term.

Routine inspection plus emergency coverage.

Fixed base fee.

Materials separate above threshold.

Critical heat loss response within two bells.

I read the total estimate twice.

Then a third time because large numbers develop extra digits when observed.

“We can do that.”

Nessa said, “Can you?”

“Yes.”

Lorn said, “Yes.”

She looked between us.

“That was fast.”

“We know the systems.”

“You know eight buildings?”

“I know four.”

“Half.”

“Lorn knows the bath.”

“I know the owner owes money to everyone.”

“That counts.”

Nessa took the notice.

“What is the call requirement?”

“Two bells.”

“Every day?”

“Critical failures.”

“That was not what I asked.”

I found the small line.

Service coverage continuous during contract term.

There it was.

“We rotate.”

“With whom?”

“Me. Lorn. Rell.”

“Rell agreed?”

“No.”

“Excellent crew.”

“She will.”

“How do you know?”

“Current evidence.”

Nessa stared.

I sighed.

“I will ask.”

The bid packet was fourteen pages.

I hated it before page three.

Guild registration. Worker list. Trade authority. Rank coverage. Emergency plan. Material rates. Wage schedule.

Then surety reserve.

“What is a surety reserve?”

Lorn looked at me.

“I don't know.”

Good.

Shared incompetence.

We went to Guild Hall.

The clerk explained that public maintenance contracts above fifty silver required proof the contractor could finish or fail safely.

Cash reserve. Bonded sponsor. Guild surety.

Enough to cover one month of wages plus emergency replacement labor if the contractor disappeared, died, became insolvent, or decided winter was spiritually optional.

“How much?”

He calculated.

“Forty-eight silver.”

I laughed.

The clerk did not.

“That is not funny.”

“I know.”

“It is your workers' wages.”

“I know.”

“Then why laugh?”

“Alternative to crying.”

We had savings.

Household savings. Private-work float. Tool replacement. Family reserve.

Not forty-eight silver we could freeze for five months without becoming stupid.

“Guild surety?”

“Premium.”

“How much?”

He gave the number.

Also not funny.

“Who pays that?”

“Contractors without reserve.”

“Then how do they get reserve?”

“By completing contracts.”

I stared.

The clerk shrugged.

“Yes.”

Civilization had discovered circles before I did.

There were more requirements.

Three comparable jobs.

We had dozens of calls. None at this scale.

Two qualified response workers available at all times.

Possible.

Written substitute coverage if both leads were unavailable.

We had none.

Approved parts account.

Mina's cousin selling fittings from a cart apparently did not count.

Liability history.

Our Rope Street complaint appeared there.

Resolved.

Still there.

I disliked records when they remembered me.

Lorn read the packet at our table that night.

“Still bid.”

“We cannot meet reserve.”

“Guild surety.”

“Costs nine silver.”

“Contract pays more.”

“Only if we win and nothing goes wrong.”

“That is business.”

Nessa looked up from her district schedule.

“No. That is gambling with invoices.”

“Same family.”

She came over.

We made numbers.

Of course.

The base fee looked large until we put wages underneath it.

Then Rell, if she joined.

Second fitter when needed.

Travel.

Surety premium.

Call availability.

Tool wear.

Filing.

Emergency cart hire.

Material float before city reimbursement.

By the bottom of the page, the impressive number had become ordinary.

Still profitable.

If we estimated correctly.

If emergency calls stayed normal.

If clients paid on time.

If nobody got hurt.

If winter behaved.

Nessa tapped the page.

“You can bid.”

“You just spent an hour making it smaller.”

“Yes.”

“Why bid?”

“Because now you know the price.”

Annoying.

Good advice often is.

Rell agreed.

Not immediately.

She wanted guaranteed minimum pay during the contract even if call volume was low.

Lorn objected.

“Why pay if we don't need her?”

Rell looked at him.

“Because if you reserve my winter, I cannot take other work.”

Lorn stopped.

Good.

We added standby pay.

Price up.

Then Rell asked who covered if my shoulder flared.

“I do.”

“That is not an answer.”

Nessa smiled from across the room.

Everyone had learned my weak points.

We arranged reciprocal coverage with another small contractor, Sera Pellin.

Not free.

Price up again.

By submission day we were no longer cheap.

That felt like failure.

Lorn said it felt like adulthood.

Neither of us knew.

The Guild opened names and totals publicly.

Five bidders.

We were second cheapest.

Lowest was Harven Works.

Established firm.

Twenty-three employees.

Two carts.

Parts yard.

Twelve ladders.

I counted.

“Who needs twelve ladders?” I whispered.

Lorn said, “People with eight buildings.”

Traitor.

Harven's price was only three silver below ours.

I became hopeful.

Mistake.

Technical review liked us.

Best emergency stop plan. Strong local references. Good field experience.

Harven beat us on coverage depth.

Parts inventory.

Substitute staffing.

Financial reserve.

Public-contract history.

And yes, ladders.

Equipment availability, technically.

Same insult.

The award went to Harven.

Lorn read the decision.

“Fuck.”

“Yes.”

“We were good.”

“Yes.”

“Then why them?”

I pointed at the scoring sheet.

He read it again.

“Because they own everything.”

“No.”

I hated the answer while saying it.

“Because if their lead gets sick, the contract still exists.”

Lorn looked at me.

That was it.

Our operation still concentrated too much risk in people.

Me. Him. Rell.

Harven had redundancy.

Not necessarily better workers.

More ways for work to continue after one worker disappeared from the schedule.

My shoulder returned to the conversation without being invited.

A public client did not want to rearrange itself around Greg Vale's tendon inflammation.

Reasonable.

I still hated them.

Harven started in autumn.

I waited for failure.

Petty.

One market regulator stuck in November.

Harven replaced it in one bell.

School heat exchanger cracked.

They had a spare plate in their yard.

Public bath lost feed on a Sunday.

A crew arrived while their lead inspector was apparently two towns away.

Infuriating competence.

Then one ladder broke.

Nobody was hurt.

I enjoyed that too much.

Nessa said so.

“They have eleven now.”

“Still excessive.”

“Let it go.”

“No.”

We did not become Harven Works.

That would have been another bad overcorrection.

We changed three things.

Reciprocal coverage with Sera became permanent instead of something invented for bids.

We opened an actual supplier account and kept a small standard parts inventory.

And I started documenting comparable jobs properly.

Scope. Hours. Workers. Outcome. Client reference.

Nobody awarding a contract could inspect the contents of my head.

They should not have to.

A year later we bid a smaller public package.

Three buildings. No continuous winter coverage. Surety twelve silver.

We won.

Good.

Not the important part.

The first loss had been fair.

No corrupt official.

No idiot procurement clerk.

No institution too stupid to recognize Greg's brilliance.

Harven Works was simply more legible as a promise.

People.

Parts.

Records.

Money held against failure.

Ladders.

Too many ladders.

I was technically good enough for the work.

The contract required more than technical goodness.

That offended me until I started paying other people.

Then it became obvious.
