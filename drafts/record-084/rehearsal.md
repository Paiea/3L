## RECORD 084

## THE HALF

The next day I brought Tracy a contract.

Not a proposal.

A contract.

This was my first mistake.

She looked at the folder.

Then at me.

Then at the food in my other hand.

“Which one is the bribe?”

“Neither.”

“The food is definitely a bribe.”

“Lunch.”

“You have learned nothing.”

“Not true. You like lamb.”

“I like money more.”

Useful.

I put both on the counter.

The lunch disappeared first.

The contract remained.

Tracy ate two bites before opening it.

That was also useful.

People reveal priorities if you stop demanding they explain them.

She read the first page.

Then the second.

Stopped chewing.

Read the first page again.

“What is this?”

“Employment.”

“No.”

“Consulting?”

“No.”

“Mentorship?”

She looked up.

“What?”

“I want you to teach me.”

That actually got her attention.

Not the way I expected.

“You’re S-class.”

“Yes.”

“I’m D.”

“Yes.”

“You want me to teach you.”

“Yes.”

“What?”

“The thing you aren’t saying.”

She stared at me.

I had apparently found the fastest route back to irritation.

Good.

“Greg.”

“Tracy.”

“You do understand that the letter after my name is lower than the letter after yours.”

“In one category.”

“In most categories.”

“Probably.”

“Definitely.”

“Fine.”

She put the contract down.

“I cannot do what you do.”

“I know.”

“I cannot support an S-class body at S-class output.”

“I know.”

“I cannot stand in front of anything that requires an S-class body.”

“I know.”

“If you get split open from shoulder to hip, I am going to look at you and recommend a better healer.”

“Comforting.”

“It should be.”

“I still want you.”

Wrong sentence.

The clerk beside her looked over.

Tracy closed her eyes.

“For work.”

“Better.”

“I want to know how you look at problems.”

“That is not a job.”

“It can be.”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because nobody hires a person to think at them.”

“I am.”

“You are weird.”

“We covered that.”

She looked back at the contract.

The first section was simple.

Ten years guaranteed at more than her Guild salary.

Not absurdly more.

Enough.

Then replacement for benefits.

Credential maintenance.

Relocation if required.

Equipment.

Professional training.

Field work paid separately.

Hazard premiums separate again.

Travel expenses.

Medical coverage.

Termination protection.

If I ended the arrangement without cause, the guarantee remained.

If I died, a funded reserve paid the remaining guarantee.

That section had required an accountant.

The accountant had asked several questions about my mental health.

I had answered the financial ones.

Tracy read slowly.

Her food cooled.

That was how I knew the contract mattered.

She reached the death clause.

“You funded this?”

“Yes.”

“You already moved the money?”

“Enough to secure the obligation.”

Her eyes narrowed.

“You were serious yesterday.”

“Yes.”

“I was joking.”

“I know.”

“No, I really don’t think you do.”

“I understood the joke.”

“Then why is there a death reserve?”

“Because I might die.”

She stared.

Reasonable.

She kept reading.

“No exclusivity?”

“Unless there’s a direct conflict.”

“So I can still take other medical work.”

“Yes.”

“Guild work?”

“If they allow it after you leave.”

“I’m not leaving yet.”

“Correct.”

“You wrote ten years.”

“You said ten years.”

“I said ten years because I wanted you to go away.”

“Poor pricing strategy.”

She made a sound through her nose.

Then reached the compensation schedule.

Stopped.

Turned the page.

Turned it back.

“Greg.”

“Yes.”

“What is this?”

“Profit participation.”

“I can read.”

Good.

“Then what’s confusing?”

“The number.”

“Half.”

“I know what half is.”

Excellent start.

She flattened the paper against the counter.

“Half of what?”

“Net distributable profit from work we originate together.”

She stared.

I continued because silence made me nervous then.

“Direct mission expenses first. Everybody else’s agreed pay first. Medical reserve. Equipment reserve. Contractual obligations. Taxes and Guild assessments. Then what remains.”

“Half.”

“Yes.”

“For me.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Alignment.”

She looked offended.

“Do not say words like that at me.”

Fair.

“If you save us money, it should matter to you.”

“It matters to me anyway.”

“Not the same.”

“If you find a better way to do something, you should benefit.”

“I have a salary.”

“You complained about commission.”

“I complained because complaining is free.”

“Now commission is also available.”

“This is not commission.”

“What is it?”

“This is half.”

“Yes.”

She leaned closer.

“I am D-rank.”

“I know.”

“I sell antidotes.”

“I know.”

“I maintain medical stock.”

“I know.”

“I have been on six real field deployments.”

“Seven.”

Her eyes narrowed.

“How do you know that?”

“Public credential history.”

“That is creepy.”

“Efficient.”

“No.”

She pointed at the page.

“You are S-class.”

“Yes.”

“You make more money by standing near a contract than I make in months.”

“Sometimes.”

“You have access I do not have.”

“Yes.”

“You are taking the risk.”

“Sometimes.”

“You are bringing the contracts.”

“At first.”

“At first?”

“You’ll bring some later.”

She looked at me like I had become less intelligent during the conversation.

“I am not a business person.”

“Neither am I.”

“That is not reassuring.”

“I didn’t hire you to be one.”

“You haven’t hired me at all.”

“Correct.”

She looked at the half again.

“I like money.”

“I noticed.”

“No. I really like money.”

“Good.”

“That is why I am telling you this is stupid.”

I paused.

That was not where I expected the sentence to go.

Tracy tapped the page.

“If I sign this, I will take the money.”

“I would hope so.”

“I will not become noble halfway through.”

“Please don’t.”

“If there is profit, I will want my half.”

“Yes.”

“If you decide later that I was only a D-rank clerk when we met, that will not become my problem.”

“It won’t.”

“If I become more useful, the half stays half.”

“Yes.”

“If you become ten times more useful, the half stays half.”

“Yes.”

She stared.

“Why?”

I thought about it.

Because I was rich enough to be generous was not the answer.

Because I liked her was premature.

Because she might become important was true but incomplete.

So I told her the actual reason.

“I think I make more with you than without you.”

She stopped.

There.

That was the first time the conversation became serious in the way I wanted.

“I have known you two days,” she said.

“I know.”

“You cannot know that.”

“No.”

“Then what you have is a theory.”

“Yes.”

“And you’re offering half on a theory.”

“Yes.”

“That is an insane way to manage money.”

“Probably.”

She picked up the contract again.

“Who wrote this?”

“Lawyer.”

“Good.”

“You sound disappointed.”

“I was hoping to insult you for doing it yourself.”

“Accountant too.”

“Better.”

She read farther.

Then found the expense definitions.

Her expression worsened.

“This is bad.”

I leaned forward.

“What?”

“Net profit.”

“Yes.”

“You define direct expenses.”

“Yes.”

“Badly.”

I looked.

She pointed.

“This lets you classify equipment after distribution if you personally own it.”

“I wasn’t planning to.”

“I don’t care what you were planning.”

Oh.

There she was.

“If it supports shared work, it needs to be expensed before the split or excluded explicitly.”

“Okay.”

“And reserve percentages cannot be whatever you feel like that month.”

“Why?”

“Because then ‘half’ means whatever you decide half means.”

Fair.

She found another clause.

“This is worse.”

“What?”

“Travel meals.”

“They’re reimbursed.”

“At standard Guild rate.”

“Yes.”

“No.”

That answer came faster than the accounting critique.

I looked at her.

“Why?”

“Guild travel food is terrible.”

“That’s not accounting.”

“It is if I have to eat it for ten years.”

I laughed.

She did not.

“You are serious.”

“I told you I like food.”

“What do you want?”

“Reasonable actual meal expense.”

“That sounds abusable.”

“Yes.”

“You’re admitting that.”

“I like money too.”

We stared at each other.

Then she smiled.

First warning.

I changed the clause later.

Not unlimited.

Reasonable actual meal expense with a cap high enough that Tracy called it survivable.

The lawyer called it eccentric.

The accountant called it immaterial.

Tracy called the accountant sensible.

Then she found the part I had thought was generous.

“You can terminate for any reason,” she read.

“With guarantee intact.”

“I know.”

“You can move me anywhere?”

“With agreement.”

“Define agreement.”

“Agreement.”

“Written.”

“Fine.”

“Before relocation costs.”

“Fine.”

“Before lease.”

“Fine.”

“Before I pack.”

“Fine.”

She kept making marks.

The contract became better.

That annoyed me.

I had brought her an offer.

She turned it into infrastructure.

At one point the clerk beside her whispered, “Are you actually considering this?”

Tracy looked at him.

“Half.”

He leaned over.

Read the page.

Looked at me.

Then at Tracy.

“Take it.”

“Thank you,” she said.

“I was not asking.”

“I know.”

She went back to the contract.

That was Tracy.

Praise did almost nothing.

Money focused her beautifully.

I tried one last argument.

“You’d also get access to S-class work.”

Nothing.

“Unusual cases.”

Nothing.

“Training.”

Slight interest.

“Better field credential opportunities.”

Nothing again.

Then I said, “The restaurant from yesterday is two streets from the office I’m considering.”

She looked up.

“Which office?”

I smiled.

“Now you care.”

“I cared before.”

“No.”

“I cared financially.”

Fair.

We did not sign that day.

Or the next.

Tracy made me bring the lawyer back.

Then the accountant.

Then she brought somebody from the Guild who understood pension transfer.

She did not care whether this made her look difficult.

That was another thing I learned.

Some people want to be seen as intelligent.

Some want to be seen as competent.

Some want to be seen as hardworking.

Tracy wanted the contract to pay.

Everything else was decoration.

The final version took nine days.

Ten-year guarantee.

Benefits replaced.

Credential and training budget.

Field pay separate.

Hazard pay separate.

Relocation protected.

No exclusivity outside direct conflict.

Actual travel meals.

A funded death reserve.

And half the distributable profit from the work we built together after agreed expenses and reserves.

Tracy read the final page.

Then looked at me.

“I still think this is stupid.”

“Probably.”

“I am absolutely taking the money.”

“Good.”

“If you regret this later, I will remind you that I told you.”

“Fine.”

“And I get half.”

“Yes.”

She picked up the pen.

Paused.

“One more thing.”

“What?”

“I am not your assistant.”

“I know.”

“I am not going to stand behind you and make your ideas happen because you are S-class.”

“Good.”

“If something is stupid, I’m saying it’s stupid.”

“That is partly why I’m paying you.”

“No.”

“What?”

“That part is free.”

She signed.

I signed.

The clerk beside her clapped once.

Tracy glared at him.

He stopped.

Ithar shifted in the cave.

“You gave half of your profit to a D-class clerk.”

“Yes.”

“After knowing her nine days.”

“Yes.”

“That was stupid.”

“Probably.”

“Did it work?”

I looked at him.

“Enormously.”

He waited.

I smiled.

“The first thing she did was tell me my marsh idea was stupid.”
