## RECORD 087

## THE YARD

Three weeks later Tracy said, “I have an idea.”

We were at Marrin’s.

Again.

This became expensive.

Worth it.

“What?”

“There’s a yard for sale.”

“What kind of yard?”

“Transport.”

I put the fork down.

“No.”

“You asked me to look.”

“I asked you to find out what transport costs.”

“I did.”

“And?”

“We should buy a yard.”

“That is not a cost.”

“It is if you buy it.”

Fair.

She slid a folder across the table.

I did not open it.

“How bad?”

“Depends.”

“On?”

“How much you enjoy owning animals.”

“No.”

“Good. Me neither.”

I opened the folder.

Six wagons.

Two heavy freight carts.

Nine regular drivers.

Three part-time.

Stable space.

Repair shed.

Feed storage.

One office.

One building that looked better on paper than in person.

The owner was retiring.

Not dramatically.

Old knees.

No children interested.

Two drivers had already left because they assumed the business would close.

The rest were waiting to see what happened.

“Why this one?”

“Cheap.”

“That is not reassuring.”

“Not because it’s bad.”

“Why cheap?”

“Owner wants out fast.”

“Still not reassuring.”

She pointed at the numbers.

“Land is included.”

I looked again.

That changed things.

“How much?”

She told me.

I looked at her.

She smiled.

“No.”

“What?”

“You have that face.”

“What face?”

“The one where you think I’m insane.”

“I do think you’re insane.”

“Good. Keep reading.”

I did.

The wagons were not excellent.

Two were old.

One needed axle work.

One had a roof leak.

The animals were healthy except one mare with a recurring hoof problem.

The repair shed had tools.

Good tools.

That caught me.

Tracy noticed.

“Don’t get distracted.”

“I’m not.”

“You’re looking at the tools.”

“They’re good tools.”

“This is why you need me.”

Cruel.

She had estimated our current transport spend.

Projected our likely spend if the marsh expanded another twenty percent.

Added storage transfers.

Emergency calls.

Worker movement.

Medical runs.

Then compared all of it against owning the yard.

Year one was ugly.

Year two less ugly.

Year three looked good.

That was assuming nothing terrible happened.

Something terrible always happened.

“Maintenance reserve is too low,” I said.

Her eyes sharpened.

“How low?”

“Double.”

“That much?”

“Old wagons.”

She frowned.

Looked again.

“Fine.”

“Driver turnover?”

“Built in.”

“Animal replacement?”

“Partly.”

“Feed spikes?”

“Buffer.”

“Insurance?”

“Here.”

“Roof?”

“Not transport.”

“It becomes transport when the feed gets wet.”

She stared.

Then wrote that down.

Good.

We spent two hours ruining her good idea.

At the end it was better.

More expensive.

Still profitable.

I looked at her.

“You have enough?”

She blinked.

“For what?”

“Your half.”

She understood.

The purchase did not require only my money.

That mattered.

Tracy looked at the total.

Then at her own available capital.

She could cover half.

Barely.

Doing so would use most of what she had made from the marsh.

For the first time since I met her, she looked unhappy about spending money.

Not scared.

Offended.

“I like having money,” she said.

“I know.”

“I just got this money.”

“I know.”

“And now you want me to put it into wagons.”

“This was your idea.”

“That doesn’t make it less rude.”

I laughed.

She did not.

Then she looked at the year-three projection again.

“I hate you.”

“No you don’t.”

“No.”

She invested.

So did I.

The purchase closed eleven days later.

We owned a transport yard.

This immediately proved we were idiots.

The roof leaked more than disclosed.

The old axle was worse.

One driver quit because he disliked the new route schedule.

The mare’s hoof problem became a leg problem.

A feed delivery arrived moldy.

Then a wheel cracked halfway through a glassroot run and delayed two wagons behind it.

The first month cost more than Kettler would have.

Significantly more.

I brought the accounting sheet to Tracy.

She looked at it.

Then at me.

“Do not.”

“I have not said anything.”

“You are going to.”

“I was going to ask how our excellent investment is doing.”

“Fuck you.”

Progress.

She took the sheet.

Read it.

Read it again.

Then went to the yard.

I followed.

Not because she asked.

Because I wanted to see what happened when Tracy lost money.

Apparently the answer was violence against paperwork.

Not people.

Mostly.

She sat in the office with the maintenance logs.

Old logs.

New logs.

Driver reports.

Repair invoices.

Feed receipts.

Route sheets.

She built piles.

Then categories.

Then a wall.

I left for two hours.

Came back.

Still wall.

“Have you eaten?”

“No.”

I put food beside her.

She opened it without looking.

Growth.

“What went wrong?”

She kept reading.

“Everything we paid Kettler not to think about.”

That was accurate.

She pointed at one stack.

“Maintenance happened when someone complained.”

“Bad.”

“Yes.”

“Inspection?”

“Visual.”

“Bad.”

“Yes.”

“Replacement cycle?”

“No real one.”

“Bad.”

“Yes.”

“Feed?”

“Cheapest available unless moldy enough to smell.”

“Very bad.”

“Yes.”

“Drivers?”

“Knowledge lives in their heads.”

“Meaning?”

“If Nerin gets sick, nobody knows the north industrial route as well.”

“Fixable.”

“Yes.”

She finally looked up.

“That is the point.”

“What?”

“We bought the failures.”

I leaned against the desk.

“You knew that.”

“I knew the sentence.”

Different.

Good distinction.

She tapped the maintenance logs.

“Now I know the failures.”

The next month was not cheaper.

That annoyed her.

The month after that was.

Then cheaper again.

She built inspection schedules.

Not elaborate.

Useful.

Wheels before failure.

Axles before noise.

Harness before tearing.

Feed supplier requirements.

Two suppliers, not one.

Route documentation.

Every regular route written down.

Two drivers trained on every high-value run.

One wagon kept unassigned most days.

I objected.

“A wagon that does nothing makes nothing.”

“A wagon that is available when another breaks prevents losing a day.”

“Still idle.”

“Reserve is not idle.”

That sounded familiar.

I stopped arguing.

She made drivers record problems before end of shift.

Not after the wagon broke.

One driver hated it.

Then his front wheel was replaced during a scheduled stop instead of collapsing loaded.

He complained less.

The mare retired.

Tracy objected to selling her cheap because someone would work her anyway.

So the mare stayed.

This was not financially optimal.

I pointed that out.

Tracy looked at me.

“She worked here twelve years.”

“That is not accounting.”

“No.”

We kept the mare.

Her name was Pella.

She became expensive grass decoration.

Worth it.

By month six, the yard handled almost all marsh movement.

Then most staff transport.

Then medical stock.

Then outside work when we had empty capacity.

That part had not been in the original plan.

A driver said:

“We’re already going south empty. Why not carry something?”

Tracy looked at him.

Then at me.

Then at the route sheet.

We carried something.

Made money on the return.

That happened again.

Soon other people were calling us.

Not many.

Enough.

Greg’s wagons.

That was what people called them.

Tracy hated that.

“They are not Greg’s wagons.”

“They kind of are.”

“I paid half.”

“Yes.”

“I rewrote maintenance.”

“Yes.”

“I bought the second feed contract.”

“Yes.”

“You forgot the roof.”

“I did not forget the roof.”

“You absolutely forgot the roof.”

The roof had become personal.

Ithar made a low sound.

Possibly amusement.

“So the transport company was hers.”

“Ours.”

“You are correcting me.”

“She would.”

Fair.

The yard taught us something the marsh had not.

Prevention cost money even when nothing happened.

That made it emotionally difficult.

A replaced wheel looked like an expense.

A broken wheel that never happened had no receipt.

A backup driver looked inefficient until somebody got sick.

A spare wagon looked idle until a loaded axle cracked.

A second supplier looked redundant until the first shipment arrived moldy.

Tracy liked money.

That was why she became very good at spending it before emergencies.

Not because she enjoyed expense.

Because she hated paying after leverage disappeared.

The yard became profitable.

Then useful.

Useful mattered more.

We stopped asking whether each wagon made money by itself.

We asked whether the whole operation worked better because the wagon existed.

That was a dangerous question.

Once you start asking it, you can justify owning many things.

A year after the purchase, Tracy walked into my office.

Not the yard office.

A better one.

We had moved.

She put a folder on my desk.

“I have an idea.”

I looked at the folder.

Then at her.

“How much?”

She blinked.

“You don’t know what it is.”

“Do you have enough?”

“For half?”

“Yes.”

She hesitated.

Then smiled.

“Yes.”

I pushed the folder back toward her.

“Run it.”

That was the first time.

She did not open the folder.

She just stood there.

“You’re not going to ask?”

“No.”

“You asked seventeen questions about the yard.”

“I remember.”

“This is more expensive.”

“I assumed.”

“You don’t know what I’m buying.”

“No.”

“That is irresponsible.”

“Probably.”

She stared at me.

“Why?”

I thought about the marsh.

The contract.

The yard.

The roof.

Pella eating grass.

Every stupid detail she had noticed before I did.

“Because you know what you’re buying.”

She looked at the folder.

Then back at me.

That changed something.

Not authority.

Trust.

She picked up the folder.

“Fine.”

“What is it?”

Now she smiled properly.

“No.”

I stared.

“You just asked.”

“You said run it.”

“That was before curiosity.”

“Too late.”

She left.

Ithar looked at me.

“You gave her permission without knowing the purchase.”

“Yes.”

“What did she buy?”

I sighed.

“The step before another problem.”
