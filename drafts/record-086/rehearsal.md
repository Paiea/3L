## RECORD 086

## THE SHARE

The first large distribution made Tracy quiet.

That was unusual enough that I noticed.

We were in the back room of Marrin’s.

Not our back room.

Not yet.

Just a private room the owner let us use because we ate there too often and tipped well enough to become predictable.

The marsh had been running scaled operations for four months.

Not continuously.

Tracy would have killed me.

Rotating crews.

Scheduled closures.

Maintenance days.

Weather holds.

One whole week lost because the clean-water pumps started producing a metallic taste and Tracy refused to accept “probably fine” as a medical category.

It was not poison.

Rust from an old fitting.

She remained pleased with herself for several days.

The operation made money anyway.

A lot more than I expected.

That was becoming a theme.

The accountant put the distribution sheet on the table.

Expenses.

Wages.

Hazard premiums.

Medical reserve.

Equipment reserve.

Taxes.

Guild fees.

Transport.

Storage.

Processing.

Then distributable profit.

Split.

Half.

Half.

Tracy stared at her number.

I ate.

She did not.

That was how serious it was.

“You okay?”

“Yes.”

“You stopped eating.”

“I’m thinking.”

“Dangerous.”

She ignored me.

Then asked the accountant, “This is after reserve?”

“Yes.”

“After next month’s committed payroll?”

“Yes.”

“Transport invoices?”

“Yes.”

“All of them?”

The accountant looked at her.

“Yes, Tracy.”

“Storage?”

“Yes.”

“Processing loss?”

“Yes.”

“Breakage?”

“Yes.”

She looked at the number again.

Then at me.

“This is mine.”

“Yes.”

“Already taxed?”

“Estimated withholding is shown below.”

“I know how to read.”

“Then why ask?”

She stared.

Fair.

I kept eating.

Brann was there too.

He had become too useful to exclude from planning and too opinionated to enjoy including.

His crew had done most of the early marsh work.

He had negotiated good hazard rates.

Very good.

Tracy had helped him negotiate them.

That became relevant when he leaned across the table and looked at her sheet.

His face changed.

“Half?”

Tracy covered the paper with her hand.

“Eyes.”

“Sorry.”

He was not sorry.

“Half?”

I kept eating.

Tracy looked at me.

Not for rescue.

Curiosity.

She wanted to know what I would say.

Brann looked between us.

“You get half?”

“Yes,” Tracy said.

“Of the whole operation?”

“Distributable profit.”

“That is half.”

“Yes.”

He looked at me.

“Why?”

“Contract.”

“No, I understand how paper works.”

“Good.”

“You gave her half.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“We agreed.”

Brann leaned back.

“That’s insane.”

Tracy nodded.

“I told him.”

“That doesn’t bother you?”

“No.”

“Of course it doesn’t. You’re getting half.”

“Correct.”

I loved her sometimes.

Not romantically.

That would have complicated payroll.

Brann looked at me again.

“I don’t get half.”

“No.”

“My crew does the harvesting.”

“Yes.”

“I run field operations.”

“Yes.”

“I have been doing this longer than she has.”

“Yes.”

“So why does she get half?”

I put the fork down.

“Because that’s the deal I made with her.”

“What about our deal?”

“You get exactly what we made.”

He frowned.

I continued.

“Your base rate went up twice.”

“Yes.”

“Hazard premium.”

“Yes.”

“Crew completion bonus.”

“Yes.”

“Profit kicker above target.”

“Yes.”

“Guaranteed minimum even during weather closure.”

He hesitated.

“Yes.”

“Did I reduce any of that because Tracy gets half?”

“No.”

“Then what are we discussing?”

“Fairness.”

“Different contracts.”

“That’s convenient.”

“It is.”

He stared.

I understood the complaint.

That mattered.

If I had been him, I might have had it too.

So I tried again.

“When we started, what was Black Fen worth to you?”

“Depends on the run.”

“Average.”

He gave a number.

“After poison?”

Lower number.

“After lost days?”

Lower again.

“After bad years?”

He stopped.

Tracy looked at him.

Brann sighed.

“Not much.”

I pointed at the distribution sheet.

“What is it worth now?”

He did not answer.

Did not need to.

His crew had made more in four months than on any previous fen season he had run.

Not because we paid them less.

Because the operation made more.

I nodded toward Tracy.

“She benefited.”

“No shit.”

“So did you.”

He said nothing.

“So did I.”

Still nothing.

“We made more with her than without her.”

Brann looked at the half again.

“You gave away half.”

“No.”

That came faster than I expected.

I thought about why.

Then understood.

“I kept half of something larger.”

Tracy stopped looking at Brann.

Looked at me instead.

That sentence mattered to her.

Brann rubbed his face.

“So if I want half, what?”

“You should have negotiated half.”

“That’s your answer?”

“Yes.”

“You’d give me half?”

“No.”

He stared.

“Why not?”

“Because that’s not the deal we made.”

“That is incredibly annoying.”

“I know.”

Tracy finally went back to eating.

Conversation apparently resolved for her.

Brann was not done.

“She was D-rank when you hired her.”

“Yes.”

“You could have hired three healers.”

“Probably.”

“Better healers.”

“In some things.”

“For less.”

“Maybe.”

“And you still think this was smart.”

I looked at Tracy.

She had already moved on emotionally and was using bread to collect sauce from the plate.

“Yes.”

Tracy swallowed.

“He should not.”

Brann stared at her.

“What?”

“It was still stupid.”

“You made him money.”

“Yes.”

“You made us money.”

“Yes.”

“You’re saying he was wrong?”

“He could have structured it better.”

I looked offended.

“You wrote half the final contract.”

“After you decided half.”

“Details.”

“Accounting is details until someone steals from you.”

Brann laughed.

Tension broke.

Useful.

The distribution finished.

People left.

Tracy did not.

She sat with the final sheet and ran her finger down the transport line.

Again.

Then again.

I recognized the behavior.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“Face.”

“I have a face.”

“Weak defense.”

She pointed at the line.

“Transport is too high.”

“Yes.”

“Storage too.”

“Yes.”

“Processing less bad.”

“Yes.”

She looked at the total.

Then at her distribution.

Then back.

I knew what was happening.

“No.”

She looked up.

“I haven’t said anything.”

“You have your money face.”

“That is absolutely not a thing.”

“It is now.”

She smiled.

Dangerous.

“How much did we pay Kettler Transport this month?”

I checked.

She already knew.

Of course she did.

I told her.

“And last month?”

I told her.

“And the month before?”

“Tracy.”

“How much?”

I told her.

She added the three numbers.

Then looked at me.

“That is stupid.”

“They move everything.”

“I know.”

“That is why we pay them.”

“I know.”

“They own wagons.”

“I know.”

“Drivers.”

“I know.”

“Animals.”

“I know.”

“Yard.”

“I know.”

“Repair staff.”

“I know.”

She waited.

I understood.

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because you just listed six things we would have to own.”

“And removed eleven invoices.”

“It is not that simple.”

“No.”

That surprised me.

She folded the distribution sheet.

“It is worse.”

“Excellent.”

“If we own transport, we own breakdowns.”

“Yes.”

“Feed.”

“Yes.”

“Drivers getting sick.”

“Yes.”

“Replacement wheels.”

“Yes.”

“Stable space.”

“Yes.”

“Maintenance.”

“Yes.”

“Idle capacity.”

“Yes.”

“Insurance.”

“Yes.”

“Then why are you smiling?”

“Because we use enough transport that all of those are already in the price.”

There.

I stopped.

She leaned forward.

“We pay Kettler to own the problems.”

“Yes.”

“Good when we use them occasionally.”

“Yes.”

“We do not use them occasionally anymore.”

No.

We did not.

Marsh crews.

Glassroot.

Medical stock.

Water.

Food.

Equipment.

Processed resin.

Workers moving between sites.

Every expansion had added another reason to call Kettler.

Usually at the exact moment everyone else wanted transport too.

Rush rates.

Weather rates.

Night rates.

Priority rates.

Rates apparently reproduced when unsupervised.

Tracy tapped the page.

“We are paying somebody else’s margin because we do not want to own the failures.”

“That can be worth it.”

“Yes.”

I looked at her.

That was important.

She was not an ownership addict.

“Do you think it is?”

“No.”

“How sure?”

“Not enough yet.”

Better answer.

She took another piece of bread.

“I want their numbers.”

“Kettler’s?”

“Or a company like them.”

“They are not going to give you their books.”

“I know.”

“So?”

“Drivers talk.”

Oh no.

“Tracy.”

“Repair shops talk.”

“Tracy.”

“Feed suppliers definitely talk.”

“You sound criminal.”

“I sound curious.”

“You sound like you are about to become a problem.”

She smiled again.

“I have capital now.”

There it was.

Not Greg’s money.

Not abstract enterprise money.

Hers.

Her half.

Her choices.

The contract had changed something I had not predicted.

I had thought profit participation would align incentives.

It did.

I had not considered that it would also give Tracy enough personal capital to start having ideas at scale.

That should have worried me more.

Instead I asked:

“How much do you need?”

She looked at me.

“I don’t know yet.”

“Find out.”

She nodded.

Then finished the bread.

That was the beginning of transport.

Not because I wanted a transport company.

Not because Tracy dreamed of owning wagons.

We had simply reached the point where paying somebody else to own a recurring problem cost more than owning the problem ourselves.
