## RECORD 090

## THE FIELD

Tracy did not like field work.

This was inconvenient because I increasingly liked having her there.

Not every contract.

Most did not need her.

If the job was ordinary, hire an ordinary healer.

If the job needed massive trauma restoration, hire someone better than Tracy.

She said this herself.

Frequently.

“If your chest opens,” she told me, “I am not your best option.”

“Comforting.”

“It should be.”

“But poison?”

“Better.”

“Channel strain?”

She hesitated.

“Depends.”

“Me?”

Longer hesitation.

“Yes.”

There.

That became the problem.

The first time I insisted, she refused.

We were planning a four-day underground survey beneath an old flood-control complex.

Not Vey.

Nothing close.

Still ugly.

Rotting water.

Mineral contamination.

Several known venom species.

Old pressure infrastructure.

Tight spaces.

An A-class combat team.

One conventional B-class healer already attached.

Tracy read the brief.

“No.”

“You haven’t reached the pay.”

“I don’t care.”

“That is a lie.”

“I care less than I care about being underground with things that bite.”

“Double field rate.”

“No.”

“Hazard premium.”

“No.”

“Meals separate.”

She looked up.

I waited.

“Good meals?”

“Reasonable actual meal expense.”

“That contract language was a mistake.”

“Yes.”

She went back to the brief.

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because you already have a healer.”

“He doesn’t know me.”

“He is B-class.”

“Yes.”

“I am D.”

“Yes.”

“He can restore more tissue in one minute than I can in ten.”

“Probably.”

“He has better emergency stabilization.”

“Yes.”

“Better barrier healing.”

“Yes.”

“Then why me?”

There.

Good question.

“You know me.”

She looked annoyed.

“That is not medical.”

“It is.”

“No.”

“You know how my channels fail.”

“I know how you complain before they fail.”

“Exactly.”

“You know your shoulder starts compensating when you overdraw.”

“Yes.”

“You know you stop noticing hand tremor when you’re concentrating.”

“Yes.”

“You know I lie about headaches.”

“You do not lie.”

“Thank you.”

“You answer a different question.”

Fair.

She closed the brief.

“You’re asking me to be maintenance.”

“Yes.”

“I am a healer.”

“Yes.”

“Those are not the same.”

“No.”

“Then stop making that face.”

“What face?”

“The one where you think you solved the conversation.”

I had.

Mostly.

She came.

Double field rate.

Hazard premium.

Meals.

And one condition.

“If the B-class healer says you need him, you listen.”

“Fine.”

“If I say sit, you sit.”

I paused.

Tracy stared.

“That pause is why I’m adding it.”

“Fine.”

“Immediately.”

“Fine.”

She came.

The first day nothing happened.

This disappointed everyone except Tracy.

She considered uneventful medicine successful medicine.

The second day I found a pressure route nobody else had noticed.

Old maintenance channel behind the flood wall.

Still carrying load.

Not much.

Enough to matter.

We could bypass two dangerous chambers if I could stabilize the transfer while the team opened the service gate.

I could.

Probably.

Tracy watched me set the first link.

“Too much?”

“No.”

Second.

“Now?”

“No.”

Third.

She said, “Stop.”

I looked at her.

“I’m fine.”

“Sit.”

“Tracy.”

“Sit.”

The B-class healer looked between us.

Confused.

The combat lead looked annoyed.

“We are in the middle of this.”

“I know,” Tracy said.

I kept working.

Wrong.

She walked over and physically put two fingers against my wrist.

Not magic.

Pulse.

Then looked at my left hand.

Tiny tremor.

I had not noticed.

Of course.

“Greg.”

“I have another minute.”

“No.”

“I need thirty seconds.”

“No.”

“Twenty.”

She looked at the combat lead.

“Close it.”

He stared.

Greg the S-class support had said twenty seconds.

D-class Tracy said close it.

Interesting room.

The combat lead chose me.

Wrong.

“Twenty.”

I smiled.

Tracy did not.

I finished the fourth route.

Then my left shoulder failed.

Not dramatically.

Dropped.

One link sagged.

The B-class healer moved.

Tracy was already there.

She did not heal the shoulder.

That would have been the obvious thing.

She cut the third link.

I swore.

Then the second.

Load redistributed.

The service gate slammed halfway shut.

The combat lead shouted.

Tracy ignored him.

She put a stabilizing seal across my upper chest.

Then another at the shoulder.

Not restoration.

Containment.

My breathing came back.

Pain remained.

Useful pain.

“Sit.”

I sat.

Immediately.

Learning.

The B-class healer crouched beside me.

Checked the shoulder.

Then the channels.

Looked at Tracy.

“You saw that before the drop?”

“Yes.”

“How?”

“He changes his grip.”

I looked at her.

“What?”

“You change your grip.”

“I do not.”

“You do.”

The B-class healer said, “He does.”

Traitor.

The service gate had to be reset.

Cost us forty minutes.

The combat lead was furious.

Then we opened it safely.

Nobody died.

By dinner, he was less furious.

By morning, not at all.

That should have been the lesson.

It was not.

The poison came on day three.

Not from a creature.

Water.

A thin mineral toxin in one lower chamber where an old filtration seam had cracked.

Levels too low to trigger the standard strip immediately.

Enough for cumulative exposure.

Tracy noticed three people drinking more water than usual.

That was all.

Not dramatic.

Thirst.

Headache.

One person rubbing the side of his neck.

She stopped the team.

Tested again.

Different reagent.

Positive.

The B-class healer said, “That is barely over threshold.”

Tracy said, “Yes.”

“We can continue.”

“No.”

He looked at her.

She looked back.

Interesting room again.

I asked, “Why?”

She pointed at the route.

“Because barely over threshold is now.”

Then at the map.

“Two more hours is later.”

There.

Cheap stage.

Expensive stage.

We backed out.

Binder.

Wash.

Fluid replacement.

No full antidote.

No organ support.

No evacuation.

Three hours later everyone was fine.

The B-class healer found Tracy while we ate.

“I would have continued.”

“I know.”

“You were right.”

She shrugged.

He waited.

Apparently expecting modesty.

None came.

“You don’t care?”

“About being right?”

“Yes.”

“I care that we didn’t need the expensive treatment.”

He laughed.

Then asked her about the reagent.

They spent an hour talking.

That mattered more than the compliment.

After the mission, the Guild evaluator adjusted Tracy’s field notes.

Not rank.

Not yet.

More qualifying exposure hours.

More documented poison cases.

More high-rank team experience.

She looked at the paperwork.

Then at her pay.

“Good.”

I pointed at the evaluation.

“Also good.”

“Does it pay?”

“Eventually.”

“Then eventually good.”

That was Tracy.

She did not chase rank.

Rank chased documented work.

Over the next years, she fielded more.

Not constantly.

Enough.

Her output improved because she trained.

Her equipment improved because money existed.

Her case history became ridiculous because Greg contracts attracted ridiculous problems.

She remained worse than many healers at brute-force repair.

Better than most at preventing the need for it.

C-class arrived quietly.

She celebrated with expensive food.

B-class took longer.

She celebrated with more expensive food.

When someone asked whether she was proud, she said:

“Of what?”

“The rank.”

“My rate went up.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“It is what I answered.”

By then, when I said I wanted Tracy on a serious deployment, people stopped asking why.

Not because she was the strongest healer available.

Because she knew the system around me.

My body.

My channels.

My habits.

My lies that were not technically lies.

The way I changed my grip before overload.

The way I went quiet when pain got bad.

The way I said “one more minute” when the correct number was zero.

Other healers knew healing.

Tracy knew Greg.

That difference saved me repeatedly.

Mostly in ways nobody wrote songs about.
