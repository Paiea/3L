## RECORD 023

## THE COORDINATOR

Nessa got promoted and I responded by reorganizing the pantry.

This was not helpful.

At the time it felt excellent.

Control is very soothing when applied to lentils.

The promotion was not called a promotion.

Institutions like avoiding words that might imply more money.

DISTRICT TRANSFER COORDINATOR, ACTING.

Six months.

Three districts.

Load scheduling during planned outages, maintenance transfers, storm response, and any ordinary week when too many stations wanted the same capacity.

Nessa brought the notice home folded into quarters and put it beside my plate.

I read the title.

Then the pay line.

Then the hours.

Then the title again.

“You took it.”

“Yes.”

“When?”

“This morning.”

“You didn't ask me.”

The words came out before my brain approved them.

Nessa stopped chewing.

I looked at the table.

Still ours.

Still not level.

“That sounded bad,” I said.

“Yes.”

“I meant schedule.”

“Did you?”

“Mostly.”

She waited.

I had spent years learning that silence was not permission to improve your answer by inventing a new one.

“The hours are five days,” I said.

“Yes.”

“Two early.”

“Yes.”

“One late.”

“Yes.”

“You still have East Four shifts?”

“Two a month to keep field qualification.”

“And South Six?”

“No regular coverage.”

District office was across Market Bridge. Not far. Far enough that dinner changed. Far enough that my parents' appointments changed. Far enough that the household ledger would notice transport.

I could already see the week moving inside my head.

Private work Tuesdays.

Inspection Wednesday and Thursday.

Parents Friday morning.

Nessa late Friday.

Laundry.

Food.

Coal.

So I tried to fix it.

That was the first mistake.

“We can move shopping to sixth day,” I said.

Nessa looked at me.

“What?”

“Market. You usually go third day after shift. I'll do sixth.”

“Okay.”

“And laundry Sunday.”

“Greg.”

“Your late night is Friday, so my mother can move healer appointments to Thursday.”

“My late night.”

“Yes.”

“My appointment.”

“Yes.”

“You have had the paper for forty seconds.”

“Approximately.”

“And you already moved your mother's healer.”

“I haven't moved anything.”

“You moved her in your face.”

Apparently the face remained a security failure.

I put down the notice.

“Congratulations.”

Nessa stared.

“That sounded painful.”

“It was delayed.”

“By the pantry plan.”

“I have not mentioned the pantry.”

“You will.”

She was right.

I hated that too.

“Do you want it?” I asked.

The question should have come first.

We both knew that.

Nessa's expression softened.

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because I am good at it.”

Simple.

I understood that answer better than most.

She continued.

“I like station work. But I am tired of getting a transfer plan after somebody who has never held a live schedule decides three outages can overlap because they belong to different departments.”

“That is specific.”

“It happened Tuesday.”

“East Three, Market Five, tram branch?”

“Yes.”

“That was stupid.”

“Yes.”

“Who fixed it?”

“I did.”

There it was.

Her work.

Not my household problem.

Not my story.

Record Nineteen had already proved she could schedule live pressure better than I could. Tova had asked whether district could hire her.

Apparently district eventually took the hint.

“Acting?” I asked.

“Six months.”

“You think they make it permanent?”

“I don't know.”

“Do you want permanent?”

Nessa thought before answering.

“Maybe.”

I nodded.

“Okay.”

She looked at me.

“That's it?”

“No. I have seventeen schedule concerns.”

“There he is.”

“But congratulations first.”

“Better.”

I got up.

Opened the pantry.

Nessa laughed so hard she had to put her cup down.

“Fuck you.”

“Lentils?”

“They are disorganized.”

“They are beans.”

“Not all of them.”

“That is apparently your crisis.”

It was not.

The first month was irritating.

Nessa left before breakfast twice a week.

I discovered I did not like waking to an empty side of the bed.

This was immature.

I allowed myself three weeks.

Her late shift moved every second Friday because transfer schedules ignored our household planning out of spite.

I burned dinner once.

Enough that Mina came upstairs from the bakery to check whether her building was on fire.

“That is insulting,” I said.

“You filled the stairwell with smoke.”

“Temporary smoke.”

Nessa came home and ate the unburned half.

“You could have bought food.”

“I cooked.”

“You fought dinner and both lost.”

Cruel woman.

My private jobs moved too. One client hired somebody else rather than wait.

That bothered me less than expected.

Lorn taking two jobs I would once have taken bothered me more.

Not because I wanted the money.

Because his work no longer required my presence enough to flatter me.

Apparently bodies learn faster than egos.

My parents adjusted.

My mother stopped assuming Nessa could take her to healer appointments because Nessa was “the one with the better schedule.”

Her schedule was worse now.

Or better.

Depending on whose needs defined the word.

Lena crossed the river twice that month. I took one appointment. My father missed one because he forgot and blamed the paper calendar.

Family systems remained unprofessional.

The ledger changed.

Transport up.

Food away from home up.

Nessa's wage up more.

Professional clothing appeared as a category because district office expected shirts without burn marks.

I argued burn marks proved relevant experience.

Nessa bought two shirts anyway.

She looked good in them.

I did not tell district.

The harder change was information.

For years Nessa knew my work because we worked in the same system. East Four. Transfers. Halden. Dera. Even after district inspection took me elsewhere, she knew the stations and half the people.

Now she came home with problems I did not understand socially.

“West Three wants a longer storm reserve.”

“Give it.”

“From where?”

I stopped.

“Market Two?”

“They have the clinic expansion.”

“South feed?”

“Foundation repair.”

“East Four?”

Nessa stared.

“Absolutely not.”

“Why?”

“Dera will kill me.”

“Professional reason.”

“Also pump replacement.”

“Secondary reason.”

She threw a cloth at me.

She had her own web now.

Crews. Foremen. Night operators. Old grudges. Stations that exaggerated reserve need because one bad winter had taught them fear. Stations that understated need because asking made them look weak.

Nessa knew which was which.

I did not.

At first I treated this as missing data.

“What does South Two actually draw at sixth bell?”

“I don't know.”

“Who is their night operator?”

“Greg.”

“What?”

“I am telling you about my day, not submitting a report.”

“Oh.”

“Can I continue?”

“Yes.”

She continued.

I practiced listening without solving.

Advanced skill.

First-life S-class had not required it often enough.

Second life did.

Nessa was also bad at it.

That helped.

I came home angry about a contractor who ignored a stop condition.

She immediately told me what form I should change.

I stared.

“What?”

“I was telling you about my day.”

She stopped.

Then laughed.

“Fuck.”

“Yes.”

“Fine. Continue.”

We became slightly less unbearable together.

The acting appointment became permanent in month five.

Not because Nessa saved anything dramatic.

She reduced schedule conflicts.

That was it.

Planned outages overlapped live maintenance less often. Emergency reserve requests stopped arriving on three different forms. Night crews got earlier notice.

One station complained she favored market districts.

Another complained she favored residential loads.

The complaints contradicted each other enough that Halden called it promising.

Nessa hated that.

“I want them to know I am fair.”

I looked at her.

She saw Record Nineteen on my face.

“Don't.”

“I said nothing.”

“You thought defensible.”

“I did.”

“Asshole.”

She accepted the permanent post.

The pay increase was smaller than expected.

Of course.

The pension was better.

The hours became slightly more predictable.

Slightly is one of civilization's most important words.

We celebrated at a restaurant we could not quite afford without calling it a special occasion.

Which it was.

Lena crossed the river. My parents came. Eli ate dessert before dinner because nobody noticed until too late.

My mother raised her cup.

“To Nessa.”

Everyone did.

Nessa looked embarrassed.

Good.

I enjoyed that.

On the walk home, she said, “You know what the worst part is?”

“What?”

“I liked East Four.”

“You still like it.”

“I know.”

“You can like a place and leave the job.”

She looked at me.

Then smiled.

“That sounds familiar.”

It did.

Her East Four shifts thinned the same way mine had.

Two a month.

Then one.

Then enough to keep field status.

Her locker stayed.

Dera refused to clear it.

“Until I need it.”

Same line.

Apparently Dera had limited material.

One afternoon I stopped by and saw Nessa's red cord tied to a peg inside the locker.

Not the original. Those things multiplied across our life like weeds.

For a second I remembered the young pressure hand scraping oxide beside me and stealing my potato.

Then Nessa came through the station door carrying district slates.

Dera said, “I need a transfer Friday.”

“No.”

“Make it possible.”

“Find another district.”

Both women looked happy.

Not young.

Not symbols.

Working.

I loved her then.

I had loved her before.

Those are not the same sentence.

You keep loving a person by letting the person inside the love change.

First-life Greg learned too much of that through loss.

Second-life Greg was learning it through calendars.

Better method.

I recommend it.
