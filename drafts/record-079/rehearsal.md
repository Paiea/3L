## RECORD 079

## THE HOUSEHOLD

Nessa and I nearly started a fight over onions.

That was not the real problem.

It rarely is.

I had forgotten to buy them.

Twice.

She had asked Sunday.

Then Tuesday.

Wednesday she came home late from regional scheduling, opened the pantry, and stared at the empty basket where onions should have been.

Our son was under the table doing something with two wooden animals that apparently required whispering.

Our daughter sat at the table separating her dinner into foods she currently respected and foods she considered suspicious.

I was reviewing contractor bids.

This was my first mistake.

“Greg.”

Dangerous tone.

“Yes?”

“Onions.”

I looked at the basket.

Ah.

“I forgot.”

“I know.”

“I can get them tomorrow.”

“That was what you said Tuesday.”

Fair.

“I had the district review.”

She stood very still.

Wrong sentence.

I knew it before the sound finished leaving my mouth.

Not because the district review was unimportant.

Because she also had work.

Because one of the twins had needed a healer visit Monday.

Because she had moved her schedule Tuesday when my licensing session ran late.

Because dinner had not assembled itself while I considered regional infrastructure.

Knowledge does not automatically become behavior.

“Say that again,” she said.

“No.”

“Growth.”

I closed the bid packet.

Our daughter looked between us.

“Papa forgot onions.”

“Yes,” Nessa said.

I had no allies.

“Okay,” I said. “What is this actually about?”

Nessa laughed once.

Not happy.

“Of course you ask now.”

That was worse.

I waited.

She put her bag down.

“Your mother had a healer visit Monday.”

“I went.”

“Yes.”

“Your point?”

“Who moved dinner?”

I thought.

She had.

“Tuesday you had licensing until late.”

“Yes.”

“Who changed bedtime because you were not here?”

She had.

“Who remembered your daughter needs the blue cup at breakfast this week or she becomes a constitutional crisis?”

Our daughter looked offended.

“Blue cup.”

“Yes,” Nessa said. “Exactly.”

“Who noticed we were almost out of washing soap?”

She had.

“Who noticed your son’s boot split?”

She had.

“Who noticed we had no onions?”

She had.

There it was.

Not onions.

Household monitoring.

The invisible work of noticing what would become a problem before it became one.

I hated that because I had spent years professionally arguing that invisible monitoring was infrastructure.

Then came home and treated it like Nessa’s personality.

Fuck.

“I have been making you carry the control room.”

She looked at me.

“That is an annoyingly good sentence.”

“Sorry.”

“Do not get rewarded for naming the problem.”

“Fair.”

Our son crawled out from under the table.

He held up one wooden animal.

“Horse sick.”

I looked at it.

“What happened?”

“He ate floor.”

That sounded serious.

Nessa closed her eyes.

I started laughing.

She tried not to.

Failed.

The fight did not disappear.

It changed temperature.

That mattered.

We finished dinner without onions.

Civilization continued.

After the twins were asleep, or at least contained in the room where sleep was legally expected, Nessa and I did what we always did when life became too complicated.

Made a ledger.

Not money this time.

Work.

Household.

Children.

Family care.

Travel.

Food.

Repairs.

Appointments.

Bills.

Social obligations.

The category SOCIAL OBLIGATIONS made me groan.

Nessa kept it.

We wrote not only tasks.

Owners.

That word mattered.

Not who physically did every action.

Who was responsible for noticing it needed doing.

Groceries.

Me.

Meal planning.

Shared.

Laundry.

Me during Nessa’s planning season, her during my heavy travel months.

Children’s clothing sizes.

Shared, because apparently they changed without filing notice.

Healer calendar.

Shared.

School and care arrangements when work overlapped.

Whoever created the overlap had first responsibility for solving it.

That rule prevented several future murders.

My parents’ repair needs.

Me.

Nessa’s family visits.

Nessa primary, me backup.

Household bills.

Nessa was still better.

I objected.

She said, “Then learn.”

Cruel.

I learned.

Badly.

The first month I paid rent twice.

Landlord returned one payment.

Eventually.

Nessa enjoyed that too much.

The system did not make chores equal.

Equal is a dangerous word.

Some work took minutes.

Some burned whole days.

Some carried emotional weight no schedule measured.

One child could be technically asleep and still consume an entire evening because every noise from the room made one of us look toward the door.

A healer appointment could take an hour and occupy your mind for three days beforehand.

When my father had a bad week, I did more family care and less household work.

When Nessa’s regional calendar entered winter planning, I carried more home logistics.

When I traveled, she absorbed things I could not.

Then I owed recovery time when I returned.

Not debt exactly.

Load transfer.

We got better at saying it before resentment.

Not always.

One month I accepted three regional jobs without checking our shared calendar.

Nessa found out when two overlapped with her South Fen planning week.

“You cannot do that.”

“They are my contracts.”

Wrong sentence again.

I knew as soon as it left.

She stared.

I rubbed my face.

“Work contracts are mine. Calendar impact is ours.”

“Better.”

“I hate you.”

“No you don’t.”

“No.”

We made another rule.

Any job requiring overnight travel got discussed before acceptance unless emergency.

Not permission.

Impact review.

Same for her regional travel.

The language mattered because adults can accidentally recreate parent-child authority inside partnerships if they are not careful.

Neither of us wanted that.

We wanted information before impact.

We also protected one evening a week.

No work.

Already had that rule.

We had violated it slowly.

First one urgent call.

Then one planning sheet.

Then I answered correspondence during dinner because technically I was not leaving the apartment.

Nessa caught me.

“That is work.”

“It is a letter.”

“About pressure systems.”

“Yes.”

“Work.”

Cruel definitions.

We moved the letters.

The twins enforced this policy more aggressively than Nessa.

My son once took a folded work order out of my hand and ran.

My daughter joined him without knowing why.

I caught neither immediately because I was laughing too hard.

The document came back with one corner wet.

No investigation identified the source.

The hardest part was not scheduling.

It was accepting that home could legitimately constrain work.

First-life Greg had treated work as the fixed object.

Everything else bent.

Friends.

Family.

Meals.

Sleep.

Women.

Relationships.

Even grief sometimes.

Important contracts felt morally superior because lives could depend on them.

Sometimes true.

Sometimes convenient.

First Life had never required me to compare an expedition to a child’s fever.

Never required me to tell someone important no because Nessa had already carried three nights alone.

Never required me to leave professional opportunity on the table because four people had made a promise about dinner.

I had never married.

Never had children.

Not because nobody wanted me.

That would have been a comforting lie.

There had been women.

Some casual.

Some not.

Some relationships I cared about more than I admitted at the time.

But I always remained arranged for departure.

A bag partly packed.

A contract possible.

My usefulness waiting somewhere else.

I could love somebody and still preserve the right to leave whenever work called.

That was not the same thing as building a household.

Second Life removed the escape hatch by making me stop wanting it.

Which led to another strange discovery.

Once I was married, settled, and genuinely not looking, women became more interested in me.

Not all women.

Enough that I noticed the pattern.

This seemed rude.

At nineteen I had spent considerable energy trying to look interesting.

By my thirties I wore practical boots, complained about chairs, left dinners early because somebody had to be home, and occasionally had dried food on my sleeve that did not belong to me.

Apparently this was powerful magic.

Part of it was reputation.

I was known by then.

Competent.

Established.

People mistook certainty for mystery because I no longer explained myself to every room.

Part of it was simpler.

I was not reaching.

Not auditioning.

Not trying to turn every attractive woman into evidence that I was attractive.

Being set inside yourself changes the way people meet you.

And part of it, I eventually realized, was Nessa.

Not literally.

Her place.

The life around me.

A woman at a regional dinner once found reasons to remain after everyone else had left.

Nothing dramatic.

She moved her chair closer while we discussed contract staffing.

Touched my forearm twice.

Asked whether Nessa traveled often.

Then asked whether I ever got tired of being “so settled.”

There are questions whose wording contains the invitation.

I knew that much in both lives.

“No,” I said.

She smiled like I had misunderstood.

I had not.

I went home.

Nessa was awake at the table finishing a schedule.

I told her.

Not as confession.

Mostly because the exchange had been interesting.

Nessa listened.

Then said, “She wants my chair.”

“What chair?”

She stared at me.

I was genuinely confused.

“My chair, idiot.”

“Oh.”

“You really are old.”

“Explain.”

“She sees you now.”

“I was visible before marriage.”

“Debatable.”

“Nessa.”

“She sees a man who knows where he is going home. Stable work. Reputation. Children you talk about when nobody asked. A wife you clearly like.”

“I clearly like you?”

“Do not fish.”

“Sorry.”

Nessa tapped her pen against the table.

“Some people see the finished house and think they want to move in.”

That stayed with me.

The finished house.

Except it was not finished.

It leaked occasionally.

Forgot onions.

Paid rent twice.

Argued about calendars.

Had children who treated clean walls as invitations.

The thing being admired from outside was partly the product of work nobody outside could see.

Nessa’s work too.

Especially Nessa’s.

People did not only want me.

Sometimes they wanted Nessa’s place.

That made the attention less flattering in a useful way.

Not insulting.

Some of those women probably did like me.

I was not going to pretend otherwise so I could become morally pure in retrospect.

Attention felt good.

Being wanted felt good.

I remained human across reincarnations.

But First-Life Greg had often treated wanting and opportunity as reasons enough to move closer.

Second-life Greg could feel the little lift of being noticed and then go home because home was not the thing preventing life.

Home was part of the life.

The distinction was enormous.

Nessa was not especially jealous about most of it.

That annoyed me slightly because vanity is stupid.

She trusted me.

More dangerously, she knew me.

Once, after watching a contractor flirt with me through an entire reception, she said, “You’re enjoying that.”

“I am being polite.”

“You are enjoying being polite.”

I considered lying.

“No.”

“Liar.”

“Fine. A little.”

“Good.”

That surprised me.

She shrugged.

“I married you. I did not blind everyone else.”

Then she stole the last sweet from my plate.

Marriage contained complexity.

So did fidelity.

It was not the absence of alternatives.

That would have been easy.

It was knowing alternatives existed and still understanding what they were alternatives to.

The household improved.

Not became perfect.

That word should be illegal around shared lives.

The onions appeared more reliably.

I learned which market stall had good ones late in the day.

Nessa stopped carrying every appointment in her head.

The twins changed faster than our systems did.

Our son went through a period where every pocket contained something.

Stone.

String.

A screw I hoped had not come from our house.

Our daughter developed strong opinions about which stories belonged to whom and corrected me if I assigned one incorrectly.

They were twins.

They were not the same child twice.

This sounds obvious until you are exhausted enough to call one by the other’s name and receive two different forms of outrage.

Fatherhood was harder than I expected.

Also better.

I did not say that at the time.

At the time I said it was loud.

Expensive.

Sticky.

Bad for sleep.

Bad for privacy.

Bad for any object placed lower than shoulder height.

I complained constantly.

Then remembered every stupid detail decades later.

Ithar noticed.

“You speak of them differently.”

I looked at him.

“Differently than what?”

“Than the regulators.”

“High praise for the children.”

“You remember the contents of pockets.”

“Yes.”

“You forgot the name of a surveyor who spent nine days beneath Vey with you.”

“That is different.”

“It is.”

I disliked agreement more than argument sometimes.

The conversation with Arcrutus returned to me often during those years.

He had called Aren a project.

A weapon he had tried to build.

I had thought he was lying about himself.

Not about the pressure.

He had pushed Aren hard.

Aren himself had told that story.

But Arcrutus remembered his failures with more fidelity than his restraint.

Hard on everyone.

Hardest on himself.

Then he had said something better over lunch while I was still half inside a transfer housing.

A child could be yours to protect completely and yours to control only temporarily.

I understood the sentence more every year.

My twins required control when they were small.

Stop meant stop near a road.

Hot meant no.

Give me whatever is in your mouth was not a democratic process.

But the job was not to become more efficient at controlling them.

The job was to keep moving the boundary as they became capable.

That was harder.

Aren’s old sentence belonged here too.

Knowing was not owning.

Parenthood involved knowing someone with terrifying intimacy while slowly accepting that the person remained theirs.

Not yours.

The phrase changed meaning once I had children.

I never told Aren that.

Different life.

Different relationship.

Some things reincarnation did not return just because I remembered them.

Nessa and I eventually started talking about another child.

Not immediately.

We were not insane.

Probably.

But the idea appeared.

First as a joke when the twins had one unusually easy week.

Then again months later without the joke.

We had survived infancy.

Mostly.

The apartment already felt too small.

Money would become tighter.

Sleep would become theoretical again.

And neither of us said no quickly enough.

That was how I knew.

I had not merely endured fatherhood.

I wanted more of that life.

First-life Greg would not have understood the sentence.

He would have understood wanting more capability.

More reach.

More difficult work.

A higher room.

A stronger team.

Second-life Greg wanted another chair at a crowded table.

That may have been the larger transformation.

Years later, people would know me for larger decisions.

Contracts.

Standards.

Failures.

The Line.

Nobody would know I had once required a written system to remember onions.

Nobody would care that my son collected pocket garbage or my daughter had assigned ownership to bedtime stories.

History is reasonable that way.

It cannot keep everything.

But if you want to understand why I became different from the man who reached Nhal in the first life, the onions belong in the record.

So do the pockets.

So does Nessa’s chair.

Annoyingly.
