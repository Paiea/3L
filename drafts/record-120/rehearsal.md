## RECORD 120

## THE BASELINE

Kelan wanted more money after session three.

Good.

Not more training.

Money.

Better.

“How much?”

He named a number.

Too high.

I said so.

He shrugged.

“You’re Named.”

“That is not an argument.”

“It is if you have money.”

“I do not have First-Life money.”

“What?”

“Nothing.”

He stared.

Bad.

I corrected.

“I can pay this.”

Lower number.

He countered.

We negotiated.

Tracy would have been proud.

Probably.

Do not make old Tracy.

Current Tracy was proud.

She said so later.

“Good.”

“Stop.”

“No.”

Kelan agreed to eight more sessions.

Not because he had discovered his calling.

Because the third session made him better at spear.

That mattered.

Relational support only interests people if it solves a real problem.

His problem was simple.

He lost structure when partners changed tempo.

In solo drills he looked good.

In paired movement his reinforcement timing became greedy.

Too much self.

Not enough shared space.

Normal.

Combat training rewards personal certainty.

Support requires something uglier.

Attention.

Kelan hated attention.

Not in life.

In magic.

He did not like another body becoming part of the calculation.

“Why should I care where his knee is?”

“Because if you reinforce through his bad angle, you make both of you worse.”

“He should fix his knee.”

“Eventually.”

“So tell him.”

“I did.”

“Then why am I responsible?”

Good question.

“You’re not.”

He frowned.

“That sounds like a trick.”

“It isn’t.”

“Then?”

“You are responsible for what your magic touches.”

That landed.

Not philosophically.

Mechanically.

If Kelan extended reinforcement into a paired stance, the other fighter’s mistakes entered his system too.

No moral lesson required.

Just load.

The first weeks were ugly.

He overcorrected.

Too much Barrier.

Too much trying.

Support became another thing to dominate.

Bad.

Familiar.

I made him stop.

“Why?”

“Because you are trying to win at helping.”

He stared.

“That is stupid.”

“Yes.”

“Then why did you say it?”

“Because you are.”

He hated me.

Useful.

We changed drills.

No magic first.

Paired stance.

Movement.

Weight.

One person closes eyes.

Other guides.

Kelan complained.

“This is children’s training.”

“Yes.”

“I’m twenty-two.”

“Congratulations.”

“Fuck you.”

“Current data.”

Wrong joke.

Not his.

He looked confused.

Good.

Do not import.

I corrected.

“Walk.”

He walked.

Slowly.

Learned.

Not fast.

Good.

I did not want fast.

That was new.

First-Life Greg learned support late because he had already built habits around individual execution.

Kelan had fewer habits.

Still enough.

I wanted to preserve what was useful and keep the channels broad before combat specialization hardened them.

That meant patience.

I hated patience.

Tracy enjoyed that.

She watched one session from the wall.

Afterward:

“You are making him slower.”

“Yes.”

“On purpose.”

“Yes.”

“He hates you.”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

“Stop.”

“No.”

Kelan heard.

“Why does she keep saying good?”

“Disease.”

Tracy glared.

Current joke.

Good.

Month two.

Kelan stopped fighting the exercises.

Not liked.

Stopped fighting.

Progress.

He could extend a small reinforcement line into a partner’s lead leg without collapsing his own stance.

Useful.

Then into shoulder.

Then weapon.

Not all at once.

Good.

When he tried all at once, the whole thing went bad.

Also good.

Failure early is cheap.

Tracy would say that.

I did.

She stared from the wall.

“Did you just steal my line?”

“No.”

“You did.”

“Public principle.”

“Thief.”

Kelan looked between us.

“Are you two married?”

“No,” both of us said.

Too fast.

He laughed for five minutes.

Bad training session.

Month three.

Kelan asked the first question that mattered.

“Am I becoming support?”

I did not answer immediately.

He noticed.

“Greg.”

“Maybe.”

“I’m a spear.”

“Yes.”

“I like being a spear.”

“Good.”

“Stop.”

Interesting.

My influence.

Bad.

He continued.

“I do not want to stand in the back and put shields on people.”

“Then don’t.”

“That is support.”

“That is one support.”

“What are you?”

“Complicated.”

“Helpful.”

Fair.

I took him to a contract.

Not dangerous.

Warehouse reinforcement.

Three-person team.

One structural support.

One labor lead.

Kelan.

He expected combat.

There was none.

Good.

We spent six hours moving load through temporary bracing so workers could replace a cracked column.

He hated it.

Then liked one part.

A worker on the upper brace misjudged timing.

Kelan caught the stance.

Not physically.

Magic.

Tiny reinforcement.

Prevented a fall.

Nothing dramatic.

The worker said:

“Thanks.”

Kelan looked surprised.

Not by gratitude.

By result.

He had done something to another person and made their body more capable without taking over.

There.

Support.

Not title.

Experience.

On the walk home he asked:

“Could I do that in a fight?”

“Yes.”

“How much?”

“Eventually?”

“Yes.”

“A lot.”

He thought.

Good.

Not destiny.

Interest.

Month four.

He started asking better questions.

Not:

How much mana?

Not:

How strong?

Instead:

How long can I keep this without making my own spear worse?

What happens if the other person changes stance?

Can I reinforce a weapon without taking the whole recoil?

Can I hold somebody’s knee stable while they pivot?

There.

Architecture.

Early.

Cleaner.

I wrote everything down.

That was normal.

Probably.

Tracy saw the notebook.

“What is that?”

“Training notes.”

She opened it.

I should have stopped her.

Did not.

Pages.

Kelan.

Age.

Baseline.

Reinforcement habits.

Channel response.

Load tolerance.

Partner adaptation.

Comparison points.

Too many.

She read one line.

Then another.

Looked at me.

“This is detailed.”

“Yes.”

“More detailed than your normal trainees.”

“I do not have normal trainees.”

“That is not an answer.”

Fair.

“He is unusual.”

“Is he?”

Good question.

Not exceptional.

That was the point.

“Useful baseline.”

Her eyes narrowed.

Same word Ithar would hate later.

I had not learned.

“What are you baselining?”

I looked at the notebook.

Training.

Support conversion.

Development.

All true.

“Whether earlier relational work changes the ceiling.”

There.

Safe truth.

Tracy considered.

“That is actually interesting.”

“Yes.”

“Does he know?”

Good.

Important.

“He knows I’m tracking development.”

“That was not my question.”

Of course.

I sighed.

“He knows I am testing whether support can be taught earlier without damaging combat.”

“Does he know you are comparing him to yourself?”

No.

There.

Not good.

“No.”

“Why?”

Because then the experiment changes.

Bad answer.

Because it sounds insane.

True.

Because I do not know what it means yet.

Better.

“I do not want to make him carry my history.”

Tracy studied me.

“Good answer.”

“Stop.”

“No.”

She put the notebook down.

“Tell him if the comparison starts changing what you ask him to do.”

Fair.

“I will.”

“Promise.”

“I promise.”

That mattered later.

Not because I broke it cleanly.

Because I found ways not to notice when the comparison was changing things.

Humans are excellent at loopholes.

Month six.

Kelan won a paired combat evaluation against someone stronger.

Not by hitting harder.

His partner did.

Kelan stabilized the partner’s back leg through the final exchange.

That let the heavier strike land without the usual recoil.

Both stayed upright.

Opponent did not.

Kelan looked at me afterward.

Wide-eyed.

“You saw that?”

“Yes.”

“What was that?”

I smiled.

“Support.”

He swore.

Long.

Then laughed.

Not because he had become someone else.

Because the thing he had dismissed was useful to the person he already was.

That was how it should begin.

Not conversion.

Addition.

Ithar listened.

Then asked:

“Was that how yours began?”

“No.”

“How?”

“Late.”

“Accidental?”

“Mostly.”

“And his?”

“Earlier.”

“Deliberate.”

“Yes.”

“Cleaner.”

I looked at him.

There.

He had heard the word.

“Yes.”

Ithar’s head lowered slightly.

“Why did cleaner matter?”

Because I had a ruined architecture to compare against.

Because Plan B existed.

Because I knew exactly which shortcuts I would never ask Kelan to take.

Because some part of me wanted to know what happened if Greg did it right from the beginning.

I said:

“Because dirty development wastes people.”

True.

Not complete.

Ithar let it pass.

For then.
