## RECORD 101

## THE PRISONER

Joren Pell did not ask for mercy.

That would have been easier.

He asked for a lawyer.

Reasonable.

The Authority gave him one.

Also reasonable.

I hated both facts.

He remained in secure custody because Oss’s restraint frame had delivered him directly into an office full of witnesses before anybody could quietly decide he was inconvenient.

That had been the point.

Public process.

Records.

Names.

Friction.

I had learned something from Hale’s clearance.

If you want an institution to carry a burden, make the burden visible before anyone can rename it.

Joren was charged.

Not with murdering my family.

That distinction mattered.

Conspiracy.

Obstruction of emergency response.

Unlawful armed action inside a civilian district.

Destruction of public infrastructure.

Several counts related to deaths that occurred on the market approach.

The legal language was cleaner than the street.

Always.

I attended the first hearing on a bad socket day.

That annoyed me enough that I remember it.

Every step pinched high on the right side.

The fitter had told me not to wear the peg more than four hours continuously while the skin settled.

The hearing ran six.

Justice is hard on residual limbs.

Joren entered in restraints.

Not Oss’s.

Authority steel.

He looked smaller.

Most people do indoors.

His lawyer spoke.

The prosecutor spoke.

Nobody sang.

Good.

Hale was not there.

Also good.

Joren saw me.

Held my eyes for a second.

Then looked away.

I wanted him afraid.

He probably was.

Did not help.

The Authority had reopened several pieces of the town inquiry because of the sealed-road capture.

That irritated people.

Old work.

Closed files.

New witnesses.

New questions.

A department that had already produced a conclusion now had to admit there were things it had not known.

Institutions dislike discovering they were incomplete more than discovering they were wrong.

Wrong can be defended.

Incomplete requires more work.

I liked that.

Tracy would have appreciated it.

I did not contact her.

That was harder than the hearing.

She had said do not follow.

So I did not.

For once, absence remained absence.

No food.

No apology delivered through work.

No clever reason to happen to be at her counter.

No “professional question.”

I hated it.

Probably healthy.

Joren asked to speak with me after the second hearing.

His lawyer made the request.

I said no.

Then yes.

Growth was inconsistent.

We met through glass.

Not dramatic glass.

Administrative.

Scratched.

Badly cleaned.

He sat.

I sat.

Peg angled awkwardly because the chair spacing assumed everyone had two ordinary legs.

Another crime.

Joren looked at it.

Then at me.

“Hale did that.”

“Yes.”

“I know.”

“Then why say it?”

He thought.

“I had not seen it.”

Fair.

I looked at his hands.

All fingers.

I disliked that.

Not rational.

“Why did you want to talk?”

Joren leaned back.

“I want to know what you want.”

“From you?”

“Yes.”

“Truth.”

He laughed once.

Not because it was funny.

“Authority wants truth.”

“No. Authority wants admissible truth.”

His expression changed.

Good.

“What do you want?”

“The parts that do not fit paperwork.”

He looked at me.

Then at the guard.

Then back.

“You think I blocked rescue.”

“You did.”

“Yes.”

No defense.

Again.

Hale’s people had this irritating habit.

“What were you blocking?”

“Armed response.”

“From the market.”

“Yes.”

“There were civilians burning uphill.”

“Yes.”

“You knew.”

“Eventually.”

There.

“Eventually.”

“Yes.”

“When?”

He looked at the table.

Not me.

“After the second collapse.”

“How long after the breach?”

“Maybe nine minutes.”

Nine.

Clean number.

Horrible.

“And you stayed.”

“Yes.”

“How long?”

“Too long.”

“How long?”

“Another eleven.”

Twenty minutes.

People lived and died inside smaller amounts.

“Why?”

He finally looked at me.

“Because I thought if I opened the approach, the house guard would come through with the responders.”

“Did they?”

“Some were mixed.”

“So you stayed.”

“Yes.”

“Knowing civilians needed help.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

His jaw tightened.

“Because we had spent six years getting there.”

There.

Not ideology.

Momentum.

Sunk cost.

Revenge as project.

Useful.

Bad.

“Hale ordered you?”

“No.”

That surprised me.

“Who did?”

“No one.”

“You chose.”

“Yes.”

“Would Hale have stopped you?”

“I don’t know.”

“Did he know?”

“Not then.”

That mattered.

I did not like that it mattered.

Joren continued.

“Hale was uphill.”

“I know.”

“How?”

I stared.

He stopped.

Right.

Future report.

The thing nobody understood.

“Did Hale tell you about the sealed report?”

“No.”

Joren looked unsettled.

Good.

I asked:

“Why were you with him?”

He rubbed his wrists against the restraint edge.

Not escape.

Habit.

“My brother was one of the four who surrendered.”

Hale’s grievance.

Shared.

Not employee.

Not thug.

Joren had his own dead.

Of course.

The world refused to simplify.

“He died there.”

“Yes.”

“Your brother.”

“Yes.”

“What was his name?”

“Pell.”

“I know your family name.”

“No.”

He looked at me.

“His name was Pell.”

I stared.

“Just Pell?”

“Everyone called him Pell.”

Good.

A person reduced to a family name while his brother carried the same one.

Memory did stupid things.

“What did the man in the hill house do?”

Joren told me.

Not song.

Not legal summary.

His version.

Pell surrendered.

Hands visible.

Weapon down.

The hill man’s soldiers killed him anyway because prisoners complicated retreat.

Then bribery.

Then records changed.

Then one investigation disappeared.

Then another.

Hale lost siblings.

Joren lost Pell.

Others lost people too.

Real grievance.

Again.

I leaned back.

“My children had nothing to do with Pell.”

“I know.”

“My wife.”

“I know.”

“You still blocked the road.”

“Yes.”

“Would you do it again?”

He did not answer immediately.

Good.

Hale always had.

“Yes.”

There it was.

I felt something settle.

Not peace.

Category.

“Why?”

“Because if I say no now, I am pretending I was someone else then.”

That was not what I expected.

He continued.

“I should have opened the road.”

I waited.

“I know that.”

“Then no.”

“What?”

“You can say no.”

He looked at me.

“You asked if I would do it again.”

“Yes.”

“I would still go after them.”

Not the same.

Good.

“But the road?”

Long silence.

“No.”

There.

Different from Hale.

Small.

Important.

I did not know what to do with it.

Revenge prefers stable enemies.

People change shape.

Joren asked:

“Does that save me?”

“No.”

He nodded.

Fair.

“Are you going to kill me?”

Good question.

I had imagined it.

Of course.

Many times.

Cell door.

Knife.

No song.

No witnesses.

Simple.

Then what?

Another dead man.

My children unchanged.

My wife unchanged.

Hale still alive.

And Joren was already inside a process that might actually punish him.

Not enough.

Maybe.

But real.

“I don’t know.”

That surprised him.

It surprised me too.

“Thought you came for all of us.”

“I did.”

“Did?”

“Do.”

“Then?”

I looked through the scratched glass.

“I am trying to determine what that means.”

He nodded slowly.

“You think Hale knows?”

“Yes.”

That answer came easily.

Joren laughed.

I did not.

“He doesn’t.”

I looked at him.

“He understands revenge better than anyone I know.”

“That is not the same as knowing what comes after.”

Interesting.

“You think you do?”

“No.”

Good answer.

We sat.

Two men connected by people who were dead because other men had decided something mattered enough.

Ugly structure.

Joren said:

“Hale thinks paying the price means accepting whoever comes next.”

“Yes.”

“That is why he left you alive.”

“Partly.”

“He told us.”

My body went still.

“What?”

“After.”

“What did he say?”

Joren looked at the guard again.

Then me.

“He said you were right.”

That sounded like him.

“He said you almost killed him.”

Also.

“He was angry?”

“No.”

Of course not.

“Scared?”

Joren hesitated.

That mattered.

“Yes.”

There.

First time someone else said it.

“Hale was scared of me.”

“Of what you knew.”

Not me.

Better.

“He said your body was weak and your decisions were not.”

I almost smiled.

Did not.

“He said if you survived, you would come back differently.”

Useful.

“Hence the leg.”

“Yes.”

“Did you agree?”

Joren looked at the peg.

“No.”

That surprised me.

“You thought he should kill me.”

“Yes.”

Honest.

“Why?”

“Because taking your leg and leaving you angry seemed stupid.”

I laughed.

Could not help it.

Joren almost smiled.

Then stopped.

Different room.

Different lives.

“What does Hale think now?”

“I don’t know.”

“You still talk?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Authority condition.”

“Would you if allowed?”

He thought.

“Yes.”

There.

Not redemption.

Loyalty remained.

People are inconvenient.

The hearings continued.

Joren was convicted on several counts.

Not all.

Enough.

Long sentence.

Restitution that would never match the dead.

Professional bans.

No field license during custody and likely none after.

The market deaths remained contested in legal causation.

That sentence made me want to break something.

I did not.

Growth.

At sentencing, Joren looked at me.

No apology.

I appreciated that.

Apologies had become cheap.

He served time.

Years.

I did not kill him.

That became the first person from the town operation I consciously chose not to kill.

Not forgive.

Not absolve.

Not forget.

Not kill.

Different.

Ithar watched me.

“You call that revenge?”

“Yes.”

“You allowed another institution to decide his punishment.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because punishment existed.”

“That was enough?”

“No.”

“Then why?”

I thought about the road.

Twenty minutes.

Pell.

Joren’s no.

The scratched glass.

“Because killing him would have answered a question I was no longer asking.”

Ithar’s eyes narrowed.

“What question?”

“Whether I could.”

The cave stayed quiet.

“That one was becoming easier.”

“And Hale?”

I looked at the peg.

“Hale was different.”

“Because he took your leg.”

“No.”

That mattered.

“Because he still believed he would make the same choice.”

Ithar waited.

So did I.

Then:

“And because I still wanted him dead.”

Honest.

“What did Tracy think of Joren?”

I looked at him.

“She found out before I told her.”

“How?”

I smiled despite myself.

“Professional gossip.”

That was how Tracy came back.
