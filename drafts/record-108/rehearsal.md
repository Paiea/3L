## RECORD 108

## THE NAME

Peg-Leg Greg began because there were two Gregs.

I wish there were a better story.

There is not.

The other Greg was a carpenter.

Good one.

Worked emergency stabilization crews.

Big shoulders.

Red beard.

Two legs.

Unfortunate.

We were both assigned to a warehouse collapse near South Market.

Not dramatic.

Rain damage.

Bad roof.

Old beams.

Too much grain stored on the upper floor because somebody had discovered greed before structural engineering.

The dispatch board listed:

GREG - SUPPORT

GREG - CARPENTRY

This was apparently insufficient.

People kept shouting Greg.

Both of us kept answering.

At one point somebody yelled:

“Greg, brace left!”

I braced left.

Wrong Greg.

The carpenter braced right.

Wrong Greg.

Nothing collapsed.

Mostly luck.

The field dispatcher became angry.

Reasonable.

She took a piece of chalk.

Looked at the carpenter.

“Beard Greg.”

He nodded.

Then looked at me.

Long pause.

I knew.

“No.”

She kept looking.

“No.”

She wrote:

PEG GREG.

I stared.

“That is terrible.”

“Do you have a better identifier?”

“Yes.”

“What?”

“Greg.”

She pointed at Beard Greg.

Problem.

I hated him immediately.

He shrugged.

Not his fault.

Then one of the loaders read the board.

“Peg Greg?”

Another said:

“Peg-Leg Greg.”

There.

Rhythm.

Humanity’s greatest enemy.

The loader laughed.

The dispatcher did not.

Then used it over the field horn.

“Peg-Leg Greg to west brace.”

I considered leaving.

Did not.

People were under the roof.

Annoying.

I went to the west brace.

The name followed.

Not instantly.

Names rarely do.

First it was that crew.

Then another dispatcher who had worked with them.

Then somebody in transport.

Then a Guild clerk wrote P.L. GREG in a scheduling margin.

Then a field captain said:

“You’re Peg-Leg Greg?”

“No.”

He looked at the leg.

“Seems like yes.”

I hated him.

Tracy loved it.

Of course.

Not the name.

My suffering.

She heard it for the first time at a treatment session.

“Peg-Leg Greg.”

“No.”

She smiled.

“Absolutely.”

“No.”

“It rhymes.”

“That is not a defense.”

“It is for names.”

“I have a name.”

“Yes.”

“Greg.”

“Unfortunately there are two.”

“Not everywhere.”

“Enough.”

I glared.

She checked the socket.

“Peg-Leg Greg.”

“Stop.”

“Does it hurt?”

“The name?”

“The limb.”

“Yes.”

“Where?”

Professional.

Fine.

I showed her.

She adjusted the plan.

Then, as I left:

“See you Thursday, Peg-Leg Greg.”

I closed the door harder than necessary.

That helped nothing.

The name should have died.

Many field names do.

Temporary.

Local.

Somebody’s joke.

This one stayed because it solved a problem.

That was irritatingly Tracy-like.

There were many Gregs.

There were fewer one-legged support specialists who kept appearing on structural emergencies, ugly field contracts, and increasingly strange Authority reports.

The peg made me visually distinct.

The work made the distinction useful.

Important difference.

Plenty of people had lost limbs.

Nobody became important because wood replaced flesh.

The peg did not make me better.

It made me easier to identify from thirty yards away.

That was all.

Mostly.

The loop complicated things.

Field crews noticed I used the prosthetic strangely.

Not magical peg.

No hidden blessing.

Mechanical contact.

Anchor.

Known surface.

I could place the wooden foot somewhere and build around its limitations because I knew exactly what those limitations were.

A natural leg would have been better.

Faster.

More adaptive.

Less painful.

But I did not have one.

So the peg became part of the system.

People saw that.

Stories simplified.

Of course.

A report from a tunnel stabilization described:

Greg anchored the east load line through his prosthetic stance.

Technically true.

Someone retold it as:

Peg-Leg Greg held the tunnel.

False.

Then:

Peg-Leg Greg held up a tunnel on one leg.

Very false.

Then:

Peg-Leg Greg held up a mountain.

Humanity should not be allowed oral tradition.

I complained to Tracy.

She ate soup.

“You are upset people exaggerated.”

“Yes.”

“You told me an ancient dragon knows your name.”

“Different problem.”

“Seems similar.”

“No.”

“Mm.”

She kept eating.

I hated mm.

The first time Hale called me Peg-Leg Greg, I nearly hit him.

Not because of the insult.

Because he was smiling.

Barely.

We crossed at an Authority office.

Different assignments.

No crisis.

He looked at the dispatch slate.

Then at me.

“Peg-Leg Greg.”

“Do not.”

“I did not name you.”

“You contributed.”

He looked at the leg.

Fair.

“Apparently.”

“Fuck you.”

“Yes.”

Same answer pattern.

Annoying.

He looked at the slate again.

“It suits.”

“Why?”

I regretted asking.

Hale thought.

Not joking now.

“Because people know which Greg they mean.”

That was all.

No poetry.

No destiny.

Logistics.

I hated that more because he was correct.

Years later the Authority would make the name official.

Not then.

Then it was only field language.

A nickname.

Something written in chalk because two men answered at once.

But names accumulate work.

Every contract added something.

Every rescue.

Every ugly success.

Every fight.

Every rumor.

Every time someone said Peg-Leg Greg and another person knew who they meant, the name became less joke and more designation.

Ithar’s mouth moved.

“Peg-Leg Greg.”

I looked at him.

“Do not.”

“You climbed a mountain to ask an ancient dragon to alter the future and object to a rhyming name.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“It is stupid.”

“It is memorable.”

“That is worse.”

He seemed amused.

Good.

Ancient dragon entertained by administrative nickname.

Worth the climb.

“When did Authority formalize it?” he asked.

“Later.”

“After what?”

I looked at him.

There was a story there.

Not yet.

“After I became expensive enough that they needed a cleaner way to refer to me.”

Ithar waited.

I sighed.

“And after the quarry.”

His eyes narrowed.

“What happened at the quarry?”

I looked at the peg.

Then at my hands.

“That was the first time people watched me beat someone I had no business beating.”

Not Hale.

Not yet.

But close enough that Hale heard about it.
