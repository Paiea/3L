## RECORD 095

## THE CLEARED

“Hale was cleared.”

Ithar’s eyes narrowed.

“Of killing your family?”

“No.”

“Then the word is doing work.”

“Yes.”

Good dragon.

I learned about the clearance while learning to stand.

Not walk.

Stand.

Different.

The healers saved the knee.

Most of it.

That was the good news.

The bad news was everything below.

Hale had cut cleanly enough that the initial damage was survivable.

Then field conditions had added contamination.

Then transport had added time.

Then swelling.

Then infection risk.

By the time a specialist looked at what remained, the conversation was no longer about putting anything back.

Not my leg.

Not his finger.

The finger had come with me.

This caused administrative confusion.

A healer held the sealed preservation jar up to the light.

“This is not yours.”

“No.”

“Why do you have it?”

“I took it.”

He looked at me.

Then at the missing leg.

Then back at the finger.

“I am going to write ‘evidence.’”

“Fine.”

It was not evidence.

Not exactly.

It became evidence anyway.

The Authority interviewed me on day three.

Two people.

One field investigator.

One legal clerk.

They asked about the town.

Then Hale.

Then the pursuit.

Then the fight.

Then the leg.

I told them.

All of it.

Including the part where I attacked him.

Including the part where he tried to send me away.

Including the part where I nearly killed him.

Including the finger.

The legal clerk stopped writing once.

“Nearly killed him?”

“Yes.”

“What rank were you?”

I told her.

She looked at the investigator.

He did not look back.

Professional.

Good.

“Then Mr. Rusk took your leg.”

“Yes.”

“After the threat had ended?”

“Yes.”

“Deliberately.”

“Yes.”

“To prevent pursuit.”

“And because I took his finger.”

She stopped again.

“He said that?”

“Not exactly.”

“What exactly did he say?”

“My turn.”

Silence.

The investigator finally looked at me.

“That is what he said?”

“Yes.”

He wrote it down.

Good.

Hale did not run.

That mattered later.

He reported to the Authority himself.

Missing finger.

Cut at the throat.

My blood on his field blade.

Probably some of the town still on his coat.

He gave his account.

I never saw the full statement.

I saw enough.

He admitted the town operation.

Admitted knowing civilians were present.

Admitted using force that could propagate beyond the hill compound.

Admitted the leg.

Admitted leaving me alive.

Admitted the finger was mine.

That sentence appeared in one leaked summary.

Apparently even the investigator had asked what the fuck that meant.

Hale explained.

I had taken it.

So he left it.

Simple.

The inquiry lasted months.

My leg healed during it.

Badly.

Then better.

Then differently.

I learned temporary crutches.

Hated them.

Learned a training peg.

Hated that more.

Fell.

Swore.

Fell less.

My left shoulder, already bad, became worse because crutches redistribute suffering rather than remove it.

People visited.

Family who survived.

Friends.

Work people.

Some stayed too long.

Some not long enough.

Nobody knew the correct amount.

Neither did I.

The town funerals happened in pieces because there were too many dead and too few intact places to put everyone.

Nessa had one.

My son had one.

My daughter had one.

Not separate ceremonies.

Separate names.

That mattered.

I remember almost none of what people said.

I remember food afterward.

Too much.

Nobody ate enough.

Then, while I was still learning how much pressure the healing stump could tolerate inside a socket, the Authority released its findings.

Not a declaration of innocence.

That would have been easier to hate.

The report was worse because much of it was true.

The man in the hill compound had arranged killings.

Bribed investigators.

Interfered with magistrates.

Used Authority contacts improperly.

Maintained armed personnel inside a civilian district.

When Hale’s group arrived, those people fought.

Some of the destruction came from them.

Some did not.

Hale escalated anyway.

The report used that word.

Escalated.

Very clean word.

He had pursued a legitimate grievance through illegitimate force.

Another clean sentence.

He had failed to protect civilian life.

Cleaner.

Civilian casualties had been foreseeable.

There.

Almost human.

The Authority censured him.

Suspended him from independent operations.

Revoked two regional privileges.

Required restitution.

Not enough money.

No amount would have been.

Placed restrictions on future deployments.

Reviewed his Named designation.

Then retained it.

That was the part everybody talked about.

Not because the rest disappeared.

Because Hale Rusk remained Hale Rusk.

A-class.

Named.

Useful.

The public phrase became:

CLEARED FOR CONTINUED AUTHORITY SERVICE.

Not cleared of wrongdoing.

Cleared for service.

People shortened it.

Of course.

“Hale cleared.”

“Rusk cleared.”

“Named Ranker cleared after review.”

Language did the washing.

Not conspiracy.

Convenience.

Headlines have limited space.

People have limited attention.

Institutions have work tomorrow.

The town remained dead.

Hale remained useful.

Both facts survived.

I hated that they could.

Ithar interrupted.

“What did the Authority need him for?”

“Everything people need A-class Rankers for.”

“Be specific.”

Fair.

Breach response.

Monster suppression.

High-risk escorts.

Flood failures.

Regional defense.

A-class work was already rare.

Named A-class work rarer.

Hale was very good.

That was the problem.

Three months after his suspension ended, a river wall failed two districts east.

Not the Line.

Ordinary infrastructure.

Ordinary until it failed.

Hale went.

Of course he did.

He held a collapsing section long enough to evacuate a neighborhood.

Pulled people out himself.

One report credited him with twenty-three direct rescues.

Another said seventeen.

Does not matter.

People lived.

There were pictures.

Hale waist-deep in floodwater.

Missing finger visible on one hand.

Blood on his forehead.

Carrying an old woman.

I saw the picture at a prosthetics office.

That detail stayed.

The fitter had gone into the back room to adjust the socket.

I was sitting with my trouser leg pinned up.

A newspaper lay on the chair beside me.

Hale on the front.

Of course.

I read the article twice.

Not because I doubted it.

Because I wanted to.

I wanted the rescues to be propaganda.

They were not.

He had saved those people.

The old woman was real.

The neighborhood was real.

The wall was real.

Hale had been exactly as useful as the Authority claimed.

I hated him more.

That surprised me.

If he had become useless, the world could have agreed with me cleanly.

Monster.

Murderer.

Discard.

Instead the world presented a worse equation.

He had murdered people.

He had saved people.

Neither canceled the other.

Authority did not need cancellation.

It needed deployment.

The fitter came back carrying a new socket liner.

Saw what I was reading.

“That guy.”

“Yes.”

“Hell of a thing.”

I looked at him.

He meant the flood.

Maybe the town.

Maybe both.

“Yeah.”

He adjusted the liner.

“Too tight?”

“Yes.”

He loosened it.

“Now?”

“Better.”

Life continued doing this.

Tiny practical questions while enormous moral ones remained unresolved.

Fit.

Pressure.

Skin.

Balance.

The correct angle of a wooden foot.

Whether Hale Rusk deserved to die.

Then lunch.

The first time I saw Hale after the inquiry was not dramatic.

No confrontation.

He was across a civic square.

Months later.

I had a better peg by then.

Still walked badly when tired.

He was leaving an Authority building.

Uniformed escort.

Not custody.

Work.

He saw me.

Stopped.

The people with him kept walking two steps before noticing.

Hale looked at the peg.

Then at my face.

I looked at his hand.

Still missing the finger.

Good.

He had kept the loss.

He could have replaced it magically enough to be functional.

Maybe not perfectly.

He had not.

That bothered me too.

He crossed the square alone.

Slowly.

Stopped far enough away that neither of us could pretend this was friendly.

“You’re standing.”

“Yes.”

He looked at the peg again.

“How is it?”

“Fuck you.”

He nodded.

Fair.

I held up the newspaper I had folded under my arm.

Not intentionally for him.

Life had become annoyingly symbolic.

“Twenty-three.”

He looked at the headline.

“Seventeen.”

Of course.

“You saved seventeen people.”

“Yes.”

“You killed more than that.”

His face changed.

Not defensive.

Tired.

“Yes.”

“They cleared you.”

“No.”

I laughed.

He waited.

“Your paper says otherwise.”

“They cleared me to work.”

“That distinction make you feel better?”

“No.”

Immediate.

Still him.

I hated how little he protected himself.

“What did you pay?”

He understood.

“Restitution.”

“No.”

“Six months.”

“No.”

“Privileges.”

“No.”

I stepped closer.

“What did you pay?”

Hale looked at me.

Finally.

“The finger.”

I almost hit him.

He saw it.

Did not move.

Then he continued.

“Not enough.”

That stopped me.

He looked toward my leg.

“Neither was that.”

I did not understand.

“Then why did you do it?”

“You almost killed me.”

“So?”

“I was not ready to die.”

There.

Human.

Not philosophy.

Not justice.

Fear.

“I wanted time,” he said.

“For what?”

“To finish what came after.”

“Your revenge was finished.”

“Yes.”

“Then what came after?”

He looked around the square.

People.

Authority clerks.

Carriages.

Somebody selling food.

A child pulling another child by the sleeve.

“Everything.”

I hated him for that answer too.

He had revenge.

Then afterward.

I had not yet understood there could be an afterward.

Hale had.

Maybe badly.

Maybe incompletely.

Enough.

“You said you’d wait.”

“I am.”

“You’re working.”

“Yes.”

“That is not waiting.”

“It can be both.”

Of course he would say that.

I looked at his hand again.

“You know I’m still going to kill you.”

“Yes.”

“Authority won’t protect you.”

“I know.”

“You think they will.”

“No.”

“Then why stay visible?”

Hale frowned slightly.

As if the answer were obvious.

“Because I live here.”

That was worse than hiding.

He had not become a fugitive.

He had not vanished into some villain’s fortress.

He bought food.

Took contracts.

Reported to Authority offices.

Saved people.

Slept somewhere.

Probably had friends.

Maybe family.

He had returned to ordinary life after murdering mine.

I could not forgive that.

I also could not dismiss it.

Hale looked at the peg one last time.

“If you come again, come ready.”

There was no swagger in it.

I believed him.

He started to leave.

I said his name.

He stopped.

“Do you regret my family?”

“Yes.”

“Would you still do it?”

Long pause.

Same question.

Same man.

“Yes.”

I nodded.

Good.

Not good.

Useful.

The answer had not changed.

Neither had mine.

“I’ll see you again.”

“Yes.”

He walked away.

Ithar’s tail moved once across the stone.

“You did not attack him.”

“No.”

“Why?”

I looked at the peg.

Then at my hands.

Because the first attempt had already answered the important question.

Anger was enough to find him.

Knowledge was enough to nearly kill him.

Neither was enough to finish.

“I had one leg,” I said.

“That had not stopped you before.”

“No.”

“What stopped you?”

I looked at Ithar.

“For once?”

“Yes.”

“I learned.”

The cave was quiet.

Then Ithar asked the question I had avoided.

“What did you do next?”

I thought about First-Life Tracy.

About the marsh.

The yard.

The washhouse.

Marrin’s.

Arcrutus.

All the things I once built outward.

Then about the woman who did not know me in this life.

“I went looking for a clerk.”
