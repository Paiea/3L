## RECORD 088

## THE STEP BEFORE

She bought a washhouse.

That was the simple answer.

The real answer was worse.

The building sat near the southern canal where freight roads crossed the old industrial quarter.

Three stories.

Ugly brick.

Good foundation.

Excellent water access.

Terrible windows.

Former dye works.

Then laundry.

Then storage.

Then mostly pigeons.

Tracy bought it anyway.

Not alone.

With my money too.

Technically ours.

She had enough for half.

Of course she did.

By then her half of the marsh and transport profits had become real capital.

Not theoretical wealth.

Not future payments.

Money.

She liked that.

Very much.

I arrived after the purchase because apparently “run it” meant I surrendered the right to complain about sequence.

Tracy stood in the main floor with dust on her coat and three contractors arguing behind her.

I looked around.

“What did you buy?”

“A building.”

“I can see that.”

“Then why ask?”

I hated when she used me against me.

“What problem?”

“Processing.”

“We already have processing.”

“We have invoices.”

“Same thing.”

“No.”

She pointed toward a table.

Glassroot lay there in three bundles.

Fresh.

Yesterday.

Three days old.

Even I could see the difference.

The resin in the oldest roots had clouded.

Not ruined.

Lower grade.

Lower price.

“How much do we lose?”

She told me.

I stopped.

“Every load?”

“Not every.”

“How many?”

“Enough.”

“Why?”

“Delay.”

“We deliver on time.”

“To the processor.”

“Yes.”

“They process when they have capacity.”

Ah.

Tracy smiled without happiness.

“We pay for transport quickly enough to get our product somewhere it can wait.”

Annoying.

“Can we negotiate priority?”

“We did.”

“And?”

“We pay more to wait less.”

“Reasonable.”

“Until volume grows.”

I looked at the bundles.

The marsh operation had grown.

So had everyone else using the processor.

Busy season turned scheduling into combat.

Sometimes our root waited.

Sometimes another client paid more.

Sometimes equipment went down.

We had reduced poison losses enough that processing loss had become visible.

That was how systems worked.

Fix one bottleneck.

Congratulations.

Meet the next one.

“So we process ourselves.”

“Yes.”

“Here.”

“Yes.”

“This place is enormous.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because processing is not the whole idea.”

Of course not.

She walked.

I followed.

Ground floor.

Wash lines.

Drainage.

Room for cutting tables.

A section where temperature could be controlled cheaply because the old dye vats had thick stone surrounds.

Back loading door large enough for two wagons.

Upstairs storage.

Dry.

Separate rooms.

One could become medical stock.

Another finished product.

Another equipment.

The top floor was mostly empty.

I looked around.

“What is that for?”

“Later.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the honest answer.”

She kept walking.

Outside, the canal ran behind the property.

Not pretty.

Useful.

A secondary freight route.

The road frontage connected to our yard without crossing the central market.

I started understanding.

“You bought location.”

“I bought time.”

Better.

She pointed toward the waterworks connection.

“Clean water is the expensive part of initial processing.”

“We already haul it to the marsh.”

“Yes.”

“Here?”

“Municipal supply plus cistern.”

“Backup?”

“Two.”

I looked at her.

She smiled.

“See?”

“What?”

“You’re learning.”

Cruel.

She had not only bought the building.

She had hired two experienced processors.

Kept one contractor from the old dye works who understood the drainage better than anyone else.

Bought cutting equipment.

Bought one pump.

Then another pump.

I objected to the second.

She ignored me.

Negotiated a maintenance contract before either had run.

Secured two resin buyers before first production.

One primary.

One lower-price secondary buyer who would take imperfect batches rather than forcing us to hold them.

I stared at the contract.

“You already negotiated an outlet for failure.”

“Yes.”

“That seems pessimistic.”

“It is profitable.”

Fair.

Then I found the food line.

“What is this?”

“Kitchen.”

“Why does a washhouse need a kitchen?”

“People.”

“People can bring food.”

She looked at me.

That look.

“What?”

“You have learned nothing.”

“I bring food.”

“For yourself.”

“And you.”

“Because I trained you.”

I looked at the kitchen allocation again.

Not large.

Enough for a cook during full shifts.

“Is this operationally necessary?”

“No.”

I waited.

“Mostly.”

There.

“What does mostly mean?”

“People work better when they eat.”

“True.”

“They leave less.”

“Maybe.”

“They stop taking forty-minute trips into the market because someone forgot lunch.”

Ah.

“And I like food.”

There it was.

“Finally.”

“I never hid that.”

She had not.

The washhouse opened six weeks later.

Nothing worked.

Not nothing.

Enough failed to feel insulting.

The first pump clogged.

The drainage backed up in one corner.

One cutting table warped.

A processor quit after receiving a better offer from the company we had stopped using.

The second buyer rejected a batch because our drying protocol produced uneven resin.

Tracy did not speak to me for most of one afternoon.

Not because she was angry at me.

Because she was calculating.

I waited.

Growth.

Eventually she said, “We expanded too much at once.”

I looked at her.

That admission mattered.

Tracy did not enjoy being wrong.

She enjoyed being seen as right even less.

Mostly she wanted the money to stop leaking.

“What do we cut?”

“Nothing.”

I frowned.

“That sounds unlike you.”

“We sequence.”

There.

Her favorite violence.

She shut one processing line.

Reduced intake.

Moved all drying through one room until humidity control was reliable.

Contracted overflow back to the old processor.

At a worse rate than before.

I enjoyed that.

Quietly.

She caught me.

“Say it.”

“No.”

“Say it.”

“We are paying somebody else again.”

“Fuck you.”

I smiled.

Then helped.

Three months later, the washhouse worked.

Not perfectly.

Reliably.

Fresh root came from the marsh.

Our wagons moved it.

Our people washed it.

Our processors graded it.

Good batches went to the premium buyer.

Imperfect went to the secondary.

Waste became compost for a nearby grower who paid almost nothing but removed it for us.

That counted.

Storage reduced rush sales.

Medical stock moved into one upstairs room.

Field gear into another.

The kitchen became unexpectedly important.

Not because of productivity studies.

Because people liked eating there.

Drivers came early.

Processors stayed for meals.

Brann started holding crew meetings over breakfast because everyone was already present.

I began using one upstairs room for planning.

Tracy complained that I had colonized her building.

“Our building.”

“Your meetings.”

“Our profit.”

“My kitchen.”

“Ah.”

She pointed a spoon at me.

“Do not.”

I stopped.

The first year the washhouse barely beat outsourcing after capital cost.

Tracy hated that.

The second year it did.

The third year we stopped measuring it only against processing.

That was the dangerous part.

It reduced transport delay.

Reduced product loss.

Reduced storage fees.

Improved medical inventory.

Gave us meeting space.

Gave crews food.

Gave us somewhere to stage equipment.

Made the yard more useful.

Made the marsh easier to scale.

No single line explained its value.

The system did.

Ithar shifted.

“You bought infrastructure.”

“Eventually.”

“Because of poison.”

“Originally.”

“And food.”

“Tracy.”

“Of course.”

He considered that.

“Did you intend to build an organization?”

“No.”

“An enterprise?”

“No.”

“A network?”

“No.”

“What did you intend?”

I thought about it.

“At first?”

“Yes.”

“Stop paying house money for poison.”

Ithar’s mouth moved.

Not quite a smile.

“And this became buildings.”

“Yes.”

“Wagons.”

“Yes.”

“Workers.”

“Yes.”

“Medical capacity.”

“Yes.”

“Contracts.”

“Yes.”

“Property.”

“Yes.”

He waited.

I sighed.

“It got away from us.”

“Did it?”

Good question.

Years later people would describe the whole thing as if we had planned it.

Vertical integration.

Operating network.

Private logistics.

Cross-Guild contracting.

Property consolidation.

One particularly dramatic writer used the word syndicate.

Tracy liked that one.

Not because it sounded dangerous.

Because the article estimated our assets too low.

She wrote a correction in the margin.

Never sent it.

I asked why.

“Why help them?”

That was Tracy too.

The truth was less impressive.

Every piece had started with a recurring irritation.

Why are we buying five antidotes?

Why do we lose money waiting?

Why are we paying rush transport?

Why does the wagon break after the noise starts?

Why are we paying storage?

Why is the root sitting?

Why is everyone leaving to find lunch?

Why are we renting the room we use every week?

The organization grew by asking one question repeatedly:

What predictable problem are we paying for too late?

Tracy had learned it from poison.

I learned it from Tracy.

Then we applied it to everything.

That was when people started getting nervous.
