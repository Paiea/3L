## RECORD 045

## THE POCKET

On the twelfth day, Orla told me I was doing support wrong.

This was useful because she waited until after breakfast.

People are more receptive to criticism after oats.

Usually.

We had camped beside an abandoned toll station where the roof had failed but one wall remained upright enough to block the wind.

Senn wanted measurements from an old retaining line two miles north.

Calder wanted the last of the coffee.

Taris wanted to know whether mold on hard cheese counted as food loss if you cut around it.

The route hand, whose name remains unfairly absent from my memory, wanted all of us to stop talking.

Orla wanted me to sit still.

"I am sitting."

"For more than a minute."

"That was not specified."

She pointed her spoon at me.

"Yesterday you crossed the road eleven times during one problem."

"You counted?"

"I got bored."

Calder looked up from the coffee pot.

"It was eleven?"

"Twelve if you count when he went back for the glove."

"The glove was equipment."

"The glove was on the ground."

"Equipment frequently is."

Orla ate another spoonful.

"You are doing support wrong."

There it was.

I looked at Calder.

"Professional opinion?"

He poured the last coffee into his cup.

"I am staying out of this."

Coward.

"How?" I asked.

Orla set the bowl down.

"You move too much."

"Movement is generally how I get places."

"Support should not need to get places that often."

"People keep having problems in different places."

"Then stop treating every problem like it needs you standing beside it."

That was irritating because it sounded related to the road.

I did not say that.

Instead I said, "Distance matters."

"Yes."

"My control is better close."

"Yes."

"Then I should be close."

"To the useful place."

"Correct."

"Which is usually not twelve useful places in six minutes."

Calder drank coffee very carefully.

I disliked him more with every swallow.

Orla held up one hand.

A faint pressure settled around her skin.

Not visible.

You could feel it if you stood close enough.

Aura.

Basic reinforcement.

Nothing exotic.

The kind of thing every competent field fighter learned to maintain before people trusted them near expensive work.

"What am I doing?" she asked.

"Showing off before sunrise."

"Aura."

"I know."

"Why?"

"Because you're fragile emotionally."

She looked at me.

"Impact. Abrasion. Bad footing. Stray debris. Something I did not predict."

"Yes."

"I spend a little all the time so I don't have to spend everything after something goes wrong."

"Yes."

"Support does the same thing."

I frowned.

"Support uses aura."

"Support uses a pocket."

That word I knew.

Not a spell.

Doctrine.

A support picked ground that reduced surprise, kept personal aura up, established the people most likely to need sustained reinforcement, and made the party work around that stable point when possible.

You could move.

Obviously.

You were not furniture.

But movement cost precision.

Reacquiring somebody cost attention.

Changing distance changed how much control you needed for the same result.

Running changed everything at once.

Footing.

Breath.

Hands.

Sight line.

Your own aura.

Theirs.

The useful shape of whatever you were trying to hold.

Support instructors had noticed this before I was born.

Annoying people.

"The pocket assumes the problem comes to you," I said.

"No. It assumes the party knows where support is."

"Same thing with better branding."

"No."

She said it flat enough that I stopped smiling.

"Greg, yesterday Calder knew where I was. I knew where Senn was. Senn knew where the cart was. None of us knew where you were because you were fixing whichever thing annoyed you most."

"The wheel was slipping."

"Yes."

"Taris was on bad footing."

"Yes."

"Calder's shoulder was compensating."

"Yes."

"So those were real problems."

"Yes."

I waited.

"And?"

"You were still wrong."

That was less satisfying.

Senn rolled one of her route sheets beside us.

"She's right."

I looked at her.

"Excellent."

"If I need support, I need to know where to move."

"You can shout."

"If I'm shouting because I need support, something already happened."

Also irritating.

Taris cut mold off the cheese.

"I think they're winning."

"Nobody asked the paperwork."

"Paperwork records outcomes."

I hated everyone.

We left twenty minutes later.

I maintained my aura.

This detail matters because at thirty-one I considered personal reinforcement boring.

It was not weak.

Mine was competent.

C-class competent, which is a phrase that sounds insulting only if you have spent too much time around people who rearrange landscapes.

I could take a bad fall better than an ordinary person.

I could turn a glancing strike into bruising instead of fracture.

I could keep cold rain from becoming quite as persuasive.

What I could not do was maintain aura, walk broken ground, watch five other people, keep a light stabilizing Barrier on the cart, and remain equally precise at all of them forever.

Nobody could.

That was why doctrine existed.

We reached the retaining line before midday.

It had once held a raised road against a steep ravine.

The road was gone.

The wall remained in pieces.

Senn wanted the old anchor spacing measured because the district was considering rebuilding the route farther inland.

The work was boring.

Good.

Boring work is where professions reveal what they actually believe.

Orla chose a flat shelf near the center of the line.

"Pocket."

I looked at it.

Back to stone.

Clear view north and south.

Thirty feet from Senn's first measurement.

Calder could range ahead without leaving sight.

Taris could bring rubbings and notes back instead of carrying his entire paper civilization over loose rock.

Reasonable.

I stood there.

For nine minutes.

This was personal growth.

Calder checked the northern break.

Orla stayed near Senn.

Taris moved between markers.

I watched.

Nothing happened.

Then Taris stepped onto a slab that shifted under him.

Not enough to fall.

His ankle rolled.

I caught it with a small plane under the outside edge of his boot.

Thirty feet.

The Barrier formed.

Late enough that he still stumbled.

He recovered.

Looked at me.

"Was that you?"

"Mostly you."

"Good."

He kept working.

I looked at my hand.

No tremor.

Fine.

Twenty minutes later Calder called from the northern break.

"Movement."

Everyone stopped.

Not people.

Rock.

A section of old wall had shifted under his weight.

Calder backed away.

Senn swore.

The wall settled.

Then stopped.

"We're done there," Orla said.

Senn looked at the unmeasured anchors.

"Need three more."

"You need none if the wall goes."

"Two."

"One."

"Fine."

Survey negotiation.

Calder moved south to a safer approach.

I stayed in the pocket.

That felt wrong.

Not morally.

Physically.

I could see Calder's route.

Could see Orla beside Senn.

Could see Taris carrying the sighting rod toward the next mark.

Everyone was inside a useful distance.

The doctrine worked.

I resented this immediately.

Then the wall made a sound.

Not loud.

Stone settling against stone.

A dry grinding knock.

Calder froze.

Orla said, "Back."

He moved.

The upper section shifted toward him.

I reinforced Calder's footing.

Correct target.

Correct timing.

Correct distance.

He pushed off cleanly.

The stone face dropped where he had been.

Good.

Then the lower course kicked outward.

Toward Taris.

He had been moving the sighting rod.

He turned at the noise.

Too late.

I dropped Calder.

Reached for Taris.

Thirty-eight feet, maybe forty.

Barrier caught the first slab.

Badly.

Too broad.

My own aura tightened reflexively when loose stone struck near my boots.

The second slab came lower.

Taris ran.

Wrong direction.

Not his fault.

People run away from falling walls.

The ravine happened to be away.

"Left!" I shouted.

He did not hear me.

Orla was already moving.

I could hold the slab.

Or I could catch Taris's footing near the edge.

Not both cleanly from where I stood.

I chose Taris.

Dropped the slab.

Put a narrow surface against the outside of his right boot.

He hit it.

Turned.

Fell on his hands instead of continuing toward the ravine.

The slab I released struck the sighting rod.

Brass snapped.

Senn made a sound I had previously associated with physical injury.

Orla reached Taris and dragged him inward.

Calder cleared the last moving stone.

Then it was quiet.

Dust.

A broken brass rod.

Taris on his knees.

Me standing exactly where doctrine had put me.

Senn walked to the rod.

Picked up one half.

Looked at the other.

"That was fourteen silver."

"Taris was more."

"Obviously."

She looked at me.

"Still fourteen silver."

Fair.

Orla checked Taris's hands.

Skin torn.

Nothing broken.

Calder came back down.

"You dropped me fast."

"You were clear."

"I was."

He rotated his ankle.

"Felt weird."

I looked at him.

"What did?"

"When you left."

"Describe weird."

"Now?"

"Yes."

"Greg."

"You brought it up."

He thought.

"Like missing a stair I knew wasn't there."

I frowned.

"You expected the reinforcement."

"For half a step."

"Did it change where you planted?"

"Maybe."

"Which foot?"

Orla said, "Can we finish not dying before you interview him?"

"This is relevant."

"So is the unstable wall."

She was right.

Again.

We moved camp-side of the old line and ate lunch without measuring the final anchor.

Senn wrote LOSS: SIGHTING ROD on a field sheet with enough pressure to injure the paper.

Taris had gravel in both palms.

Calder kept testing the step where I had dropped support.

I watched him do it three times.

Then Orla noticed me watching.

"Don't."

"I am not doing anything."

"You're about to."

"That is an accusation against future behavior."

"Accurate one."

I ate cheese.

The mold had been successfully negotiated.

After lunch we took the inland route.

The road climbed.

Not dramatically.

Just continuously.

The kind of grade that makes conversation expensive after an hour.

I tried the pocket again.

Orla near the cart.

Senn and Taris inside a reasonable working radius.

Calder ranging farther but self-sufficient.

I maintained aura.

Kept one low-cost cart correction active.

Moved only when the formation actually moved.

It worked.

I hated that too.

For perhaps two hours, I was a much better conventional support than I had been the day before.

Then Calder found the boars.

Not ordinary boars.

Mountain brushbacks.

Six of them.

Thick shoulders.

Bad temper.

Long enough tusks to make classification feel academic.

Calder came down the trail at a run.

"Move."

Orla looked behind him.

"How many?"

"Enough."

Useful number.

Senn pulled Taris and the cart toward a wider shelf.

Orla took the trail center.

I established behind her.

Pocket.

Aura.

Anchor first.

Textbook.

The first brushback hit Orla's shield.

I reinforced her rear leg and shoulder.

She gave ground one step.

Good.

Second came around the side.

Calder intercepted.

I stayed on Orla.

Correct.

Calder could handle one.

Third animal did not agree with our formation.

It went uphill.

Around Orla.

Toward the cart.

Taris had nowhere useful to go.

I shifted support.

Distance.

Too far.

Started moving.

Orla shouted, "Stay!"

I did not.

This was the mistake.

Not the idea.

The execution.

I ran uphill toward Taris while maintaining his Barrier and my own aura and trying not to lose awareness of Orla behind me.

Twenty yards is nothing.

Run twenty yards while doing three precise things and suddenly distance becomes a profession.

My first step was good.

Second.

Third.

Taris backed against the cart.

Brushback lowered its head.

I narrowed the Barrier in front of him.

Better.

Closer.

Ten yards.

My breathing changed.

Aura pulled tighter around my ribs as I ran.

Barrier edge fluttered.

I corrected it.

That correction cost attention.

My foot hit loose stone.

Warrior training kept me upright.

Barely.

The Barrier widened when my shoulder turned.

Wrong.

The brushback hit it.

Instead of turning the animal, I stopped too much of it.

Force came through the spell hard enough to numb two fingers.

I kept running.

Five yards.

Taris moved left.

Good.

The animal followed.

Bad.

I changed the plane.

Late.

Its tusk caught the cart cover.

Canvas ripped.

Senn screamed something about equipment.

I reached Taris.

Put my hand on the cart frame because apparently touching wood was easier than admitting I needed to stop moving.

The brushback turned for another charge.

I had it now.

Close.

Clear.

I set the Barrier narrow and angled.

It hit.

Slid.

Passed us by.

Calder came from the side and struck it behind the jaw with the flat of his sword hard enough to make the animal reconsider its career.

It ran.

The others followed after Orla and Calder convinced them the road was professionally disappointing.

Then I bent over.

Hands on knees.

Breathing like I had personally carried the cart uphill.

Taris looked at me.

"Are you dying?"

"No."

"You sound expensive."

Senn had been a bad influence.

Orla walked up.

She did not look pleased.

"What did I say this morning?"

I held up one finger.

Needed another breath.

"Several things."

"Stay in the pocket."

"The pocket was in the wrong place."

"Then move the pocket before the problem."

"The boar did not submit an itinerary."

"And you ran uphill maintaining aura and a live Barrier until your control went to shit."

I straightened.

"Taris is alive."

"Yes."

"Then again, evidence."

She stepped closer.

"Your Barrier stopped the animal instead of turning it."

I said nothing.

"If that had been a fighter instead of a brushback, you would have taken the hit through your control."

"Probably."

"If you fell, who supports Taris?"

I looked at Taris.

He raised both scraped hands.

"Not me."

Orla waited.

I looked back at her.

"You were right."

She blinked.

Apparently this was less common than I believed.

"About that run," I added.

There.

Balance restored.

"Greg."

"The useful place still moved."

"Yes."

"And the pocket did not."

"Yes."

"So staying still was also wrong."

"I did not say stay still forever."

"You said stay."

"Because you were already late."

That stopped me.

Calder had come close enough to hear.

He wiped dirt from his sword.

"She's right."

"This party has developed a theme."

"You saw the boar when I did," he said.

"Yes."

"You started moving after it picked Taris."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because before that it was a boar."

"It was uphill of Orla."

I looked back down the trail.

The route.

Orla's position.

The cart shelf.

Taris.

Where the animal had broken away.

There had been a point.

Earlier.

Not when Taris became the target.

When the animal stopped being part of Orla's problem.

I had seen it.

I had not moved because doctrine said the anchor was still the highest-value target.

Then I had moved too late and tried to make my legs repay the delay.

They had not.

Interesting.

I did not say that aloud.

Everyone was tired of my interesting things.

We repaired the cart cover with one of my two clamps.

Orla did not mention the second clamp.

Professional courtesy.

That night my calves hurt.

Not badly.

Enough.

My left hand hurt more.

I sat beside the fire opening and closing it while Calder cleaned his sword.

"When I dropped you at the wall," I said.

He sighed.

"Which foot?"

"Right."

"Did you change the plant because you expected me?"

"A little."

"Would you have moved differently if you knew I was leaving?"

"Yes."

"How?"

He showed me.

Not with words.

Stood up.

One step with support expected.

One without.

Small difference.

Hip.

Rear foot.

Recovery angle.

I watched.

"Again."

He did it again.

"You're annoying," he said.

"Again."

Third time.

Orla came back from checking the cart.

"What are you doing?"

Calder pointed at me.

"Apparently research."

I looked at her.

"When I reinforce your shield shoulder, what do you stop doing?"

She frowned.

"What?"

"You change something. Calder does. What do you change?"

"I don't know."

"Next time, tell me."

"During the fight?"

"After would be more conversational."

She sat down.

"You know supports train this."

"Train what?"

"Feedback."

"Not enough."

"There it is."

"What?"

"You deciding an entire profession is wrong because you asked Calder one question."

Calder sat back down.

"She is also right about that."

"Fuck both of you."

Orla smiled.

"Better."

The fire shifted.

A piece of wood broke inward.

Sparks climbed and disappeared.

I rubbed my calf.

"Who teaches Barrier past field utility?"

Orla looked at me.

"What do you mean?"

"Not bigger. Better. Failure. Countering. What actually breaks a maintained ward besides hitting it harder?"

Calder stopped cleaning the sword.

"Why?"

"Because I keep finding out what my Barrier does after something hits it."

I looked at my hand.

Two fingers still felt slightly wrong from the brushback.

"I'd rather know before."

Orla considered that.

"Ward crews."

"Construction?"

"Some. Security. Siege people. Breakers."

"Breakers use Barrier?"

"They break it."

I looked at her.

She looked back.

Calder said, very quietly, "Oh no."

I ignored him.

"Where do we find one?"

Orla pointed north with her spoon.

"Not tonight."

Fair.

My calves agreed.
