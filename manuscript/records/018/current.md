## RECORD 018

## THE WITNESS

The Guild hearing had bad chairs.

I remember that before I remember the panel.

Three witness chairs.

Straight backs.

Shallow seats.

Built by somebody who believed discomfort improved truth.

Maybe it did.

Lorn sat beside me and bounced one knee.

I told him to stop.

He bounced the other.

Senn sat across the room with a folder on his lap.

He looked less angry than he had on Rope Street.

That made me nervous.

Angry people are easy to read.

Prepared people have pages.

The panel had three members.

A Guild contract officer.

A building-trades representative.

A technical reviewer.

The technical reviewer was Tova Renn.

I looked at her.

She looked at me.

“No,” she said.

The contract officer frowned.

“No what?”

“He is about to make a face.”

“I am not.”

Tova looked at the officer.

“There.”

The officer rubbed her temple.

“We have not started.”

“Good,” I said.

Lorn covered his mouth.

Senn did not smile.

Probably wise.

The officer's name was Aven.

Not a clerk in the front-window sense.

Guild clerks ran half the civilization nobody noticed.

Aven had gray hair tied in a knot and the expression of a woman who had spent thirty years watching people discover that agreements meant words.

She opened the file.

“Complaint by Orren Senn regarding private Line inspection at fourteen through eighteen Rope Street.”

There were only three buildings.

Street numbering is not required to be logical.

“Allegations: unauthorized removal of interior wall finish, unauthorized service isolation, failure to complete contracted scope, and resulting loss of heat to occupied rental units.”

She looked at Senn.

“Accurate summary?”

“Yes.”

Then at me.

“Gregory?”

“Greg.”

“That was not the question.”

Tova looked pleased.

I disliked the hearing already.

“Yes. Accurate summary.”

Aven looked at Lorn.

“Lorn Esset?”

He nodded.

“Speak.”

“Yes.”

“Good. You may all continue doing that.”

The trades representative coughed into one hand.

Possibly laughter.

Aven started with the contract.

One-page private call sheet.

SITE.

CLIENT.

SCOPE.

RANK OR TRADE AUTHORITY.

FEE.

My handwriting sat in the middle.

INSPECT RECURRING WALL KNOCK / HOUSE-SIDE LINE PRESSURE LOSS.

That was it.

Aven tapped the line.

“Does this authorize wall opening?”

“No.”

“Service isolation?”

I hesitated.

“Not explicitly.”

“Does it prohibit either?”

“No.”

Senn said, “It says inspect.”

Aven looked at him.

“You will get your turn.”

Senn stopped.

She looked back at me.

“What instructions did you give Esset?”

“Visual only. Do not open wall.”

Lorn's knee stopped bouncing.

Aven turned toward him.

“Did you receive that instruction?”

“Yes.”

“Did you open the wall?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

I had spent the weekend wanting to answer that question for him.

The urge came back immediately.

I kept my mouth shut.

Lorn looked at Senn.

Then the panel.

“The wall knocked when heat came up. Metal contact sound. Couldn't see the service sleeve. Plaster was already cracked along the knock point.”

“Was the plaster loose?”

“Yes.”

“How much did you remove?”

Lorn held his hands apart.

Aven looked at the trades representative.

He checked a note.

“Approximately two feet square.”

Lorn nodded.

“That.”

“Why did you not wait for Gregory?”

“Thought the contact could wear the sleeve through.”

“Did you know it had?”

“No.”

“So you opened property against instruction based on a suspected hazard.”

“Yes.”

“Did you notify Senn first?”

“No.”

Senn sat slightly straighter.

Aven made a note.

“Why not?”

Lorn thought.

“Because I was already in the wall.”

I closed my eyes.

Tova's mouth moved.

Not a smile.

Almost.

Aven did not react.

“That is sequence, not reason.”

Lorn nodded.

“I didn't think I needed permission to verify a pressure hazard.”

“Were you operating under Rank authority?”

“No.”

“Trade emergency authority?”

“Station hand.”

“Private station hand.”

“Yes.”

“Different authority.”

“Yes.”

“Did you know that at the time?”

Lorn's knee started again.

“No.”

Aven wrote something.

That hurt more than I expected.

Not because Lorn looked incompetent.

Because I had hired him without making sure either of us knew what authority traveled with the contract.

At East Four, station rules lived in our bodies after years of repetition.

Private work looked similar enough that we assumed the same instincts transferred.

They did not all transfer.

Aven moved to the shutdown.

“You observed a pressure sleeve with visible wear and active condensate.”

“Yes.”

“You isolated the shared building feed.”

“Yes.”

“Could you isolate only the affected building?”

“No.”

“Did you attempt to?”

“Yes.”

“How?”

Lorn described the manifold.

Shared upstream shutoff.

Individual branches after the damaged section.

No safe way to keep the other two live without pressurizing the worn sleeve.

Tova spoke for the first time since my face.

“Depth of groove?”

“Didn't measure before shutdown.”

“Why?”

“Leak bead.”

She nodded.

“Good.”

Senn shifted in his chair.

Aven looked at him.

“Your turn.”

Senn opened his folder.

Prepared people have pages.

He had photographs.

Actual chemical plates from a neighborhood survey photographer.

One of the open wall.

One of the cracked plaster before repair.

One of coal braziers in a wash yard.

One of a tenant holding a rent receipt.

I understood what he was doing before he spoke.

The complaint was not really about the sleeve anymore.

It was about consequence.

“I am not disputing the leak,” Senn said.

That changed the room.

Lorn looked at me.

I kept watching Senn.

“The fitter I hired next morning replaced it. He said shutdown was appropriate.”

Aven asked, “Then what remedy are you seeking?”

“Wall repair and one day's service loss.”

“Service loss to whom?”

“My tenants.”

“Did you refund rent?”

Senn hesitated.

Interesting.

“No.”

The trades representative leaned forward.

“Then whose loss are you claiming?”

“The buildings lost heat.”

“That is not a person.”

Senn looked irritated.

“The tenants complained to me. I supplied coal heaters. I paid for them.”

There.

Actual cost.

“How much?” Aven asked.

Two silver and six copper.

Coal from district emergency stores.

Cart fee.

One broken brazier handle.

He had receipts.

Prepared.

Reasonable.

“I also paid for plaster repair.”

“How much?”

One silver and four.

Aven wrote both numbers down.

“Anything else?”

“I lost half a day arranging it.”

“Not compensable under private works rules.”

Senn nodded.

He knew.

That bothered me more than anger would have.

He had not come to punish us.

He came to recover costs he believed our process created.

Some of them it had.

Aven looked at me.

“Response?”

I had several.

The leak was real.

Shutdown was necessary.

Senn would have paid more if the sleeve failed.

We probably prevented worse damage.

All true.

Also defensive.

“The isolation was correct.”

Tova nodded slightly.

“The wall opening was not properly authorized.”

Lorn looked at me.

I kept going.

“My work order was incomplete. I told Lorn visual only and gave him no emergency expansion procedure.”

Aven asked, “Does that make his decision yours?”

There it was.

The question I had been avoiding since the notice arrived.

“No.”

Lorn's knee stopped.

“He chose to open the wall.”

“Against your instruction.”

“Yes.”

“Do you believe he was wrong?”

I looked at Lorn.

Then the photograph.

Cracked plaster.

Bright groove.

Wet bead.

“No.”

Aven's face did not change.

“Explain.”

“He violated my instruction and still made the safer field decision.”

The trades representative leaned back.

Tova watched me carefully.

Aven said, “Those can coexist.”

“Yes.”

“You understand that is not comfortable for a contract system.”

“I am learning.”

That almost got a smile.

Almost.

Tova asked, “If you had been present, would you have opened the wall?”

“Yes.”

“Before client approval?”

I thought.

“Probably not.”

“Would waiting have increased immediate risk?”

“Maybe.”

“Evidence?”

“No evidence the sleeve was minutes from failure.”

“Then?”

“I would have told Senn first.”

Lorn looked at the floor.

Not shame.

Thinking.

Good.

Aven closed one page and opened another.

“We are going to separate the actions.”

Of course.

Good institutions love separating things people keep trying to bundle into moral stories.

“Service isolation. Technical reviewer?”

Tova said, “Supported. Active pressure leak on a worn shared sleeve. Continued operation would be outside ordinary safe practice.”

“Any fault?”

“No.”

Aven wrote.

“Wall opening.”

The trades representative answered.

“Technically useful. Contractually outside stated scope. Emergency property entry can be justified if delay presents likely immediate harm. The record does not establish immediate harm before opening because the leak was discovered after opening.”

Circular.

Annoying.

Correct.

“So not justified?” Lorn asked.

“Not clearly justified under the contract you had.”

Different sentence.

Lorn nodded.

Aven turned to me.

“Supervision.”

I sat straighter.

“Inadequate delegation terms. You hired a station worker for private work and relied on station emergency norms without writing or confirming private authority.”

“Yes.”

“You have already changed your work sheet?”

I handed her the new one.

She read it.

STOP AUTHORITY.

OPEN AUTHORITY.

CALL AUTHORITY.

Tova leaned slightly toward the page.

“Better.”

Aven looked at her.

“Do not grade documents while I am holding them.”

“Sorry.”

She was not.

The finding took fifteen minutes.

No fine.

No discipline for Lorn.

Service isolation upheld.

Senn's claim for lost heat denied because the shutdown responded to an actual building hazard, not contractor error.

His wall-repair claim was partially granted.

We owed one silver and four copper because the opening exceeded explicit scope and client notice was feasible before removal.

Coal costs denied.

The building would have needed alternate heat during necessary repair regardless of who found the leak.

Senn did not look happy.

He did not look cheated either.

Probably the best a hearing can do.

Aven read the final instruction.

“Future private work involving pressure systems must state emergency stop authority and property-opening authority in scope. Workers operating under another registrant's contract must receive those terms before site work.”

She looked at me.

“Write less badly.”

“Yes.”

Then at Lorn.

“Ask before removing someone's wall when the building is not actively trying to kill you.”

“Yes.”

Then at Senn.

“If you want guaranteed heat during emergency repair, buy a building with redundant service.”

Senn stared at her.

“That was a joke,” Aven said.

“Oh.”

“Poor one.”

She closed the file.

Hearing over.

Outside Guild Hall, cold air felt better than the chairs.

Senn caught us on the steps.

I expected another argument.

Instead he said, “The repair held.”

Lorn nodded.

“Good.”

Senn looked at me.

“You still owe me one and four.”

“I know.”

“I expect it.”

“You'll get it.”

He started down the steps.

Then stopped.

“The back building knocks too.”

I looked at him.

He looked embarrassed to be asking.

“Same time?”

“No. Rain.”

Lorn said, “Roof.”

Senn glared at him.

“Could be.”

I should have refused the job.

Probably.

Instead I said, “Eight silver.”

Senn's eyebrows went up.

Lorn looked at me.

I kept my face still.

“Eight?” Senn asked.

“Inspection only. Wall opening requires notice.”

He almost smiled.

“Six.”

“Eight.”

“Seven.”

I thought about Nessa.

“Eight.”

Senn sighed.

“Fine.”

Lorn waited until he was gone.

Then said, “You learned pricing at a disciplinary hearing.”

“Not disciplinary.”

“Review hearing.”

“Yes.”

“You still owe him money.”

“Yes.”

“So we are working for the man we owe money to.”

“Yes.”

“That is terrible business.”

“Eight silver.”

Lorn considered.

“Getting better.”

We paid the one and four from PRIVATE WORK.

Nessa circled it in the ledger.

“Why?”

“Education.”

“That is not a category.”

“It is now.”

She wrote LESSON beside it.

I crossed it out.

She wrote it again.

The word stayed.
