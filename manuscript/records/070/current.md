## RECORD 070

## THE EXPEDITION

The first thing I learned about S-class field work was that breakfast remained disappointing.

Three of the Seven did not improve porridge.

Pava tried salt.

This was a mistake in a salt basin.

Mael ate without expression.

Arcrutus added dried fruit he had not packed for me.

I considered that a betrayal.

The technical crew broke camp while we ate.

Not quickly.

Cleanly.

Every piece had a place because deep expeditions punish improvisation after the third day.

One bag each.

Tools clipped externally only if they could survive pressure shear.

Water accounted twice.

Food once because hunger is slower than dehydration.

Nothing left on the ground.

Nothing buried casually.

Salt remembers metal better than people expect.

Mael gave the morning brief standing beside a black route marker hammered through six inches of crust.

“Greg.”

I looked up.

“Insurance remains insurance.”

“Yes.”

“No Draw from expedition personnel unless requested or immediate life safety removes the time required to ask.”

“Obviously.”

His face changed enough to tell me I had used the wrong word.

“Not obviously. Your history includes learning that rule through injury.”

Right.

Fair.

“Yes.”

“No experimental links.”

“Yes.”

“No adapting expedition procedure because you think you see a cheaper arrangement.”

I opened my mouth.

Closed it.

Pava smiled into her cup.

“Yes.”

“You may use ordinary support at technical-crew request. You may use Barrier freely where it does not alter mapped pressure behavior. If an S-class member asks for support, ask what they want before improving it.”

That last one bothered me.

Not because it was wrong.

Because it was specific.

“You've had support try to improve you before.”

Mael looked at me.

“Yes.”

“How bad?”

“Later.”

Pava said, “Very.”

Mael ignored her.

“Route window is nine hours. First descent point by midday. Lower shelf by evening if conditions hold. We do not chase schedule through changing pressure.”

Everyone nodded.

Good rule.

S-class did not mean weather became obligated.

We moved north.

The salt flats looked empty until you walked them with people who knew where not to step.

Then they became crowded with invisible things.

Old pressure scars.

Thin crust over brine.

Mineral ridges carrying more weight than their shape suggested.

Wind-polished markers from survey crews decades dead.

The route surveyor walked first with a narrow rod pressed to the surface every twenty paces.

Mael followed.

Then the two technical specialists.

Pava.

Healer.

Me.

Arcrutus last.

“Because you are watching me?” I asked without turning.

“Yes.”

“Comforting.”

“No.”

Aren had inherited that problem honestly.

The first hour was boring.

Good expeditions contain a lot of boring.

We walked.

Adjusted face cloths.

Checked water.

Stopped twice for readings.

I learned that Pava hummed when standing still and stopped the instant anything changed.

Mael's left knee clicked.

That surprised me.

Not because S-class people should have perfect joints.

Because people rarely put clicking knees in songs.

Arcrutus carried his half-empty expedition bag without comment.

I kept noticing what was missing from it.

Second pressure anchor.

Condenser.

Repair plates.

He had distributed some small redundancies across the others after I appeared.

Not enough to replace what he left behind.

His decision remained present in the expedition even when nobody discussed it.

That was how real costs worked.

They did not need speeches.

Two hours north, the route rod sank three inches farther than expected.

Everyone stopped.

No command.

The surveyor knelt.

Pressed again.

Moved left.

Again.

“Void changed.”

Mael asked, “Size?”

“Unknown.”

Pava unclipped one metal rod from her back.

Up close it was not a weapon.

Not exactly.

Six feet long.

Flat on two sides.

Covered in tiny adjustable collars.

Each collar held a different mineral insert.

A field instrument designed by someone with enough money to stop pretending instruments needed to be convenient.

Pava drove it into the salt.

Not hard.

Precision mattered more.

Then she touched two fingers to the top.

Pressure spread outward.

I felt it before the instrument responded visibly.

Not a pulse.

A question.

The salt answered badly.

A shallow section thirty feet ahead flexed.

Pava's second hand came up.

The pressure changed direction.

The flex stopped.

I stared.

She had not reinforced the weak section.

She had made the surrounding crust carry the question instead.

Mael looked at me without turning his head fully.

“Face.”

I frowned.

“What?”

“You have one.”

Everyone had become unbearable across generations.

Pava released the rod.

Her right hand shook once.

Tiny.

Gone immediately.

I saw it anyway.

“Channel?” I asked.

She looked at me.

Mael looked at me harder.

Right.

Insurance.

Pava flexed her fingers.

“Fine.”

That meant nothing.

I knew better than to continue.

The route surveyor marked a new line twenty yards west.

We detoured.

Pava kept full walking pace.

Her reserve felt enormous.

That was the obvious thing.

The less obvious thing was that I would not have drawn from her if she offered.

Not then.

Her problem was not reserve.

The rod technique had driven strain through the same forearm channels she would need for another pressure read.

Taking mana would have reduced the wrong resource.

Giving mana would have solved nothing.

The useful support, if she needed any, would have been stabilization or load redistribution around the channel path.

I wrote that down at the next stop.

PAVA: POWER NOT LIMIT.

Then crossed out Pava.

Too personal from one observation.

WROTE:

S-CLASS: POWER MAY NOT BE LIMITING VARIABLE.

Better.

By midday the surface opened.

Not dramatically.

No canyon.

No black mouth in the earth.

A shallow depression between two salt ridges ended at a wall of gray stone twenty feet wide.

Natural at first glance.

Then I saw the seam.

Straight.

Old.

The rock had been placed.

The technical specialist nearest it unpacked three thin gauges.

Mael said, “Nobody crosses the seam until we know where the load goes.”

I liked him more immediately.

The gauges went onto the stone.

One low.

One middle.

One against the salt floor.

Numbers did not agree.

Of course.

The lower gauge showed almost no pressure.

Middle showed more than the wall should carry.

Upper showed intermittent movement.

Pava frowned.

“Hidden support.”

The specialist said, “Or bad coupling.”

“Test.”

Mael placed one hand against the wall.

Nobody told me what he was doing.

I felt his reinforcement change anyway.

It did not rise much.

That was the terrifying part.

He pushed.

The wall moved.

Not collapsed.

Moved.

Half an inch inward across twenty feet of fitted stone.

The sound traveled through the salt beneath my boots.

My body reacted before my brain.

Barrier came up around the nearest technical specialist.

Cheap shell.

Dust and chips only.

Mael released pressure.

The wall settled back.

My Barrier stayed for another beat.

Then dropped.

Mael looked at it.

I expected criticism.

“Good,” he said.

That single word from him was irritatingly effective.

Pava checked the gauges.

“Middle pressure changed. Lower didn't.”

“Load bypass,” the specialist said.

“Where?”

Nobody knew.

I did.

Not where.

What kind of question.

The wall was visible structure.

Something else was carrying the consequence.

I crouched beside the lower gauge.

Did not touch.

Mael let me look.

Progress.

“Barrier?” I asked.

The specialist shook her head.

“No maintained edge detected.”

“Draw test?”

Mael said, “No.”

I looked up.

“Why?”

“Because we do not siphon from unidentified buried systems to satisfy curiosity.”

Again.

Fair.

Pava looked at me.

“Do you always ask the dangerous question first?”

“No.”

Arcrutus said, “Yes.”

Traitor.

We opened the wall manually.

That took three hours.

Not because three S-class humans lacked the power to break it.

Because breaking it was not the job.

That distinction became the expedition.

Mael could have shattered the fitted stone in one hit.

Pava could probably have made the pressure behind it choose another county.

Arcrutus could have found some upsetting physical solution involving leverage and contempt.

Instead the technical crew mapped each block.

We loosened four.

Supported two.

Removed one.

Then another.

Pava held the upper pressure relation stable while the stones came out.

Mael carried each block away.

Arcrutus worked the lower seam because his missing second anchor meant he had to do manually what the expedition plan expected equipment to share.

There.

Cost again.

He did not complain.

Neither did I.

Halfway through, Pava's rod hand started shaking again.

Stronger.

She still had reserve.

I could feel that from fifteen feet away.

Enough mana to do the technique again.

Her body disagreed.

Mael noticed too.

“Switch.”

Pava said, “One more.”

“No.”

“I have it.”

“Your hand doesn't.”

She looked down.

Angry.

Not at him.

At the fact.

The technical specialist adjusted the plan.

Different anchor.

Slower removal.

No one gave Pava more mana.

No one praised her reserve.

No one asked whether she was strong enough.

She was obviously strong enough.

That was not the question.

I stood there with an A-class support badge and finally saw the S-class problem clearly enough to feel stupid.

At lower levels, support often meant increasing capability.

More reinforcement.

More reserve.

More protection.

Enough strength to survive what came next.

These people already had more capability than most operations could use safely.

Their limits lived elsewhere.

Hands.

Channels.

Footing.

Recoil.

Transition.

Attention.

The body part that had to survive the second impossible thing after completing the first.

Support for them could not merely mean more.

More was sometimes the least useful thing available.

I wrote nothing.

Not yet.

Some thoughts become stupid when forced into sentences too quickly.

Near evening the second block came free.

Cold air moved from the opening.

Not surface cold.

Deep cold.

The kind untouched by the day's heat.

A passage waited behind the wall.

Stone floor.

Black mineral lines running along both sides.

Old fittings.

No dust near the center.

That bothered me.

Pava noticed too.

“Airflow.”

The specialist held a thread into the opening.

It moved inward.

Something below was drawing air.

Mael looked at the sky.

Then the route clock.

“We camp here.”

Nobody argued.

We had enough day to descend badly.

Not enough to descend well and still recover.

S-class did not make tomorrow unnecessary.

Camp went up against the stone ridge.

This time I knew where the secondary wind screen belonged before anyone asked.

I raised it.

Pava altered the cook flame around it.

Arcrutus drove anchors.

Mael sat with the technical crew over the route slate.

I ate better porridge because someone found dried onion.

Night came hard across the salt.

The opening in the stone remained black.

Pressure moved beneath us twice after dark.

Each time the old fittings inside answered with a vibration too low to hear and easy to feel through the ground.

I lay awake inside my shelter and thought about Pava's hand.

Not her power.

Her hand.

Then Mael's knee.

Arcrutus's missing anchor.

The wall carrying less load than the gauges said it should.

Three S-class humans above an underground system that did not appear impressed by any of them.

Somewhere below us, something was paying for stability.

We did not know what.

That was the first night I understood why Arcrutus had put me in the bag.

I was still angry.

Understanding is not forgiveness.

But they had enough power.

What they did not have was certainty about where the cost was going.

That was my kind of problem.
