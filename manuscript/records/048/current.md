## RECORD 048

## THE OPENING

Orla made me promise not to use Draw on her before she let me sign the contract.

This was unfair.

I had never used Draw on her.

"That is why the promise should be easy," she said.

Calder witnessed.

Senn was not on this job.

Neither was Taris.

That mattered more than I expected.

Repeat work is not repetition.

You return to three familiar faces and discover the fourth place has been filled by somebody else.

The south-quarry stabilization crew belonged to a contractor named Deren Holt, who had too much beard and an admirable hatred of avoidable paperwork.

He had six laborers, one stone engineer, Orla on hazard control, Calder on forward work, and me because Orla had apparently decided I was useful despite the possibility that I might attack her aura for educational purposes.

"I said I wouldn't."

"Good."

"You keep saying it like you don't believe me."

"I believe you now."

"That sounds worse."

It was late summer.

Hot enough that dust stuck to sweat before lunch.

The quarry had stopped active cutting seven years earlier, but the south shelf still supported a district road above it.

Bad combination.

Old extraction tunnels ran under the road.

Some had been filled.

Some were recorded as filled.

Different category.

We were there because two sections of retaining wall had started pushing outward after spring rain.

No collapse yet.

The road remained open to light traffic.

District wanted reinforcement before weather returned.

Reasonable.

For the first two weeks nothing tried to kill us.

I appreciated this less at thirty-two than I should have.

We drilled test bores.

Mapped voids.

Moved timber.

Ran temporary brace lines.

Argued about whether one tunnel had been deliberately blocked or simply filled with thirty years of garbage.

Garbage won.

I worked support where needed.

Normal support.

Aura up.

Pocket when useful.

Orla as primary anchor when heavy work moved.

Labor crews self-reinforced for lifts.

Calder handled spaces where falling rock or bad footing justified a Ranker.

I did not drain anyone.

Orla mentioned this on day nine.

"Very proud of you."

"Fuck you."

"Good restraint."

"I hope your lunch is dry."

It was.

Mine too.

Field justice.

I had started running in the mornings.

Nothing impressive.

Two miles if camp schedule allowed.

Sometimes less.

Mostly because Record Forty-Five had made something impossible to ignore.

I could see where I needed to be before I could get there.

That offended me.

So I ran.

Badly.

First week my calves hurt.

Second week my lungs stopped acting surprised.

Then I tried maintaining a thin Barrier around a pebble in my palm while jogging and discovered my lungs had simply delegated the complaint to my hands.

The Barrier wandered.

Pebble bounced.

Once it hit me in the chin.

Calder saw.

He told Orla.

I considered murder.

Training continued.

The accident happened on day seventeen.

Of course it was not supposed to be an accident.

We had cleared one of the old upper galleries enough to install temporary timber under the road bed.

The gallery was narrow.

Eight feet high at the best point.

Five at the bad one.

Old tool scars in the limestone.

Timber black with age where earlier braces remained.

Deren's crew had built a new support frame near the entrance while the stone engineer checked the deeper wall.

The plan was simple.

Calder and two laborers moved a crossbeam into place.

Orla stood under the inner side with shield and aura ready in case loose stone came down.

I held support position near the entrance where I could reinforce the crew and maintain a temporary dust Barrier around the active cut.

Pocket doctrine.

Useful place.

Everyone knew where I was.

See?

Growth.

The first sound was not rock.

Timber.

A short wooden crack.

Not loud.

Deren heard it.

So did I.

"Stop."

Everybody stopped.

The new frame had not failed.

An old brace behind Orla had.

Thirty years of pressure had bent it inward.

Now that we had changed load with the new frame, the old timber decided its career was complete.

It split.

The limestone above it settled.

Not much.

An inch maybe.

Enough to turn a quiet gallery into six people making decisions at once.

Orla raised her shield.

Calder dropped the crossbeam.

Laborers moved back.

Deren shouted for everyone out.

Correct.

Then a slab over Orla's left side shifted.

She could not move back without letting it fall across the exit path.

So she stayed.

Also correct.

Aura flared around her.

Not visible.

Again, not exactly.

Pressure changed.

The air around her became harder to ignore.

I reinforced her shoulder and rear leg.

She caught the slab on the shield.

Weight came down.

Too much.

"Move!" Calder shouted.

"Can't!"

He went toward her.

I caught his footing.

Deren's laborers dragged the dropped crossbeam out of the route.

Good.

The slab settled another fraction.

Orla's aura tightened.

I felt something I had not known how to feel a year earlier.

Not low mana.

Not weak reinforcement.

Pressure with nowhere useful to go.

Her aura had reinforced the shoulder, ribs, hip, and rear leg exactly as trained.

She was surviving.

That was the problem.

Every part of the structure was insisting on staying where it was while the stone continued moving.

Her left arm shook.

Not because the aura was failing.

Because it was succeeding too hard.

"Orla," I said.

"Busy."

"Your shoulder."

"I know."

"Can you let it move?"

"Not while holding this."

There.

The old answer would have been more reinforcement.

Make the shoulder hold.

Spend harder.

I had enough for another burst.

Maybe.

But the Draw pressure was obvious to me now.

Her aura was a maintained system.

A very good one.

Too good in exactly one place.

I broke my promise.

Not proudly.

Not strategically.

I reached.

Tiny Draw.

Left shoulder path only.

At least that was what I intended.

Orla screamed.

Her aura opened under my hand like I had cut a strap.

Not much.

Enough.

Her left shoulder dropped three inches.

The shield angle changed.

The slab slid.

Instead of driving straight down through her arm, part of the stone rode across the shield edge and hit the gallery wall.

Better.

Also worse.

A chunk the size of my head broke free from the wall.

Calder was underneath it.

I had mana in me that did not belong there.

That is the simplest way to say it.

Orla's.

Hot with reinforcement pattern.

Wrong shape.

My aura tried to catch it.

I dropped my aura almost by reflex because Mara had spent a year hitting me verbally every time I trapped foreign flow.

For one very clear second I was standing in an unstable quarry gallery with no continuous self-reinforcement while a stone fell at Calder.

Good planning.

I put the drawn mana into Barrier.

Not cleanly.

Not efficiently.

Enough.

A plane appeared above Calder's right shoulder.

Stone hit.

Barrier folded.

Calder ducked.

Rock glanced past him and shattered on the floor.

Then my stomach turned over.

Not metaphorically.

I vomited against the wall.

Deren shouted something.

Orla shouted something worse.

Calder got under her shield side.

The laborers pulled one of the new braces forward.

I wiped my mouth.

Aura back up.

Hands shaking.

Support again.

This time normal.

I reinforced the temporary brace while Deren's crew wedged it.

Calder took part of Orla's load physically.

Orla stepped back.

The slab settled onto timber instead of person.

We exited.

Nobody died.

Wonderful standard.

Outside, Orla hit me.

Open hand.

Chest.

Not hard.

Still enough to surprise me because my aura had come back but pride had not.

"You used Draw."

"Yes."

"On me."

"Yes."

"After promising."

"Yes."

"Anything else?"

I considered.

"It worked."

She hit me again.

Calder looked away.

Cowardice had become tradition.

Deren's stone engineer came over before Orla found a heavier object.

Her name was Edda Pell.

No relation to the tournament food-poisoning victim as far as I know.

She had been checking the deeper wall and missed the first seconds of the failure.

She did not miss the argument.

"What did you draw?"

I told her.

"How much?"

"Small."

"That is not a measurement."

"I know."

"How much?"

I did not know.

That answer ended the debate for a moment.

Edda looked at Orla.

"Any numbness?"

"No."

"Weakness?"

Orla rotated the shoulder slowly.

Winced.

"Pain was already there."

"Different pain?"

She thought.

"Less sharp now. More muscle."

Edda looked at me.

"You got lucky."

"Probably."

"Not probably. You did not know how much you removed."

"I knew the path."

"You knew what you thought was the path."

That one landed.

Arcrutus had ruined civilization again from hundreds of miles away.

Edda continued.

"If you had pulled deeper, her shield arm could have failed while the slab was on it."

"Yes."

"If you had hit the wrong reinforcement path, ribs instead of shoulder, same."

"Yes."

"If her aura had responded by hardening the remaining paths, you could have concentrated load into the joint you were trying to relieve."

I had not considered that exact sequence.

My face must have shown it.

"There," she said. "That one."

Orla crossed her arms.

"I hate when other people get his face."

"My face is normal."

Nobody answered.

Edda pointed toward the gallery.

"Your idea was not nonsense."

That surprised me.

"Thank you."

"Do not enjoy it."

"Apparently everyone knows me."

"Pressure relief is real. Medical teams relieve overloaded channels. Bonded pairs redistribute. Reinforcement instructors teach selective drop. What you did was use an attack technique as emergency relief without consent or calibration."

"When you say it like that..."

"I am saying it accurately."

Orla said, "Keep going."

Edda did.

"You were right that holding harder was not automatically the answer. You were wrong to assume seeing the problem meant you had earned the intervention."

There.

I disliked her immediately.

Useful person.

Deren suspended gallery work for the day.

Good decision.

We rebuilt the brace plan outside.

Orla did not speak to me for three hours.

That was impressive given we worked twenty feet apart.

Calder spoke enough for everyone.

Mostly about timber.

He had excellent survival instincts.

At dinner Orla sat across from me.

"Did you know it would hurt?"

"No."

"It felt like someone pulled a stitch out from inside my shoulder."

I stopped eating.

"That specific?"

"Yes."

"Before the shoulder dropped?"

She stared.

I put my spoon down.

"Sorry. Later."

She kept staring.

Then said, "Before. Very fast. Then the arm felt loose."

"Loose bad?"

"At first. Then better."

"How much better?"

"Greg."

"Right."

She ate two bites.

Then pointed the spoon at me.

"You do not get to use me because you're curious."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"Yes."

"You broke a promise today."

"Yes."

No joke available.

That was fine.

"I thought the slab was going to break your shoulder."

"Maybe it was."

"I should have asked."

"There was not much time."

"Still."

She looked down at the bowl.

"If you ask next time, the answer might still be no."

"I know."

"Good."

We finished dinner.

The next morning she let me support her normally.

That mattered.

She did not let me use Draw.

Also mattered.

For the remaining four weeks I did not use Draw on a person.

Not once.

I used it on two dead ward plates the engineer had brought for access work.

One cracked storage stone.

A contaminated anchor we needed empty before disposal.

Objects.

Consent uncomplicated.

I kept running mornings.

Two miles became three.

Then three with a hill.

Then three while maintaining a thumb-sized Barrier around a pebble without getting hit in the face.

Progress.

Small.

Real.

Orla's shoulder healed.

The bruise spread yellow down her arm and disappeared.

The gallery got proper cribbing.

The district road never closed.

Deren paid on time.

At the end of six weeks, Edda handed me a folded page.

I assumed invoice.

Worse.

It was a list.

CONTROLLED CHANNEL RELIEF.

Three training references.

Two healer texts.

One name in Vey who worked transfer compatibility.

At the bottom she had written:

DRAW IS STILL NOT SUPPORT.

I looked at her.

"Mixed message."

"No. Very clear message. Learn the legitimate methods before you invent reasons yours is special."

"You think it's special?"

"I think you're going to keep doing this whether I help or not."

"Probably."

"Exactly."

Orla came over with her pack.

"What did she give you?"

"Homework."

Orla looked at Edda.

"You have made a mistake."

"I know."

I folded the page into Mara's old pamphlet.

That winter I read all five references.

Not quickly.

I worked.

Travelled.

Ran.

Did support the normal way more often than not.

And every time someone spent too much in one place while someone else stood nearby with more than they needed, I noticed.

I did nothing.

Mostly.

That was training too.
