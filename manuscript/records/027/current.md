## RECORD 027

## THE LEASEHOLDER

We signed for five years because Mina threatened to rent the room to musicians.

That was not the only reason.

It was the fastest one.

The room was behind ours.

Same floor.

Different door.

For years it belonged to an old tailor named Siva who worked downstairs from sunrise until his eyes became bad enough that thread started winning.

Then his daughter moved him north.

The room sat empty for six weeks.

I did not notice.

Nessa did.

Of course.

We were coming upstairs after dinner at my parents' when she stopped on the landing.

The door beside ours stood open.

Mina was inside with a broom and a cloth tied over her hair.

Dust came out around her ankles.

Nessa looked in.

"Siva gone?"

"Six weeks."

I looked at Nessa.

"You knew?"

"Yes."

"Why didn't I?"

"You thought he was sleeping."

"For six weeks?"

"You do not monitor neighbors well."

Fair.

Mina leaned on the broom.

"You want it?"

"No," I said.

"Yes," Nessa said.

Mina smiled.

Traitor.

I stepped inside because apparently refusal now required inspection.

The room was larger than ours by half.

Two windows.

Small stove.

Separate wash basin.

One narrow back room that could hold a bed if the person using it had no objection to walls.

The front room had enough space for a proper desk and shelves.

Nessa walked straight to the windows.

I walked straight to the floor.

Uneven.

I put my heel against one board.

It rocked.

"Absolutely not."

Nessa looked at me.

"You haven't heard the rent."

"Floor slopes."

Mina said, "Toward the drain."

I looked around.

"There is no drain."

"Then toward where a drain should be."

"This building is a crime."

"You live here."

"Yes. I have evidence."

Mina swept around my boots.

Not subtly.

The rent for both rooms together was cheaper than two separate apartments and much more than one.

Obviously.

Numbers are cruel that way.

Mina offered us the connecting wall.

Not the wall itself.

Permission to open a doorway through it.

I stared at her.

"You want me to open your wall."

"You fixed the bakery feed."

"That is not masonry."

"You know masons."

"That is closer."

Nessa had already started measuring the front wall with her hands.

Not accurately.

Enough to annoy me.

"What are you doing?"

"Desk."

"We have a desk."

"We have your desk with my papers occupying contested territory."

"Shared desk."

"Contested territory."

She moved to the window.

"Mine could go here."

Not our desk.

Hers.

Good sign.

Bad for my negotiation position.

We went back into our apartment.

The difference between the rooms became offensive immediately.

Our table took most of the center.

The pantry door I had repaired the week before closed now.

Mostly.

Three chairs fit without argument.

Four required diplomacy.

The old stool lived under the window until somebody needed to risk it.

Nessa stood in the middle of the room and looked around as if seeing the dimensions for the first time.

I knew better.

She had probably known them for years.

That night the household ledger came out.

Of course.

Nessa wrote the new rent beside the old.

Circled the difference.

"We don't need it," I said.

"No."

That answer stopped me.

"No?"

"We don't need it now."

I looked at her.

"That is a dangerous word."

"Now?"

"Yes."

"You use it at work constantly."

"Buildings are easier."

"I know."

I looked down at the ledger.

We could afford the room.

That was not the same as comfortable.

Our wages had grown.

Nessa's district position paid steadily.

My inspection hours were steady enough.

Private work remained unreliable but useful.

Family support still happened often enough that I refused to call it charity and my mother refused to call it anything else.

My shoulder added healer costs occasionally.

Transport was up.

Food was up because apparently working adults responded to exhaustion by buying soup from other working adults.

The new rent fit.

Barely.

That bothered me.

If the answer had been no, numbers would have protected me from deciding.

Instead the numbers said possible.

I hated possible.

Nessa tapped Mina's rough sketch of the second room.

"Tools in the back."

"I have a tool cabinet."

"You have tools under the bed."

"Temporary."

"Seven years."

"Long temporary."

"Desk for me in front."

"Where do my records go?"

"You have district office."

"I also have records."

"Then we share shelves."

I made a face.

Nessa saw.

"Your old station books do not deserve more room than my current schedules."

"I did not say they did."

"Face."

"Security flaw."

She smiled.

Then she tapped the narrow back room again.

"Guest bed sometimes."

"Tools."

"Guest bed first if somebody needs it."

"Tools can move."

"Good."

She said it too quickly.

I looked at her.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"That was a suspicious good."

"You agreed."

"That does not make it safe."

Nessa folded her arms.

"Your mother after a healer visit. Lena and Oren if transit fails. Eli if he gets stuck in town. Someone visiting. Someone sick."

"Yes."

"Children."

The room changed shape.

Not physically.

Unfortunately.

I looked at the sketch.

Then at Nessa.

She did not look away.

"Children," I repeated.

"That is the word."

"Plural."

"Usually the category is."

"You are doing this badly."

"I am doing nothing. We are discussing a room."

"You inserted children into the room."

"You asked what we might need space for."

"I absolutely did not."

She smiled.

I hated that smile because it meant the record would not support me later.

We had talked around children before.

Around.

Other people's babies.

Lena's friends.

A cousin's new son.

The cost of school shoes.

Whether our stairs would murder a toddler.

Whether I would be unbearable teaching a child to use tools.

Nessa said yes.

I said that was speculation.

We had never put the possibility in the household ledger.

There are ideas that remain abstract until they threaten rent.

"Do you want children?" I asked.

Nessa looked at me for a long second.

Not offended.

Not amused now.

"Yes."

Simple.

I had expected explanation.

She gave me none.

"You?"

First-life Greg had no answer stored anywhere useful.

He had slept with women.

Loved some of them.

Left all of them eventually.

Children had belonged to other people's houses.

Things you visited.

Things you bought small gifts for when someone reminded you.

Things that grew while you were away.

I had never built a schedule around one.

Never chosen a room because one might exist later.

Never stayed somewhere long enough that the question became structural.

"I think so," I said.

Nessa nodded.

No celebration.

No immediate plan.

No dramatic kiss over the ledger.

She wrote the rent difference again because the first number had smeared.

That helped.

"Not because of the room," she said.

"Good."

"And not now."

"Also good."

"Coward."

"Financially responsible coward."

"Put that in the ledger."

I looked at the sketch again.

One extra room.

We thought it looked enormous.

That judgment did not survive our life.

At the time, though, it felt like abundance.

Enough space that our life could become something else without moving immediately.

Work.

Family.

Guests.

A sick parent.

A child.

More than one, apparently, if Nessa was going to be difficult about grammar.

The room did not promise any of it.

That mattered.

We were not renting a nursery.

We were renting capacity.

I understood capacity.

Much safer word.

The lease was the harder part.

Mina wanted five years.

I wanted one.

Nessa wanted five before hearing why.

"Why five?" I asked Mina.

We stood in the second room the next afternoon while Mina held the lease against the wall because she had not brought a board to write on.

"Because opening the wall costs me money if you leave."

"We pay the wall."

"Then because I don't want to find tenants next year."

"Honest."

"Yes."

"One year."

"No."

"Two."

"Five."

"Three."

"Five."

"You are bad at negotiation."

"I own the building."

Strong position.

Nessa asked, "Rent fixed?"

Mina looked at her.

"First three years."

"Five."

"No."

"Then four fixed, fifth capped."

I turned.

"What are you doing?"

"Negotiating."

"I was negotiating."

"You were counting down."

"That is a recognized technique."

"Not when she only says five."

Mina smiled.

They negotiated without me for ten minutes.

I resented efficiency when excluded from it.

Five-year lease.

Rent fixed three years.

Fourth-year increase limited.

Fifth could rise more but required four months' notice.

We paid for the doorway.

Mina paid plaster finish.

Repairs to existing structure hers.

Damage caused by my improvements ours.

That line had my name on it without using my name.

Nessa made her add stove maintenance responsibility because Siva had apparently complained about it.

I asked how she knew.

"She talks to people," Mina said.

Interesting method.

Then came the signature.

Not like East Two.

No safety decision.

No workers standing beneath my judgment.

Just five years.

That was somehow heavier.

First-life Greg had signed leases.

Rooms.

Warehouses.

Temporary houses near contracts.

I remembered none of the signatures.

Most places had been containers around work.

Sleep there.

Store equipment there.

Leave when the contract moved.

The address belonged to the job more than it belonged to me.

This one felt different before I even touched the pen.

Not marriage.

Not ownership.

Not forever.

Five years was aggressively not forever.

It was still long enough that I could not pretend the apartment was temporary housing between important events.

Long enough for jobs to change.

Parents to age.

Nessa to move again inside district work.

Long enough for a possibility to stop being theoretical.

I thought of the word children and nearly became interested in the plaster.

Nessa signed first.

NESSA VALE.

Clean handwriting.

No hesitation I could detect.

I signed beneath her.

GREGORY.

I hated the full name here too.

Mina witnessed.

Then took our deposit and immediately became less friendly.

Landlord transformation.

The doorway took two days.

I did not open it myself.

Growth.

Also the wall contained an old drain sleeve I did not trust.

A mason named Davi opened it.

I stood nearby offering suggestions until he asked whether I wanted to pay double.

I left.

Mostly.

The first hole through the plaster was small enough to see light from the other room.

Nessa stood on one side.

I stood on the other.

She put one eye to it.

"Very useful."

"Ventilation."

"For one eye."

"Specialized system."

Davi told us both to move.

The finished doorway was narrow enough that Nessa hit her shoulder on the frame the first week.

She blamed me.

I had not built it.

Evidence did not matter.

We moved her district desk into the front room.

Mine went beside it eventually.

Two desks.

Different heights.

Her shelves neat.

Mine vertical piles defended as active reference.

The narrow room became tools first.

Then records.

Then winter storage.

Then tools again after Nessa discovered I had put oily fittings beside blankets.

"We need categories."

"We have categories."

"Everything is under MISCELLANEOUS."

"That is a category."

"It is surrender."

We bought another chair.

Proper one.

The old stool moved to the back room.

The table stayed.

Of course.

It looked smaller in the larger space.

That bothered me for a day.

Then people filled around it.

Lena and Oren came for dinner and stayed too late because nobody had to balance a plate on a knee anymore.

Eli slept in the narrow room once when the bridge tram stopped during a storm.

My mother stayed two nights after a healer procedure because our stairs were fewer than hers.

My father complained that the second room was wasted on paper.

Nessa showed him the district map.

He stopped complaining and began giving opinions about it.

Lorn used my desk to write private-work sheets when his house was too loud.

Perrin drew something obscene in the margin of one.

We kept it until a client nearly saw.

The apartment learned new uses faster than we did.

That was the first thing I got wrong about extra space.

I thought an empty room meant unused capacity.

It did not.

People find space.

Then they become the reason it exists.

The room did not decide what our family became.

I want that clear.

Space is not destiny.

Neither is a lease.

Signing for five years did not produce children.

It did not guarantee health.

It did not protect anyone.

We simply made room before we knew every reason we might need it.

The fifth year seemed absurdly far away when we signed.

Then it arrived with the casual cruelty of calendars.

We renewed.

Different terms.

Higher rent.

Mina claimed the building had improved.

I pointed at the floor.

Still sloped.

She said character had appreciated.

Nessa laughed.

I lost again.

By then the question of whether our life might grow had stopped sounding theoretical.

Not solved.

Not scheduled.

Real.

There is a difference.

I remember the first lease because it was the first time staying acquired a duration.

Before that, staying had been what happened when I did not leave.

Four weeks became eleven years.

One night became thousands.

Nessa moved in because rent was stupid.

Life accumulated by default.

The lease was different.

Five years.

Written down.

Chosen.

A door opened between two rooms because we paid someone to put it there.

A future did not walk through it that day.

We made space anyway.

Not forever.

Long enough.
