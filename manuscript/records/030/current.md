## RECORD 030

## THE MECHANIC

East Four started coughing when Mira and Tomas were fourteen months old.

Mira had learned to walk.

Tomas had learned to say no.

Both developments made leaving the apartment harder.

Dera called the station problem a cough.

Halden called it cyclic return instability.

Nessa called both descriptions annoying.

I called it impossible before I heard it.

That was efficient stupidity.

The station had been losing return pressure for three seconds at a time.

Not enough to shut anything down.

Enough to trip two alarm bells and make the upper feed regulator hunt for balance.

Every forty minutes.

Approximately.

Not exact.

Nothing useful is exact when you first meet it.

East Four's crew had checked the obvious things.

Regulator seat.

Clean.

Return valve.

Good.

Market branch draw.

Normal.

Buried chamber monitoring.

Stable.

No new foundation movement.

No old bronze pressure response outside known range.

The cough remained.

Dera sent me a note.

COME LISTEN.

That was the whole note.

No title.

No courtesy.

Good to know some institutions remained stable.

The note arrived while Tomas was sitting inside our empty washing basket.

Not beside it.

Inside.

He had climbed in and could not climb out without admitting the decision was poor.

He objected to assistance.

Mira was walking from chair to table with both hands full of wooden spoons.

She had discovered carrying objects made falling more dramatic.

Nessa read the note over my shoulder.

"You should go."

"I know."

"That sounded disappointed."

"It is a machine making a strange noise."

"Exactly."

I looked at her.

She smiled.

Cruel woman.

Nessa had district work that afternoon.

My mother could come after midday.

That gave me four hours at East Four before I needed to be home again.

Four hours used to be the amount of time I spent deciding whether a job was worth starting.

Life had changed.

My mother arrived twenty minutes late because my father had misplaced his glasses while wearing them.

Family support has limits.

I put on my work coat.

Mira immediately wanted to be held.

Of course.

She had ignored me for most of the morning.

Now departure made me valuable.

I picked her up.

She put one damp hand against my cheek.

Tomas saw this and also wanted up.

I had two arms.

Technically sufficient.

Practically stupid.

I held both for perhaps thirty seconds before Tomas leaned backward to inspect the ceiling and Mira tried to steal his ear.

My mother took Tomas.

He objected.

Then noticed her necklace.

I ceased to exist.

Children are loyal under very specific conditions.

I left.

Halfway down the stairs I realized there was a wooden spoon in my coat pocket.

I considered going back.

Then remembered we owned more than one.

Growth.

East Four looked the same from the street.

Warehouse pretending importance.

Tram above.

Brick dark from years of weather.

Inside, everything had changed slightly enough to be rude.

Different tool hooks.

New pressure desk lamp.

My old locker still there.

Another worker's gloves on top.

Nessa's red cord still tied to a peg inside hers even though she barely worked station shifts now.

Dera stood near the upper manifold.

"Late."

"I have children."

"Poor planning."

"You did not give a time."

"I expected competence."

"Then why call me?"

She smiled once.

There she was.

The current station Ranker was a woman named Seli Dorn.

B-class.

Stronger than I was then by a useful margin.

Younger by six years.

She had joined East Four after I moved to district hours and apparently resented the fact that people still referred to certain repairs as Greg's way.

I liked her immediately for that.

"Inspector," she said.

"Ranker."

Dera said, "If both of you start measuring titles, I will drown you in the lower intake."

We stopped.

The station was hot.

Loud.

Smelled like oil, damp brick, and old metal.

Nobody touched my face.

Nobody screamed because I had cut a pear incorrectly.

Nobody climbed onto my leg while I tried to read.

For perhaps six minutes I experienced East Four as a place of profound peace.

This was not an opinion I shared with Nessa.

The cough happened fourteen minutes later.

I heard it before the gauge moved.

A hollow double knock somewhere behind the return manifold.

Then the Line hum dipped.

Not much.

Three seconds.

Regulator wheel twitched.

Pressure returned.

Alarm bell clicked once but did not fully ring.

I frowned.

Seli said, "There."

"Yes."

"Useful."

"Fuck you."

She smiled.

Dera handed me the log.

Twenty-one events in two days.

Intervals from thirty-three minutes to fifty-two.

Not periodic enough for an automatic control cycle.

Too regular for random demand.

Most happened during daylight.

Two at night.

Temperature unrelated.

Market draw unrelated according to current meters.

Rain unrelated.

The old me would have started with the buried chamber.

The slightly less old me did anyway.

I went down.

Checked the access plate.

Old load marks.

Reference chalk.

Temperature tabs.

Nothing.

Seli followed.

"Thought district said chamber stable."

"It is."

"Then why are we here?"

"Because stable things can change."

"Useful philosophy."

"Do you want help?"

"Not particularly."

Good.

We measured.

Listened.

Waited through one cough.

No corresponding movement below.

Not old Line.

Probably.

I hated probability less by then.

We went back up.

A mechanic named Jori Pell was lying under the return assembly.

Not related to Inspector Pell as far as anyone knew.

The city reused names irresponsibly.

Jori was fifty-eight and had worked rotating equipment longer than I had been alive in the second life.

He held a metal rod against the housing with the other end touching his ear.

I had seen mechanics do that in my first life.

I had also seen them get mocked by young Rankers with expensive sensing gear.

The mechanics usually kept working after the sensing gear broke.

"What do you hear?" I asked.

Jori did not move.

"Nothing yet."

"That is encouraging."

"No."

"Why?"

"Because it means I have to stay here."

Fair.

We waited.

Maintenance contains a lot of waiting beside objects that refuse to perform when observed.

Half an hour.

No cough.

Seli checked the market board.

Normal.

Dera did paperwork.

I inspected the regulator again because doing nothing offended me.

Jori remained under the housing.

The station stayed hot.

The tram passed overhead twice.

Nobody needed anything from me.

I sat on an overturned parts crate and nearly fell asleep.

Dera looked at me.

"You sick?"

"No."

"Then why do you look dead?"

"Children."

"Still?"

"Apparently they remain."

Jori, without moving from under the housing, said, "How old?"

"Fourteen months."

"Twins?"

I looked down.

"How do you know?"

"You look worse than one."

Seli laughed.

I disliked the station again.

Then the cough came.

Knock.

Knock.

Dip.

Jori sat up so fast he hit his head.

"Fuck."

"What?"

"Bearing."

I looked at the return assembly.

"Bearing reads clean."

"Not that one."

He pointed upward.

At the tram viaduct.

I stared.

"The tram."

"Maybe."

Dera came over.

"Explain."

Jori held out the listening rod.

"Second knock comes through the housing after the first. Not pressure hammer. Mechanical."

"From above?"

"Feels like."

I listened.

Nothing.

Of course.

Seli looked toward the ceiling.

"A tram passes every few minutes."

"Not every tram," Jori said.

Dera pulled the street schedule.

East freight line crossed the viaduct twice an hour during day.

Less at night.

Heavy cars.

Different axle spacing.

Intervals roughly matched.

Not exactly because freight ran late.

I looked at the log.

Thirty-three.

Forty-one.

Fifty-two.

Two night events on late freight runs.

There it was.

Maybe.

We went outside for the next freight.

Rain had stopped but the street still smelled wet.

Jori crouched beside the station wall with his rod against a support plate.

I felt ridiculous.

Good sign.

The freight tram came.

Heavy cars over the viaduct.

First axle.

Nothing.

Second.

Nothing.

Third car crossed.

Knock.

Inside the station, alarm clicked.

Dera swore.

Jori smiled.

"Bearing."

"Which bearing?" I asked.

He pointed to the viaduct support directly above the station return anchor.

Not Line bearing.

Tram bearing.

Two systems sharing masonry.

Modern tram support had been replaced four years earlier.

The new bearing plate sat slightly differently from the old one.

Under heavy freight, the viaduct shifted a fraction.

That load traveled through the brick support.

The station return anchor moved with it.

Not enough to damage the pipe.

Enough to momentarily bind the regulator linkage mounted beside it.

Three seconds.

Release.

Cough.

I stood under the viaduct feeling stupid.

Seli looked delighted.

"Old Line?"

"No."

"Buried chamber?"

"No."

"Ancient mystery?"

"Fuck you."

Jori said, "Could still be ancient."

I looked at him.

He smiled.

Everyone had become unbearable.

Finding the cause did not fix it.

That took three days.

Not three continuous days for me.

That distinction mattered now.

I spent the first afternoon at East Four.

The next morning I stayed home while Nessa went to district.

Tomas had decided oatmeal was an insult.

Mira had discovered the lower shelf where I kept blank inspection paper and redistributed twenty-three sheets across the apartment.

I recovered eighteen.

The other five entered history.

After midday my mother arrived and I went back to East Four.

The third day Nessa worked from home until lunch, then we switched.

There had been a time when a difficult station problem consumed whatever hours it demanded.

Now the problem had appointments.

I expected this to bother me more than it did.

The station remained broken while I was home.

Other people continued working on it.

When I returned, they told me what had happened.

Civilization survived.

Mostly.

Tram authority said the bearing was within tolerance.

They were right.

District Line said the return anchor was within tolerance.

Also right.

Two acceptable movements combined into one unacceptable interaction.

I had seen that pattern before.

Not the specific fault.

The institutional version.

No single part failed.

The boundary between them did.

That sentence tried to become profound.

We refused it.

We needed a bracket.

Jori designed the first one.

Simple slotted mount that let the regulator linkage float slightly relative to the masonry movement.

I disliked it.

"Too much play."

"How much?"

"Enough."

"That is not a measurement."

Tova had infected everyone.

We measured.

I was wrong.

The play was small.

Seli wanted a stiffer anchor instead.

Jori said stiffness would transfer tram movement into the return housing.

She disagreed.

They built both test setups on a spare bench.

Jori's worked better.

Seli accepted it without ceremony.

Good Ranker.

I did not contribute much to the bracket.

That sentence still irritated me when I first told the story.

I had found nothing underground.

Jori heard the mechanical transfer.

Seli challenged the anchor design.

The bench test settled the argument.

My primary contribution was knowing enough to stop insisting the answer should belong to the kind of problem I understood best.

Useful.

Less satisfying.

We installed the slotted mount during a one-hour off-peak reduction.

Dera made me hold the lamp.

"I can fit the bracket."

"Jori designed it."

"I understand brackets."

"Hold the lamp."

"I used to work here."

"Yes."

"That is my point."

"No. That is mine."

I held the lamp.

Jori fitted.

Seli handled pressure isolation.

A younger station hand tightened the final bolts.

Dera watched all of us.

Then the freight tram came.

Heavy cars.

Viaduct moved.

No knock.

Regulator steady.

No cough.

Everyone looked at the gauge.

Nothing happened.

Excellent.

We waited for the next freight.

Still nothing.

The fault was gone.

Or hidden.

Maintenance teaches humility by making success indistinguishable from a machine simply choosing not to embarrass you yet.

East Four logged the modification.

TRAM-INDUCED REGULATOR BINDING.

FLOAT MOUNT INSTALLED.

DESIGN: J. PELL.

FIELD REVIEW: S. DORN / G. [my full name because paperwork hated me].

Dera added:

TOOK THREE DAYS BECAUSE INSPECTOR WENT LOOKING UNDERGROUND.

I crossed it out.

She wrote it again.

Some records resist revision.

I stayed for lunch.

Old habit.

Different crew around the table.

Seli had my old seat.

Not assigned.

Just happened.

I noticed.

Then did not care enough to move her.

Growth.

Jori ate slowly because two fingers on his right hand were stiff with age.

Lorn was not there.

Nessa was at district.

Dera still drank tea that looked like metal wash.

The station hummed.

Healthy.

I knew the sound.

Still.

That mattered.

Then I looked at the wall clock.

The twins usually woke from their midday sleep around then.

Not exactly.

Nothing useful is exact when you first meet it.

Apparently children qualified.

I pictured Mira standing in the crib because lying down had become beneath her.

Tomas would wake slower.

One eye first.

Then the other.

Then a complaint if nobody appeared quickly enough.

I had spent the morning relieved nobody needed me every thirty seconds.

Now I wanted to go home.

Annoying.

Dera saw me looking at the clock.

"Go."

"I am eating."

"You have been holding the same bread for five minutes."

I looked at my hand.

True.

"I can stay."

"Problem is fixed."

"Probably."

"Then go be useful somewhere else."

There were several ways to hear that.

I chose the least insulting.

I wrapped the bread in cloth.

Seli looked at it.

"For later?"

"Mira likes the crust."

Dera looked at me.

I looked back.

"What?"

"Nothing."

I disliked her expression.

I went home.

Mira was awake.

Tomas was not.

Of course.

My mother sat at the table while Mira marched around the room carrying a cup with both hands like an unstable offering.

She saw me.

Stopped.

Then walked directly past me toward the bread in my hand.

"Da," she said.

I looked down at her.

Then at the bread.

"You meant the bread."

She reached up.

Evidence supported my theory.

I gave her the crust.

She sat on the floor and became busy with it.

My mother said, "She asked for you twice."

"Did she?"

"Then you arrive and she chooses bread."

"Reasonable priorities."

Tomas woke in the other room.

I heard the first small sound.

Not crying yet.

The warning stage.

I knew it immediately.

I had been called to East Four because I knew how a healthy station sounded.

Jori had solved the problem because he knew how a bad bearing sounded.

At home I knew the difference between Tomas waking and Tomas deciding the entire building had failed him.

Different expertise.

Same method, maybe.

Listen long enough that the ordinary becomes specific.

That sentence also tried to become profound.

Mira dropped bread on the floor.

My mother told me to pick it up.

Problem solved.

Mostly.
