## RECORD 021

## THE BROTHER

My sister moved across the river because I told her not to.

That is not exactly why.

It is the version she preferred.

Lena had worked at the printer since I came home nineteen again.

Not the same job the whole time.

Jobs grow around people if the people stay long enough.

She started trimming paper. Then setting type. Then correcting other people's setting because apparently literacy becomes management if enough errors happen nearby.

By thirty-one, I was doing district inspection and pretending private repair work was not becoming a second job.

Lena supervised the evening floor at South Print.

She had Eli.

A husband named Oren who repaired clocks badly enough to stay employed and well enough to stay married.

A rented house three streets from my parents.

And, according to me, a perfectly good arrangement.

That should have warned everyone.

She told us about the offer at my father's dinner.

He still cooked once a week because retirement had not damaged his confidence with carrots.

The stew was orange.

Nessa looked into the pot.

"You know other vegetables exist."

"Unproven," my father said.

Lena waited until bowls were full.

Smart.

People argue worse when hungry.

"I got offered North River."

My mother looked up first.

"North River what?"

"Floor manager."

My father stopped chewing.

Oren smiled into his bowl.

That meant he already knew.

Traitor.

I said, "No."

Everyone looked at me.

Lena blinked once.

Then she smiled.

Not kindly.

"I wasn't asking you."

"I know."

That made it worse.

North River Print was much larger than South.

Government contracts. Guild manuals. School books. Election sheets when the city bothered pretending ballots were interesting.

Higher pay.

A regular day schedule after six months.

Better pension contribution.

And across the river.

Not another province.

Not even another city.

Forty minutes by tram when the bridge line behaved. An hour when it did not.

A different school for Eli if they moved near the shop.

Less time near my parents.

That was the part I heard.

Lena heard the rest.

"It's nearly four silver more," she said.

"After transport?"

"If we move."

"So move."

"I am."

I put down my spoon.

My mother did not.

Interesting.

"You already decided."

"Yes."

"Then why are we discussing it?"

Lena's face changed.

Nessa kicked me under the table.

Hard.

I looked at her.

She kept eating.

Coward.

"Because I live in a family, Greg," Lena said.

"I know."

"Do you?"

Rude.

Also fair.

My father cleared his throat.

"How far?"

"Three stops past the bridge."

"You'll bring Eli Sundays?" my mother asked.

"Yes."

"Every Sunday?"

"Most."

My mother nodded.

No objection.

No speech about distance.

No reminder that her knees hurt and my father's balance had become an unreliable employee.

I waited for somebody else to say the obvious thing.

Nobody did.

So I did.

"You help here."

Lena looked at me.

"Yes."

"Appointments."

"Yes."

"Medicine."

"Yes."

"Eli comes after school sometimes."

"Yes."

"Moving makes all of that harder."

"Yes."

She kept agreeing.

I hated when people did that.

It removed the easy argument.

"So?"

"So it gets harder."

"That's your answer?"

"No. My answer is I still want the job."

I laughed once.

Wrong sound.

Lena heard it.

"You think that's stupid."

"I think you're adding a problem to solve a problem."

"What problem am I solving?"

"Money."

Her expression went flat.

Oren stopped pretending the stew mattered.

Nessa kicked me again.

I moved my leg.

Lena said, "You think my job is money."

"That is generally why people have jobs."

"Is that why you inspect old stations?"

"Yes."

Everyone stared at me.

"Mostly."

Nessa made a sound into her cup.

I ignored her.

Lena leaned back.

"I want the floor."

"What?"

"The floor. Forty people. Two presses. Scheduling. Training. I want it."

I had not expected that sentence.

Which was embarrassing.

Not for her.

For me.

"You supervise now."

"Twelve people."

"Still supervision."

"You were C-class and turned down the west contract because you'd already been S-class once."

The table went quiet.

My parents did not know the truth.

Not the whole truth.

Lena knew pieces. Enough, apparently, to weaponize my own philosophy at dinner.

I looked at Nessa.

She shook her head.

Not me.

Right.

Lena had heard me say it myself years earlier.

People remember when you are dramatic near food.

"You had somewhere higher to compare yourself to," Lena said. "I don't."

That sat between us.

My first life had given me a bad habit.

Not foreknowledge.

Scale.

I knew what continental work looked like.

S-class.

Kings.

Guild halls changing schedules because I arrived.

That made ordinary ambition look small if I was not careful.

Four silver.

Forty workers.

A print floor.

Three tram stops.

I had almost reduced my sister's next life to transportation math because I could not feel the size of it from inside my own history.

Then there was the other problem.

In the first life, Lena had stayed close.

Not always the same house.

Close.

I remembered letters.

Her first child.

A second baby who did not exist in this history.

A winter when Oren's shop failed and they moved back near my parents.

A later year when she managed a small printer, not North River.

Ink shortages.

Her handwriting getting sharper when she was angry.

Visits I postponed because whatever city I was in had temporarily convinced me it mattered more.

The memories made a shape.

Home.

Lena nearby.

Parents aging.

The shape felt safe because it had already happened.

Safe is not the same as good.

I knew that.

Apparently knowing and using knowledge remained separate careers.

"North River closed in my first life," I said.

Nessa looked at me sharply.

Lena frowned.

"What?"

Too late.

There are sentences you cannot put back in your mouth.

"When?" Lena asked.

I should have stopped.

I did not.

"Not soon. Years."

"How many?"

"I don't remember exactly."

"Why?"

"Contract loss. Maybe. Government moved school printing. There was a strike too."

The memory came as a notice in a Guild hall.

Delayed maps.

A clerk swearing.

North River named because document supply had been disrupted.

That was all it had meant to me then.

Now my sister wanted to move her husband and son there.

"If you stay at South, you already have seniority," I said. "North River could be gone before Eli finishes school."

Oren spoke for the first time.

"Could."

"Yes."

"You know?"

"No."

"Then why are you talking like that?"

Good question.

I hated him for asking it.

Lena was not angry anymore.

That would have been easier.

She was thinking.

I had put doubt into her using evidence from a dead history.

Nessa put down her spoon.

"What do you know now?"

I looked at her.

She meant current evidence.

Our rule had started with choices touching her.

Apparently jurisdiction had expanded.

"North River has city contracts."

"Current?"

"Yes."

"Solvent?"

"I don't know."

"Staffing?"

"I don't know."

"Management?"

"I don't know."

Lena said, "I do."

We looked at her.

She had interviewed twice.

Walked the floor.

Talked to three supervisors and two press operators.

Read the contract board.

Asked about four years of layoffs.

North River had lost one government contract and replaced it with school work.

The day schedule was real.

The pension was real.

The move allowance was small but real.

They had been profitable three years.

Not rich.

Stable.

Current life.

Current evidence.

My memory had a closure years from now under conditions I did not understand, in a history already broken in several places before North River ever reached it.

I looked at my sister.

"You did your homework."

"Yes, Greg."

"I didn't mean that badly."

"You usually don't."

That hurt because it was accurate.

My mother finally spoke.

"I want you closer."

Lena's eyes went to her.

There.

The real argument.

Not contracts.

Not my memories.

A mother whose knees hurt wanted her daughter nearby.

Lena reached across the table.

My mother took her hand.

"I know," Lena said.

My mother squeezed once.

"I still want you to go."

That surprised me.

Then she looked at me.

"Children leaving is not betrayal."

I knew that sentence was for me.

I did not appreciate it.

My father said, "You came back."

Everybody looked at him.

He shrugged.

"You left first. Then came back."

First life, I had left.

Second life, I had stayed.

My sister had lived with both consequences without knowing there were two versions.

I rubbed my face.

"Fine."

Lena's eyebrows rose.

"Fine?"

"That sounded like permission."

"It did."

"I withdraw it."

"Good."

"You should go."

She watched me.

Then smiled a little.

"Still sounds like permission."

"Fuck."

Nessa laughed.

My father added carrots to his bowl.

Conversation moved on because families do that even when one person has just exposed an entire private defect over stew.

Not completely.

Nothing moves on completely.

For three days after dinner, I behaved normally.

On the fourth, I went to the district records office and asked for North River's contract filings.

Not because I was checking.

Because I was curious.

Those are different words.

The clerk gave me six years of public sheets and charged two copper for copies.

I read them at our table after Nessa went to bed.

That detail matters because it means I knew exactly what I was doing.

North River's contracts were ordinary.

School printing.

Medical forms.

A municipal ledger run.

One Guild manual contract that paid less than I expected.

No obvious collapse.

No hidden crisis.

Nothing that justified the certainty I had brought to dinner.

Nessa came out for water and found me with the papers spread around the lamp.

She stopped.

I looked at her.

She looked at the papers.

I said, "Current evidence."

She leaned over one sheet.

"Are you trying to prove yourself wrong or prove yourself right?"

"I am gathering information."

"That was not the question."

I hated when everyone in my life learned follow-up questions.

"I don't know."

That answer made her less amused.

She pulled out the chair opposite me.

"If North River is healthy now, does Lena get to go?"

"She already gets to go."

"Greg."

"Yes."

"If it is healthy now."

I stared at the contract totals.

"Yes."

"And if it closes in ten years?"

"I was right."

Nessa waited.

I rubbed my eyes.

"No. She still got ten years."

"Better."

"I dislike your grading system."

"You keep submitting work."

She took her water and went back to bed.

I stayed with the contracts another ten minutes.

Then folded them.

The next morning I gave them to Lena.

She looked at the stack.

"What is this?"

"Current evidence."

Her eyes narrowed.

"Nessa?"

"Partly."

Lena flipped through the first page.

"You paid for copies?"

"Two copper."

"You are insane."

"Probably."

She kept them.

That was the closest thing either of us offered to peace before the move.

Lena and Oren moved six weeks later.

North River district.

Two rooms over a cooper's shop for the first year.

Eli hated the new school for twelve days and then developed a best friend named Jori who taught him how to spit between tram rails.

Apparently adaptation had standards.

My mother cried after they left.

Not before.

She made Lena take three blankets she did not need and a pot she did not want.

My father gave Oren a box of tools and explained each one twice.

I carried furniture.

Useful.

Simple.

The apartment stairs turned halfway up and narrowed for no reason anybody could defend.

Oren argued with the cooper downstairs about whether the stair had been built before furniture existed.

Eli sat under a table that was not yet assembled.

Lena had pinned a North River press schedule beside the kitchen door.

She caught me looking at it.

"You want to inspect that too?"

"No."

"Sure?"

"Yes."

"North River might close."

I deserved that.

"I was an asshole."

She stopped.

I had not planned to apologize while holding a chair.

Good apologies rarely arrive under ideal conditions.

Lena leaned against the wall.

"You were."

"I used something I remember as if it gave me authority over your choice."

"You do that a lot?"

"Less than I used to."

"That is not comforting."

"I know."

Oren shouted something about the stair.

The cooper shouted back.

Neither sounded endangered.

Lena looked tired.

Happy too.

Both can happen.

"Do you remember me making bad choices?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Good ones?"

"Yes."

"Did you stop either?"

I thought about the first life.

Letters.

Visits.

Advice from cities far away.

Money sent when asked.

Advice sent when not.

"No."

"Then maybe keep that tradition."

I laughed.

"Fair."

She took the chair from me.

"Also I'm older than you."

"No, you're not."

"In this life?"

"No."

"In your head?"

I hesitated.

Lena smiled.

"There. That's the stupid part."

She carried the chair inside.

I stood in the hall.

Forty extra years in my head did not make me forty years older than my sister.

It made me a man with more memories.

Some useful.

Some dangerous.

None of them promoted me to father.

North River did not close that year.

Or the next.

I stopped checking after a while.

Not because I stopped caring.

Because checking had become another way to wait for my memory to be right.

Lena became floor manager.

Then she became good at it.

Those were different events.

The first happened when they gave her the key.

The second took years.

She trained people.

Fired one.

Hired three.

Lost a school contract and gained a medical-printing contract I had never heard of in the first life.

Eli learned the tram route.

My parents learned to plan appointments around Lena's days off instead of assuming she lived three streets away.

I learned to take my mother to the healer without treating the schedule change as evidence Lena had chosen wrong.

Mostly.

Once, when the bridge tram failed and Lena missed dinner, I said nothing.

Nessa noticed.

"You want credit?"

"Yes."

"No."

Cruel woman.

Years later I remembered North River's first-life closure more clearly.

It had not failed because the company was badly run.

The city consolidated printing after a budget crisis.

The strike came afterward.

I had reversed the order.

Memory had preserved the shape and lost the mechanism.

Again.

By then the second-life city had different contracts, different officials, different workers, and a print floor run partly by my sister.

The old future had no jurisdiction there.

Neither did I.

I was her brother.

That turned out to be enough work.
