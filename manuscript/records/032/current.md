## RECORD 032

## THE SUCCESSOR

Dera left East Four when Mira and Tomas were three.

She did it by insulting everyone individually.

That took most of the morning.

Mira considered this excellent conversation.

Tomas considered Dera's hammer drawer more important.

Both were probably right.

Dera had been foreman longer than I had been in the second life.

Not forever.

Stations make people feel permanent because the building stays while shifts change underneath them.

Children do something similar for different reasons.

If someone has existed every time you remember a place, then obviously they belong there.

Mira and Tomas believed East Four belonged to Dera.

So did I, apparently.

Dera encouraged that illusion by refusing illness, vacations, and most forms of happiness on principle.

Then district offered her a job.

Not management.

She would have refused management.

They called it Field Practice Supervisor.

Terrible title.

Better work.

Her job was to review station procedures before district turned local methods into copied standards.

Which meant someone had finally decided the woman who spent thirty years saying "that form will get someone burned" might be useful before the form was printed.

Halden claimed this was institutional maturity.

Dera called it delayed listening.

Both were true.

She told East Four on a Tuesday.

I was not there.

That offended me.

Nessa told me that evening while Mira was asking why onions make eyes hurt and Tomas was feeding one of his peas to the table.

"Dera's leaving."

I stopped cutting bread.

"What?"

Mira looked up immediately.

"Dera leaving where?"

"District field practice," Nessa said.

"Why?"

"New job."

"Why?"

"Because they asked her."

"Why?"

Nessa looked at me.

Cowardly delegation.

"When?" I asked.

"Six weeks."

"She didn't tell me."

Nessa looked at me.

"You don't work there."

"I work there."

"You visit."

Mira said, "Dada visits Dera's station."

There it was.

Witness testimony.

"Fuck both of you."

Mira gasped.

Not because of the word.

Because she enjoyed catching adults violating rules.

"Dada said fuck."

"Yes," Nessa said.

"Why?"

"Emotion."

"Can I say fuck?"

"No."

"Why?"

"Because I said so."

Mira's face hardened.

This was her least favorite reason.

Tomas put another pea on the table.

I went to East Four the next morning.

Dera saw me come in.

"No."

"I haven't said anything."

"You have the face."

Everyone had learned the face.

Unacceptable.

"You are leaving."

"Yes."

"You didn't tell me."

"I told my crew."

"I am your crew."

Dera stared.

I heard it.

Past tense had attacked without warning.

"I was your crew."

"Yes."

"Eleven years."

"Yes."

"That should qualify me for direct notice."

"No."

"Why?"

"Because I knew Nessa would tell you and I enjoy efficiency."

Cruel old woman.

I followed her into the break room.

"Who takes East Four?"

"Sera Venn."

I knew the name.

Night supervisor.

Forty-four.

Sixteen years at East Four, longer than I had worked there.

Quiet.

Good maintenance record.

Hated unnecessary meetings.

I approved.

That was my second mistake.

"Good," I said.

Dera looked at me.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"What?"

"You said good like you were asked."

"I know Sera."

"So do I."

"I think she will be good."

Dera leaned against the table.

"Wonderful. I can leave now."

"Fuck you."

"There he is."

Sera became acting foreman two weeks before Dera left.

Not ceremonial.

Practical overlap.

She ran schedule.

Signed work orders.

Told Dera no twice.

That last part mattered.

First time was a lower-gallery inspection Dera wanted added after a valve job.

Sera refused because the crew had already worked ten hours.

Dera argued.

Sera held.

The inspection moved.

Second time Dera wanted to keep an old parts cabinet because she knew every drawer by memory.

Sera replaced it with labeled bins.

Dera called the bins childish.

Then used them correctly.

I watched both events with professional satisfaction and emotional confusion.

A successor should not be a copy.

Obvious sentence.

Harder when the person being succeeded has become part of the station's weather.

Dera trained me by refusing to train me gently.

Sera trained differently.

She wrote instructions.

Actual ones.

She had new hands repeat isolation order aloud before touching controls.

Dera considered this unnecessary because "people should listen the first time."

Sera said people did not.

Evidence favored Sera.

Dera hated evidence when it became personal.

Beautiful.

During those six weeks Mira asked about Dera's new job often enough that I regretted explaining it.

"She goes district?"

"Yes."

"Every day?"

"Probably."

"No East Four?"

"Not as foreman."

"Why not?"

"Because Sera will be foreman."

"Both?"

"No."

"Why?"

There are bureaucratic questions adults build entire departments to avoid answering clearly.

Three-year-olds ask them over breakfast.

Tomas had fewer questions.

He had discovered that Dera kept a lower drawer full of old pencils, chalk stubs, bent tags, and small tools nobody else wanted.

The first time he saw it he sat on the floor in front of the open drawer for twenty minutes.

Dera watched him.

"He broken?"

"Focused."

"Same face you make before damaging paperwork."

"Fuck you."

Mira said, "Dada said fuck."

Dera looked at me.

"You brought witnesses now?"

"Unfortunately."

The farewell was not a party.

Dera specifically prohibited a party.

East Four responded by holding an extended lunch with cake.

She called it lunch.

Everyone respected the fiction.

I came.

Nessa did too even though district transfer was busy.

We brought Mira and Tomas because they knew Dera and because my mother had a healer appointment.

Those facts were equally important.

Taking two three-year-olds into East Four required more planning than some inspections.

No lower gallery.

No pressure desk.

No touching valves.

No running.

No licking metal.

That last rule existed because Tomas had once licked a tram rail support and then objected to the taste.

Experience produces policy.

Mira repeated every rule back to me.

Tomas said, "No lick."

Good enough.

Halden arrived late carrying a wrapped object.

Dera looked suspicious.

"No plaque."

"It is not a plaque."

She opened it.

A new hammer.

Heavy station hammer with her initials burned into the handle.

Dera stared at it.

Then Halden.

"Too nice."

"You can damage it."

"Good."

She accepted.

That was the closest she came to sentiment.

Tomas had gone completely still.

He pointed.

"Hammer."

"Yes," Dera said.

"Mine?"

"No."

"Why?"

Mira asked it for him.

Dera looked at both children.

"Because I said so."

Mira frowned.

"Bad reason."

The room went quiet for half a second.

Then Lorn laughed loud enough to cough.

Dera stared at Mira.

Mira stared back.

Three years old.

No fear.

Poor judgment.

Dera said, "Because it is heavy enough to break your foot and belongs to me."

Mira considered.

"Better."

Dera looked at me.

"I hate her."

"She likes you."

"Worse."

Tomas continued staring at the hammer.

Lorn gave Dera a cabinet key.

Old lower cabinet.

The one she gave me years earlier with don't make me regret this.

She looked at it.

"You stole this."

"Retirement gift."

"I am not retiring."

"Departure theft, then."

She kept it.

Nessa gave her red cord.

New one.

Dera looked confused.

"What do I do with this?"

"Catch it on a valve and learn empathy."

Dera laughed.

Mira asked why.

Nobody answered.

Some education can wait.

I had no gift.

That bothered me all morning.

Every object felt wrong.

Tools she had better versions of.

Alcohol she would complain about.

Books she would not read.

So I gave her paper.

One copied page from East Four's old station book.

My first year.

Dera's handwriting beside the housing I had argued should not be replaced.

TEMP WORKER GREGORY OBSERVED CRACKED BOLT. CORRECT FIND. POOR RESPONSE TO DIRECTION. WATCH.

She read it.

Looked at me.

"You stole this too."

"Copy."

"Why?"

"You were right."

"About the bolt?"

"About watching."

Her face changed slightly.

Dangerous territory.

She rescued both of us.

"I stopped watching years ago."

"That explains a lot."

She folded the paper and put it in her pocket.

Good enough.

Then she looked at Mira and Tomas.

"You two."

Both looked up.

Dera opened the old pencil drawer.

Sera, already acting foreman, said, "I thought you were clearing that."

"I am."

Dera pulled out two pencils.

Both too short for normal adults.

One slightly longer than the other.

She handed one to Mira.

One to Tomas.

"Do not eat these."

Mira said, "I don't eat pencil."

Dera looked at Tomas.

He looked offended.

"No lick."

"Good," Dera said.

He put the pencil in his pocket immediately.

Mira held hers like a credential.

"Mine from Dera."

"Yes."

"At Dera station."

Dera's face moved.

Barely.

She looked toward the pressure desk.

Then at Sera.

"For four more days," she said.

Mira did not understand.

I did.

At the end of lunch Sera stood.

No speech prepared.

Good.

She said, "Dera leaves Friday. I take the full roster Saturday. If anyone has a complaint about the new schedule, write it down so I can ignore it accurately."

Everyone laughed.

Dera did too.

Then Sera added, "And stop asking Dera what she would do after Friday."

That quieted the room.

Smart.

Dera looked at her.

Sera looked back.

There it was.

The actual handoff.

Not key.

Not title.

Authority becoming real because the new person was allowed to make different decisions without the old one haunting every choice.

I needed that lesson more than Sera did.

Mira whispered, "Sera boss?"

Nessa crouched beside her.

"Yes."

"Dera boss."

"Until Friday."

Mira frowned at the calendar as a concept.

Tomas had begun drawing a line on scrap paper with his pencil.

His priorities were healthier.

Dera's last Friday arrived.

She worked the whole shift.

Of course.

At third bell she cleared her desk.

One drawer contained eighteen years of pencils too short for normal use.

Sera threw half away.

Dera objected.

Sera kept throwing.

Beautiful.

At fifth bell Dera put on her coat.

Not district brown.

Her own.

She stood near the pressure desk.

I expected something.

Everyone did.

She said, "Don't trust gauge seven."

Sera said, "We replaced gauge seven last month."

Dera frowned.

"Then trust it until it earns otherwise."

That was all.

She walked out.

I followed.

Not immediately.

Thirty seconds.

Enough dignity.

Outside, the tram passed overhead.

Dera looked up at the viaduct.

"Jori's bracket still holding?"

"Yes."

"Good."

"You nervous?"

"No."

"Liar."

She looked at me.

"About district?"

"Yes."

"Of course."

There.

Human.

Annoying.

"I haven't been new at a job in thirty years."

"You will hate everyone."

"I already know Halden."

"Head start."

She smiled.

Then looked back at East Four.

"Do not bother Sera."

"I do not bother Sera."

"You bother walls."

"That is my job."

"Her station."

I heard the word.

Her.

Not mine.

Not Dera's.

Sera's.

For now.

Stations belong to people only in grammar.

Still matters.

"I know."

Dera nodded.

Then walked toward district offices.

No retirement.

No disappearance.

She became harder to avoid, actually.

Her new job put her inside every procedure meeting I wanted to skip.

She sent notes in the margins.

Rejected three of my field forms.

Forced one district standard to include a stop condition operators had been using informally for years.

Her career continued.

That should not have surprised me.

Mentors are dangerous in stories because stories like freezing them at the age when they became useful to the protagonist.

Life is less cooperative.

Dera had things to do after teaching me how not to argue with every foreman.

She did most of them while still arguing with every engineer.

Good for her.

The part that stayed with me happened the following week.

We passed East Four on the way back from my parents' place.

Mira walked between Nessa and me holding one hand each because she had discovered curbs and considered them invitations.

Tomas carried a smooth stone he had refused to abandon three streets earlier.

The station doors were open to afternoon air.

Someone inside laughed.

Mira pointed.

"Dera station."

I answered automatically.

"Yes."

Then stopped.

Nessa looked at me.

Did not help.

Cruel woman.

Mira looked up.

"Dera station?"

I looked through the doorway.

Sera stood near the pressure desk with a slate under one arm.

Different posture.

Different schedule.

Same brick.

Same hum.

"Sera's," I said.

Mira considered that.

"Dera district."

"Yes."

"Sera station."

"Yes."

That was enough for her.

Children are sometimes better at succession than adults because they have not yet learned to confuse memory with ownership.

Tomas threw his stone into a gutter.

Then immediately wanted it back.

We spent five minutes finding it.

Important work.
