## RECORD 044

## THE ATTACHMENT

Nine days is enough time to reconsider a three-month contract.

I did not.

This was either confidence or evidence.

At thirty-one I owned one good field coat, two acceptable shirts, three pairs of socks that disagreed about what constituted a pair, a bedroll, a repair kit, a staff, and enough professional equipment to make carrying all of it irritating.

I packed twice.

The second time I removed things I had packed because I was afraid of needing them.

Then I put half of those things back.

Experience.

The north-road party met before dawn outside Vey's eastern freight gate.

Orla was already there.

Of course.

Shield wrapped.

Pack closed.

Hair tied back.

She looked at my bag.

Then at me.

"Three months."

"I know."

"You packed for a siege."

"I packed for uncertainty."

"Uncertainty has weight."

"Apparently."

She took the repair kit off the outside of my pack.

"What is this?"

"Repair kit."

"For what?"

"Things."

"Which things?"

"Things that break."

She opened it.

Needles.

Cord.

Wire.

Two small clamps.

Wax.

Leather patches.

A file.

Three kinds of adhesive.

Orla held up the second clamp.

"Why two?"

"If one breaks."

"The repair tool."

"Yes."

"Needs a backup."

"Now you understand field work."

She put both clamps back.

That felt like victory.

Calder arrived carrying less than either of us.

This annoyed me immediately.

"Where is the rest of your equipment?"

"In the cart."

I looked behind him.

A narrow two-wheel survey cart stood beside the gate.

"We have a cart?"

"Yes."

I looked at Orla.

She had known.

"You watched me carry this here."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Observation."

Arcrutus had damaged civilization.

The rest of the party arrived by first light.

I will give you the names that mattered then.

Not all at once.

That is not how people arrive in memory.

The survey lead was a woman called Senn.

Forty, maybe.

Could have been thirty-five with field weather.

Could have been fifty with good knees.

Surveyors age strangely.

She had three rolled route sheets, a brass sighting frame, and no patience for introductions longer than useful.

"Senn. Survey. I decide where we're going. Orla decides whether the route is safe enough to keep going. Calder handles forward contact. Greg is support."

She pointed at the cart.

"Do not let that fall off a mountain."

I looked at Calder.

"My responsibilities are clear."

"Good."

There were two others.

A route hand whose name I have genuinely lost.

Sorry.

And a young ruin clerk named Taris who had been sent to copy markings and inventory anything the district thought might justify the trip.

Taris was twenty-two and carried too much paper.

I liked him immediately for professional reasons.

We left Vey under gray light.

No ceremony.

No tournament bell.

No crowd.

No Arcrutus.

Just boots, cart wheels, cold air, and six people discovering one another's walking speed.

Ithar shifted in the cave.

"You remember the departure poorly."

"Nothing happened."

"You left for three months with strangers."

"Yes."

"And nothing happened."

"At the gate? No."

His eye stayed on me.

"You prefer events."

"Most people do."

"Do they?"

I considered telling him yes.

Then remembered who I was talking to.

"I did."

"Better."

The first day was mostly road.

That mattered more than I understood.

Tournament support had taught me to think in exchanges.

Pressure arrived.

I changed where it went.

Someone moved.

I adjusted.

A mistake became recoverable.

Then a judge shouted reset or the match ended and everybody got water.

Roads do not shout reset.

The first lesson was Orla's left boot.

She started favoring it around midday.

Barely.

Not a limp.

A shorter plant on uneven ground.

I watched for twenty minutes.

Then said, "Your foot hurts."

"Yes."

"How long?"

"Since yesterday."

"And you took a three-month contract."

"My foot is not dying."

"That is a low medical standard."

She kept walking.

"Hot spot. New boot lining."

"Fix it."

"At camp."

"Why not now?"

"Because we're walking."

I looked at Senn ahead of us.

"We can stop."

Orla looked at me like I had proposed stopping winter.

"For my boot?"

"Yes."

"No."

"Why?"

"Because it is a hot spot."

"Which becomes a blister."

"At camp."

"Then tomorrow it becomes my problem."

That got her attention.

She stopped.

Not the party.

Just her.

"Your problem?"

"If you change your stance because your foot hurts, yes."

"You have known me nine days."

"Ten."

"That is worse."

Calder had heard enough to turn around.

"Boot?"

"Apparently a strategic emergency," Orla said.

He looked at her feet.

"Left?"

She stared at him.

"Fuck both of you."

We stopped for four minutes.

Orla changed the lining, taped the skin, and continued walking.

Nobody died from the delay.

I remember feeling vindicated.

This was premature.

By the third day we had left the maintained road.

The old quarry route climbed north through broken limestone shelves where carts had once moved stone toward Vey.

Half the retaining walls had slumped.

Drainage channels were full of dirt and small trees.

In places the road remained wide enough for three carts.

In others we had six feet of usable ground and a very educational drop.

Senn stopped talking while we crossed those sections.

So did everybody else.

The cart became my obsession.

Not because Senn had told me not to let it fall off a mountain.

That helped.

Because I could see every bad transfer before it happened.

Wheel catches stone.

Handle twists.

Calder compensates.

Orla takes more weight.

Rear wheel slides.

Taris grabs the wrong side because he is carrying paper and optimism.

Small failures.

Connected.

I started using Barrier on the cart.

Not much.

A little resistance at the downhill wheel.

A brief catch under the axle when a rut tried to turn it.

A pressure surface against a crate so it did not shift when the cart tilted.

Useful.

Cheap.

At least individually.

Around noon we reached a collapsed switchback.

The original road turned left around a limestone face.

The outer half had fallen away.

What remained was enough for people.

Maybe enough for the cart.

Maybe is an exciting word when your equipment represents six weeks of district paperwork.

Senn studied the shelf.

Orla walked it first.

Came back.

"Passable. One at a time. Unload the upper crate. Rope the cart."

Calder nodded.

Nobody argued.

I liked that too.

We unloaded the brass equipment and the document chest.

Taris carried the chest himself.

This was wise.

We ran rope from the cart to an old quarry anchor cut into the inner rock.

Orla went ahead.

Calder took the handles.

I stood uphill on the rope.

Senn behind me.

The route hand watched the rear wheel.

Simple.

Then the limestone under Calder's right foot broke.

Not the whole shelf.

A piece the size of a dinner plate.

His foot dropped six inches.

Cart handle went down with him.

The left wheel lifted.

Rear load shifted outward.

Orla grabbed the front frame.

Wrong angle.

Necessary angle.

The rope snapped tight through my hands.

Everything happened correctly.

That was the problem.

Calder let the handle go rather than let it pull him over.

Correct.

Orla took the frame rather than let the cart rotate.

Correct.

Senn braced me from behind.

Correct.

The route hand shoved the rear wheel inward.

Correct.

Every correct decision loaded the same three feet of road.

I saw it.

Left side.

Again.

Apparently the universe had limited teaching material.

I put Barrier under the outer wheel.

Not a wall.

A plane.

Angled inward.

The wheel hit it and stopped dropping.

Load slammed through my control.

Too much.

I let the Barrier move.

An inch.

Two.

Three.

Bleeding force instead of pretending force had ceased existing.

The cart rotated back toward the rock.

Orla got her feet under her.

Calder climbed out of the broken patch.

The route hand shoved.

We recovered.

Five seconds.

Maybe six.

Nobody fell.

Nothing broke.

Taris said, "Holy shit."

Then a stone came down from above us.

Small.

Fist-sized.

It struck the road between Orla's boots.

She looked up.

"Move."

That was not addressed to anyone individually.

We moved.

More stones followed.

Not a landslide.

Loose debris shaken free by six people and a cart fighting the shelf.

Still enough.

Calder grabbed the handles again.

Orla went forward.

I held the outer wheel line.

We pushed through the switchback under falling rock.

A stone hit Calder's shoulder.

Barrier caught some of it.

Another clipped Orla's shield.

I stabilized her footing.

The cart bounced over the broken section.

I caught the axle.

Taris stumbled ahead with the document chest.

I put a brief surface behind his heel.

He recovered without knowing why.

Good.

We cleared the shelf.

Then we kept moving because the slope above us was still shedding stone.

Two hundred yards.

Maybe three.

Around the next shoulder of rock.

Safe ground.

Stop.

Everyone breathed.

I was grinning.

This is embarrassing now.

At the time I thought I had done extremely well.

I had.

That was the problem.

Calder rolled his shoulder.

"Good catch."

"Which one?"

"Cart."

"Also your shoulder."

"I noticed."

Orla checked the wheel.

"You let the Barrier slide."

"Had to."

"Could you have held it?"

"Maybe for half a second."

"Then?"

"Something expensive."

"On you or the cart?"

"Yes."

She nodded.

No praise.

Useful question.

Senn gave us six minutes.

We drank.

Retied the upper crate.

Checked the wheel pin.

Then she said, "Ready?"

I stood.

My left hand trembled.

Not much.

Enough that the water in my cup moved.

I closed my fingers around it.

The shaking stopped.

Mostly.

We walked.

Ten minutes later it started again.

Twenty minutes after that, Calder slipped on gravel.

Nothing dramatic.

Ordinary slip.

I reached automatically.

Barrier formed late.

Not dangerously late.

Noticeably late.

Calder recovered himself.

Then looked back at me.

"You good?"

"Yes."

"That was slow."

"You didn't fall."

"I wasn't going to."

"Then efficient allocation."

He kept looking.

I disliked this.

"My hand is tired."

"From the shelf?"

"Probably."

"How tired?"

"Normal amount."

"Greg."

"I don't know."

That answer was more accurate.

Orla heard us.

Of course she did.

"Show me."

"No."

"Hand."

"You are not my mother."

"Correct. Hand."

I held it out.

The tremor was visible when I stopped trying to hide it.

Orla looked at Calder.

Then at me.

"How much did you spend back there?"

"Spend what?"

"Whatever you call it."

"Control."

"How much?"

"Enough."

"How much do you have left?"

I opened my mouth.

Nothing useful arrived.

She waited.

"I don't know."

"Good."

"That does not sound good."

"It isn't."

Senn had stopped ahead of us.

"Problem?"

Orla answered before I could.

"Support burned too much on the shelf."

I objected to the grammar.

"Support has a name."

Senn looked at my hand.

"How long?"

"How long what?"

"Until you're normal."

"An hour?"

"Question or answer?"

"Question."

Senn looked north.

Then at the route sheet.

"We have six hours of exposed road before camp."

There it was.

Six hours.

I had been thinking about the shelf.

The cart.

The falling stones.

Five seconds.

Maybe six.

I had solved the five seconds beautifully.

Then the road continued.

Ithar's voice came from the dark.

"You forgot the road."

"I knew there was a road."

"You forgot it while solving the shelf."

"Yes."

"Because the shelf was dangerous."

"Yes."

"And the road was not?"

I rubbed my left hand.

Old memory made it ache sometimes.

Or maybe age did.

"The road was longer."

Ithar waited.

"That is not an answer."

"It became one."

We changed the order.

That annoyed me.

Calder took rear watch.

Orla stayed with the cart.

The route hand moved forward with Senn.

Taris walked near me because apparently a twenty-two-year-old clerk carrying seventeen pounds of paper had become my assigned safety officer.

"If you fall," he said, "am I supposed to catch you?"

"No."

"Good."

"You sound relieved."

"You're large."

Fair.

For the next hour I used almost nothing.

This was harder than using support.

Calder stepped badly twice.

I watched him recover.

Orla took the cart over a rut and absorbed the handle twist through her shoulder.

I watched.

Taris nearly lost a rolled sheet to the wind.

I did not save it.

He caught it himself.

This was probably not a support decision.

It felt related.

My hand steadied by degrees.

Not suddenly.

Capacity returned slowly enough that I could feel the difference between available and comfortable.

I had never cared about that distinction in a tournament.

Available meant use it.

Why else have it?

Field work disagreed.

By late afternoon we reached a narrow drainage cut where water had eaten under the old road.

Nothing dramatic.

No falling stones.

No enemy.

Just six tired people, a loaded cart, and a section of ground that would punish one stupid step.

Senn looked at me.

Not Orla.

Me.

"How much?"

I knew what she meant this time.

I flexed my hand.

Checked the pressure response at my fingertips.

Built a small Barrier against my own palm.

Released it.

"Enough for one bad problem."

"And after?"

I looked at the drainage cut.

Then at the remaining light.

"Depends how bad."

Senn nodded.

"Good."

Same word Orla had used when I admitted I did not know.

I was beginning to understand that field people used good incorrectly.

We crossed without needing me.

That was also good.

Camp was a roofless quarry shed with three standing walls and a fireplace that smoked unless the wind came from exactly the correct direction.

The wind did not.

We ate lentils, hard bread, and something Calder claimed was sausage.

Orla disagreed on taxonomic grounds.

Taris burned his first piece of bread.

Then his second.

Senn took the third away from him.

I sat with my back against warm stone and realized I had known these people for less than two weeks.

Calder handed me the salt without asking.

I took it without thanking him because my mouth was full.

Orla tossed me a strip of leather.

"What?"

"Your pack strap is cutting your shoulder."

I looked down.

It was.

"You noticed."

"Unfortunately."

I repaired it after dinner.

With the second clamp.

Vindication.

Ithar said, "You enjoyed them."

"Some of them."

"You have named four."

"That is not evidence."

"You remember the salt."

I looked toward the cave mouth.

Night outside.

Black stone inside.

"People remember stupid things."

"You do."

"Frequently."

"You had known Calder ten days."

"Approximately."

"And he knew you wanted salt."

"The lentils needed salt. Everyone wanted salt."

"Mm."

"Do not."

"I have not."

"You're doing it silently."

"You are interpreting."

I hated him a little.

The next morning Orla woke me by kicking the sole of my boot.

Not hard.

Enough.

"Hand?"

I flexed it.

Normal.

"Fine."

"How much?"

I almost said enough.

Stopped.

Thought.

"Full, I think."

"You think."

"I do not have a gauge."

"Get one."

"Excellent suggestion. I'll buy one at the next support shop."

"Build one."

That irritated me because it sounded possible.

We left before sunrise.

By the fifth day I had started measuring myself against ordinary things.

How many small catches before the fingers felt warm.

How long warmth took to become tremor.

How quickly control returned after food.

After water.

After ten minutes sitting.

After an hour walking without use.

Not scientific.

Useful.

I learned that holding a weak Barrier for a long time cost differently than catching a hard impact.

I learned repeated little corrections accumulated when I pretended they were free.

I learned hunger made precision worse before it made strength worse.

I learned cold did something unpleasant to my left hand that I had previously blamed on being cold.

Very sophisticated.

Mostly I learned to ask a new question before intervening.

Not only where does the failure go?

Not only who carries it?

When does it arrive?

And after I move it, what do I still need to be able to do?

That last question was the expensive one.

It made me leave problems alone.

Small ones.

Recoverable ones.

Problems people could solve themselves.

I did not enjoy that at first.

Support feels most like support while you are doing something.

Field work taught me that sometimes the useful act was keeping capacity unused.

Reserve looked like nothing.

Until it wasn't.

On the sixth day Calder caught his boot between two stones and twisted his ankle.

Not badly.

He swore.

Sat.

Tested weight.

I watched.

He looked at me.

"Well?"

"What?"

"Support."

"Can you walk?"

"Yes."

"Then walk."

He stared.

Orla laughed from ahead of us.

"He's learning."

"I liked him better at the tournament."

"You barely knew me."

"Exactly."

We wrapped the ankle.

Adjusted his pack.

Slowed slightly.

No Barrier.

Three hours later, when a rotten edge gave under Taris near a ravine, I had both hands available.

I caught him before his second foot left the road.

One hard surface behind the hip.

One under the heel.

Release as Calder grabbed his coat.

Done.

Taris sat down very carefully.

Looked at the ravine.

Then at me.

"Thank you."

"You're welcome."

Calder was still holding the back of his coat.

"You had that fast."

"Yes."

He looked at his wrapped ankle.

Then back at me.

I knew what he was thinking.

"Do not make it meaningful."

"I didn't say anything."

"Good."

Orla had reached us.

She checked Taris.

Checked the road.

Then looked at my hand.

"Tremor?"

"No."

"Good."

This time I agreed with the word.

That night we camped beside an abandoned lime works.

The roof leaked.

Calder snored.

Taris talked in his sleep.

Senn wrote route notes until the fire was nearly gone.

Orla discovered I carried coffee and became temporarily less hostile.

I had packed too much.

Apparently some uncertainty was worth its weight.

We had been gone six days.

Eighty-four remained.

I remember lying in the dark listening to five other people breathe and thinking the arrangement was almost ideal.

We had work.

We had rules.

We needed one another.

Nobody had promised forever.

The contract had an end date before it had begun.

At thirty-one, that felt clean.

Ithar's head lifted slightly.

"Temporary belonging."

"You already used that phrase."

"You objected to it."

"I still do."

"Because it is inaccurate?"

I was quiet.

The dragon waited.

I had climbed six days to reach him.

Apparently he could spend some of them waiting for answers.

"No."

"Then why?"

I looked down at my hands.

Older now.

Scarred differently.

One finger that never closed completely after Nhal.

A life of things not yet reached in the story.

"Because I liked the temporary part too much."

Ithar said nothing.

That was worse.

So I went back to the road.

At the time, I did not think I had found a way to belong without staying.

I thought I had found good work.

That was true too.

The two truths fit together comfortably for years.

Professionally, the first week gave me a simpler problem.

I had thought support meant keeping people functional.

It did.

I had misunderstood the unit of time.
